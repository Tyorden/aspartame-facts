Where the claims came from

One email, and one genuinely messy approval

Nearly every viral aspartame claim still circulating traces to a single document written by one person in 1995. The story about how the FDA approved it is a different matter — most of its factual skeleton is real.

The chain email

If you've heard that aspartame causes multiple sclerosis, that MS is "really" methanol poisoning, that diet soda left in a hot car turns into formaldehyde, that it caused Gulf War Syndrome, or that it triggers lupus, blindness and seizures — you have read the descendants of one document.

It was written by Betty Martini of Duluth, Georgia, founder of an activist group called Mission Possible World Health International. She posted it to Usenet in late 1995 as a first-person account of lecturing at a "World Environmental Conference." From around December 1998 it circulated as a chain email under the byline "Nancy Markle."

No trace of Nancy Markle has ever been found — not by Snopes, not by McGill's Office for Science and Society, not by anyone in nearly thirty years. Neither has any conference talk matching the letter's account.

The quote that wasn't

The letter's central factual anchor is an EPA announcement of an "epidemic" of MS and lupus caused by aspartame. The scientist who actually gave that keynote, Dr Jessica Gaylord, put it plainly in writing:

From the person the claim is attributed to

"I did not make the quote you referenced… that comment was made by a woman in the audience during the Q & A session and her name was Betty Martini."

And: "I never used the word 'epidemic' because as a former NIH science administrator, I know better."

So the document's foundational claim is an author quoting herself from the audience, and attributing it to the speaker. Everything downstream inherits that.

The letter isn't 100% invention

Several of its claims were true, and a debunking that skips past them overreaches. Aspartame does yield methanol. The phenylalanine warning label is real and necessary. The 1980–81 approval was genuinely contested. And some of the 1999-era debunkings themselves overclaimed — particularly on weight control, where the confident claims of that era have since been walked back.

The 1981 approval, conceded

This is the strongest anti-aspartame argument in circulation, and most of its factual skeleton is documented and real:

  • An FDA task force found the manufacturer's animal studies so poorly conducted that the FDA suspended its own approval.
  • A federal grand jury investigation of the company lapsed — while the prosecutor handling it was being recruited by the company's law firm.
  • A Public Board of Inquiry unanimously refused approval.
  • A majority of the FDA's own brain-tumour reviewers remained unconvinced.
  • A new Commissioner, appointed by an incoming administration, overruled all of them three months into his tenure.
  • That Commissioner later took a paid consultancy at the company's PR firm.

None of that is invented. If it makes you uneasy, it should.

Where that story stops being true

What is not documented is the causal link — that anyone leaned on the FDA. Two GAO investigations, a Senate-requested post-employment review and a D.C. Circuit ruling all examined that allegation directly and found no evidence for it. The GAO reviewed the FDA's chronological files from 1964 to 1985, the Commissioner's phone log and his appointment calendar, and found no evidence that anyone outside the FDA was involved.

Several vivid details that circulate are also simply garbled:

Circulating versionRecord
Hayes added a sixth panel member to force a tie, then broke it himselfNo primary-source support found at all. The advisory panel was established in March 1981 — before he took office — had around eight members, took no vote and reached no consensus. The Commissioner had sole statutory authority regardless.
"Arthur Hayes Hull Jr."His name was Arthur Hull Hayes Jr. The transposition is a reliable tell that a source was copied from the chain email rather than from records.
Reagan issued a day-one executive order stripping the FDA commissioner's powerThere was no FDA commissioner on 21 January 1981. The previous one left on the 20th; Hayes didn't take office until 13 April.
The Public Board of Inquiry found aspartame caused brain damageThe opposite. It concluded aspartame would not cause brain damage, mental retardation or endocrine dysfunction at projected intakes. It objected on rat brain tumours only.
The GAO found officials violated post-employment lawIt found the reverse — the contacts were "permissible under the postemployment statute," and the officials "had no financial interest in Searle while employed by HHS."

Why 1981 no longer decides anything

Here's the part that makes the whole argument beside the point, however you feel about the events themselves.

The WHO's expert committee set the same 0–40 mg/kg limit in 1980 — before the FDA Commissioner ruled, and entirely outside US jurisdiction. Searle was sold in 1985. The NutraSweet patent expired in 1992. And the evidence base EFSA reviewed in 2013, running to more than 600 datasets, is overwhelmingly not Searle's work.

So even if every accusation about 1981 were true in full, it would tell you something about the FDA in 1981. It would not tell you whether aspartame is safe now. Those are different questions, and only one of them has forty-five years of subsequent evidence attached.
