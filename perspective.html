In perspective

Same category as your phone

Headlines about aspartame lean on a WHO classification that almost nobody explains. Once you know what the category actually contains, the headlines read differently.

What "possibly carcinogenic" is a list of

In July 2023, the WHO's cancer agency put aspartame in Group 2B. That sounds severe until you look at what else is on the list. Group 2B held 329 agents as of July 2026, and among them is the radiofrequency signal your mobile phone runs on, classified back in 2011.

Also on the list: aloe vera whole leaf extract. Traditional Asian pickled vegetables. Gasoline. Working as a carpenter.

The joke writes itself, and it's a fair one to make. But it's worth understanding why the category is so mixed, because that's the part that actually settles the question.

The distinction the headlines skip

IARC does one job: it asks whether there is evidence something can cause cancer under any circumstances at all. It does not ask how much of it you'd need, or whether anyone is exposed to that much. Its groups sort agents by how strong the evidence is, not by how dangerous the thing is.

IARC says this itself, in plain terms — its classifications "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level," and two agents in the same group "may be very different" in actual risk.

So Group 2B is not a danger ranking. It's a filing cabinet for "there's a signal here that someone should look into properly." Sunlight and processed meat sit in Group 1 alongside plutonium, for the same reason in reverse: the evidence that they can cause cancer is solid, which tells you nothing about how careful you need to be at breakfast.

The agency that does ask the dose question is JECFA, which published on the same day. It looked at what people actually consume and left the safe intake level untouched.

Use the comparison, but know its limits

"Same category as your phone" is a genuinely useful way to explain what the label covers, and we're not going to pretend otherwise. Just don't mistake it for the argument. The argument is that hazard classification ignores dose, and that measured intake sits far below any level of concern. The phone comparison is how you explain that to someone in one sentence — it isn't the evidence itself.

The other ingredient in the can

A 12 oz can of Diet Coke contains roughly 200 mg of aspartame and 46 mg of caffeine. Both are regulated. Only one of them has a dose you can actually reach.

For a 68 kg adult, caffeine toxicity starts around 0.7 g and death becomes possible around 10 g. Those are real numbers describing real cases — people have died from concentrated caffeine powder, which is why the FDA issues warnings about it.

Aspartame has no equivalent row. Not because nobody looked, but because animal studies fed it at 4,000 mg per kg of body weight per day and found no adverse effect at any dose they tested. That figure isn't the threshold where harm starts. It's the highest dose the experiment went to. The safe intake limit was then set a hundredfold below it.

Run your own numbers on the calculator and you'll find the caffeine ceiling arrives first at any body weight. The ingredient in diet soda with documented effects at reachable doses is the one nobody writes headlines about.

"The dose makes the poison"

Paracelsus wrote the line in the sixteenth century and it has survived because it keeps being right: all things are poison, and nothing is without poison; the dose alone makes a thing not a poison.

It cuts both ways, which is why it's useful rather than glib. Substances people consider obviously safe will kill you at the wrong dose — water, salt, and caffeine all have documented fatal doses. Substances people consider obviously dangerous are harmless at low enough exposure; that's the entire basis of anaesthesia and chemotherapy.

So "aspartame is a chemical" and "aspartame breaks down into methanol" are not arguments. They're descriptions. Everything is a chemical, and a glass of tomato juice yields several times more methanol than a can of diet cola. The only question that means anything is how much, compared to what threshold — and for aspartame the honest answer is that typical intake sits well under a tenth of a limit that was itself set a hundredfold below a no-effect dose.

Where this argument stops working

Dose reasoning assumes a threshold exists. For the cancer question that's the mainstream view, but it isn't unanimous — some researchers argue certain exposures have no safe floor, and observational studies have reported associations at intakes far below the ADI. JECFA looked at those and found them unconvincing rather than nonexistent. "The dose makes the poison" is the right frame here. It is not a universal trump card, and a site that used it that way would be doing the same thing it criticises.
