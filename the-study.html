Study appraisal

The sweetener–brain aging study, in full

A real study, in a top neurology journal, with a genuine finding — that has also been corrected twice, describes exposures under 7% of the ADI, and whose own authors call it "preliminary and hypothesis-generating."

If you only read one paragraph

This study is not fake, not retracted, and not junk. It found a statistical association between sweetener intake and slightly faster cognitive decline in adults under 60. It also pooled seven chemically unrelated compounds, measured diet once by questionnaire at baseline, found nothing in adults over 60, and required two published corrections — the second of which involved re-running the entire analysis from raw data. The correct conclusion is "interesting, unreplicated, don't change your life over it," which is close to what the authors themselves say in print.

What the study is

Gomes Gonçalves N, Martinez-Steele E, Lotufo PA, Bensenor I, Goulart AC, Barreto SM, Giatti L, de Faria CP, Molina MDCB, Caramelli P, Marchioni DM, Suemoto CK. "Association Between Consumption of Low- and No-Calorie Artificial Sweeteners and Cognitive Decline: An 8-Year Prospective Study." Neurology 2025;105(7):e214023.

Six of the seven sweeteners — all but tagatose — showed associations with faster decline. The cohort is large, prospective, multi-wave, and reasonably diverse (54.8% women, 43.2% Black or mixed race), with a validated questionnaire and adjustment for a wide confounder set. This is not a junk study, and the direction of its finding is genuinely unfavourable to aspartame. A site claiming no study has ever found an aspartame–cognition association would be wrong.

The two corrections

This is the single most load-bearing fact about the paper, and it is verifiable independently — confirmed on PubMed, via the Crossref API (which lists two is-corrected-by relations), and in Europe PMC's correction list.

What this does and does not support:

  • It supports: reduced confidence in the original analysis. Two rounds of confidence-interval errors, followed by a self-described re-run "using fully scripted, reproducible code," implies the original analysis was not scripted or independently reproduced. That is a legitimate reason for caution.
  • It does not support: "the study was retracted," "the study was debunked," or "the numbers were wrong." There is no retraction, no expression of concern, and no Retraction Watch coverage. The corrections were author-initiated and transparent, including a voluntary full audit — behaviour that is, if anything, creditable.
  • Do not claim any specific number changed. Neither notice publishes a before/after table, and the "version with changes highlighted" PDF is paywalled. Which values moved is not publicly verifiable — including for anyone quoting the notices at you.
One thing worth noticing about the timing

Correction #2 went online 30 July 2026. A wave of alarmed coverage followed in early August 2026. The study is from September 2025 — what was new in the news cycle was the correction notice and the print issue, not the research.

What "62% faster" and "1.6 years" actually mean

Those figures come from the American Academy of Neurology's press release, not from the paper's abstract. The underlying corrected estimates, for adults under 60 at baseline, highest versus lowest tertile of combined sweetener intake:

  • Verbal fluency: β = −0.040 (95% CI −0.066 to −0.015)
  • Global cognition: β = −0.026 (95% CI −0.043 to −0.008)

"62% faster decline" is a relative-rate framing of a very small absolute effect. Any confidence interval you see quoted from before November 2025 has been superseded by the corrections.

In adults 60 and over, no significant association was found — the group where dementia risk actually concentrates.

The exposure level is the most underreported detail

191 mg per day — the highest-intake group's mean, across all seven sweeteners combined
<7% of aspartame's ADI, even if every one of those milligrams had been aspartame

This cuts both ways, and it is worth being straight about both.

Against alarm: if a genuine dose-dependent neurotoxic effect existed, seeing it at under 7% of a limit carrying a 100-fold safety margin — while seeing nothing in the older group — is a difficult pattern to explain biologically. It looks more like confounding than like pharmacology.

Against complacency: the flip side is that this cohort had very low sweetener exposure overall — baseline was 2008, when Brazilian sweetener use was low, as the authors concede. The study therefore says nothing about heavy consumers. "The doses were realistic — just a diet soda a day" is a half-truth: 191 mg is roughly one can's worth, but it is the ceiling of the highest exposure group, not a typical American intake.

What the published correspondence says

Neurology published three reader responses and two author replies. This is peer criticism in the journal of record, not social-media commentary.

The criticisms

  • Wölnerhanssen, Meyer-Gerspach & Söderling (29 Oct 2025): exposure misclassification from a food frequency questionnaire; 8–9 years is short for neurodegenerative outcomes; reverse causality; no low-sweetness comparator; and the pooling of chemically unrelated high-intensity sweeteners with natural polyols. They note the classic Turku xylitol trials used tens of grams per day with no evident cognitive harm.
  • Arduini & Masola (6 Nov 2025): the body's own production of xylitol via the glucuronic acid pathway runs to 5–15 g/day, dwarfing the roughly 3 mg/day of dietary intake the study modelled.
  • Wang & Liao (23 Dec 2025): residual confounding by prediabetes and insulin resistance, and the absence of a sugar-sweetened-beverage comparator — which is the question clinicians and consumers actually face.

The authors' replies, which concede a great deal

Suemoto and Gomes Gonçalves (3 Nov 2025) accept that the study is observational and cannot establish causality. Suemoto (12 Nov 2025) agrees that endogenous xylitol synthesis "can reach gram quantities daily," states that natural dietary sources need not be avoided, clarifies that the association refers to "overall LNCS exposure patterns, not to natural dietary sources," and says the findings should be interpreted as "preliminary and hypothesis-generating."

The fair summary

A large, competently run observational cohort found a small association at trace exposures, in a subgroup, using a single baseline dietary measure, pooling seven unrelated compounds — and needed two corrections including a full re-analysis. Its authors describe it as hypothesis-generating and say they would not tell someone to stop drinking diet soda on the strength of it. That is what the record supports. Anything stronger, in either direction, is being added by whoever is telling you about it.

What we could not verify

  • Which specific numbers either correction changed. Neither notice publishes a before-and-after table, and the "version with changes highlighted" PDF sits behind the publisher's paywall.
  • Whether a complete-case sensitivity analysis was null. The American Council on Science and Health asserted on 30 July 2026 that restricting to participants with complete follow-up showed no association across tertiles. We could not confirm this against the paper's supplementary material, and ACSH is an advocacy organisation with a history of industry funding. Treat it as unverified; it is not cited as fact anywhere on this site.
