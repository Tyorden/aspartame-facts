The regulatory record

What the regulators actually found

Two assessments carry most of the weight: EFSA's 263-page re-evaluation in 2013, and the WHO's dual review in July 2023 that produced two headlines which sounded contradictory and were not.

EFSA 2013: the most thorough assessment ever done

In December 2013 the European Food Safety Authority published a full re-evaluation of aspartame (E 951) — 263 pages covering absorption and metabolism, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, the epidemiological literature, all three breakdown products, and a fresh dietary exposure assessment across 17 European countries.

Ahead of it, in November 2011, EFSA did something unusual: it obtained and publicly released the 112 original industry studies submitted to support aspartame's European authorisation in the early 1980s, and stated that over 600 datasets were available for the review. It ran two public calls for data and a public consultation that drew more than 200 comments.

The conclusion, verbatim from the published abstract: "The Panel concluded that aspartame was not of safety concern at the current aspartame exposure estimates or at the ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day. Therefore, there was no reason to revise the ADI of aspartame."

What it found, endpoint by endpoint

EndpointEFSA's findingStrength
Genotoxicity Not mutagenic in bacterial systems; in vivo systemic studies mostly negative. "The available data do not indicate a genotoxic concern." EFSA explicitly noted a data gap at the gene and chromosomal level in mammalian systems. Strong
Carcinogenicity Three chronic rat studies and one mouse study "revealed no aspartame-related increase in any type of neoplasms at all doses tested." NOAEL of 4,000 mg/kg bw/day — 100× the ADI. Strong
Neurotoxicity "The Panel did not see any convincing evidence of neurotoxicity associated with aspartame." EFSA's press release: it "does not harm the brain, the nervous system or affect behaviour or cognitive function in children or adults." Strong
Reproductive / developmental Could not exclude developmental toxicity below 4,000 mg/kg bw/day in animals. EFSA attributed those effects to phenylalanine and pivoted the assessment to human phenylalanine kinetics. Moderate
Methanol Aspartame releases about 10% of its weight as methanol; no risk identified at the ADI. Methanol occurs naturally in fruit and vegetables at levels that dwarf the aspartame contribution. Strong
Cancer epidemiology "No epidemiological evidence for possible associations of aspartame with various cancers in the human population" — as of the 2013 evidence base. Moderate

The pregnancy question, which is more interesting than it's usually reported

This is the part most coverage gets wrong in both directions. EFSA did not find nothing in the animal reproductive data. It found effects it could not exclude at doses below 4,000 mg/kg, identified a NOAEL of 1,000 mg/kg for maternal and developmental toxicity in one rabbit study, and then argued — via a formal mode-of-action analysis, supported by the observation that equimolar L-phenylalanine produced similar effects — that the effects were driven by phenylalanine rather than by aspartame as such. It therefore based the risk characterisation on human phenylalanine kinetics instead of the animal data.

The resulting arithmetic: the clinical ceiling for maternal plasma phenylalanine protective of the fetus is 360 µM. EFSA assumed a worst case where a meal already contributes 120 µM, leaving 240 µM of headroom. Reaching that headroom would require a single bolus of 103 mg/kg body weight in a normal adult, or 59 mg/kg in a PKU heterozygote. Sustaining it would require 40 mg/kg every hour.

Be precise about this

That pregnancy conclusion rests on a concentration-response model, not a trial. EFSA acknowledged uncertainties from limited kinetic data, and reported that for PKU heterozygotes the 95th-percentile lower-bound dose exceeded 40 mg/kg in 82% of simulations — meaning in 18% it did not, with the gap covered by layered conservative assumptions. That is a legitimate regulatory method. It is not the same as direct evidence, and this site will not describe it as though it were.

July 2023: the WHO's two answers

On 14 July 2023, two WHO-affiliated bodies published on aspartame the same morning.

IARC — the International Agency for Research on Cancer — classified aspartame Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans." This was the first time IARC had ever evaluated aspartame. The working group of 25 experts from 12 countries screened over 7,000 references and reviewed roughly 1,300 studies.

JECFA — the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives — reaffirmed the Acceptable Daily Intake at 0–40 mg/kg body weight, unchanged, and concluded the evidence of an association between aspartame and cancer in humans is not convincing.

These were not rival camps. Three JECFA members attended the IARC meeting as observers, both secretariats attended both meetings, and IARC briefed JECFA on its outcome in confidence. The coordination was deliberate.

Why both answers were correct

IARC answers a hazard question: is there evidence this agent can cause cancer under some conditions? JECFA answers a risk question: does it cause harm at the amounts people actually consume? Different questions, different answers, no contradiction.

IARC's own documentation states that its groups "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level," and that "the cancer risk … associated with two agents classified in the same IARC Group may be very different."

Group 2B is also large and heterogeneous. As of the classification list updated 3 July 2026, it contains 329 agents, against 135 in Group 1, 98 in Group 2A and 498 in Group 3.

A rhetorical move worth avoiding

You will often see "Group 2B also includes aloe vera and pickled vegetables" deployed as a punchline. It's true, and it's a useful way to show people what the label covers — but on its own it isn't an argument. Group membership encodes evidence strength, not potency, so listing harmless-sounding companions proves nothing by itself. Open with it if you like; the weight is carried by the hazard/risk distinction above and the exposure data below.

What the "limited evidence" consisted of

IARC's human evidence came from three studies — four cohorts — all using artificially sweetened beverages as a proxy for aspartame rather than measuring aspartame directly:

  • EPIC (477,206 participants, 11.4 years): hazard ratio 1.06 (95% CI 1.03–1.09) per serving increment for hepatocellular carcinoma, from 151 cases (the paper originally printed 101; formally corrected in 2024).
  • NIH-AARP and PLCO pooled: no significant association in people without diabetes; among people with diabetes, HR 1.13 (95% CI 1.02–1.25).
  • CPS-II: reports obesity-related cancers combined at HR 1.05 (1.01–1.08), null after adjustment for BMI.

IARC's own wording: "In all three studies, a positive association was observed … either overall or in important subgroups … but bias or confounding could not be ruled out as an explanation for the positive findings."

What IARC and JECFA genuinely disagreed about

Sites that claim the two agencies "agreed completely" are overclaiming. They differed substantively on two of three evidence streams:

Evidence streamIARCJECFA
Human cancer evidence Limited evidence, hepatocellular carcinoma Not convincing
Animal evidence "Limited evidence for cancer in experimental animals" "No concern for carcinogenicity in animals from oral exposure"
Mechanistic evidence "Consistent and coherent evidence that aspartame induces oxidative stress in experimental systems" Those studies "had limitations in their design"

These are real scientific disagreements between expert panels, not vocabulary differences. They are also, notably, disagreements about evidence quality rather than about whether people consuming normal amounts are at risk — where the two bodies did agree.

The dose arithmetic

The ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day was allocated at JECFA's 24th meeting in 1980 and confirmed at the 25th in 1981, derived from a NOAEL of 4,000 mg/kg bw/day in a 104-week rat feeding study, divided by a 100-fold uncertainty factor. It has not moved in 45 years.

The margin is not the same for everyone

"You'd need 14 cans a day" describes a 70 kg adult. JECFA's own high-exposure estimate for children is 20 mg/kg bw/day — half the ADI, not a hundredth of it. EFSA's worst-case model put 95th-percentile toddlers at up to 36.9 mg/kg bw/day, about 92% of the ADI. Both agencies regard these as substantial overestimates, and neither exceeds the limit. But the honest framing is "high-consuming young children approach the ADI under conservative assumptions," not "everyone is nowhere near it."

What has happened since

The evidence has continued to accumulate, and it has not moved in one direction.

Against the liver-cancer signal: a 2026 pooled analysis of 11 prospective cohorts — 1,518,411 participants, 2,811 liver cancers, median 17.8 years of follow-up — found sugar-sweetened beverages associated with liver cancer but "little evidence that [artificially sweetened beverage] intake was associated with liver cancer risk overall or by subtype." This is the largest test to date of the specific signal that drove IARC's classification, and it did not replicate.

Not helpful to aspartame: a 2026 analysis of the CPS-II Nutrition Cohort (100,004 adults, 21,356 cancers) reported associations between aspartame consumption and higher cancer risk — though with no dose-response relationship, which the authors themselves flag as a reason for caution.

Still open: EFSA's aspartame assessment is now over a decade old and predates the 2023 IARC classification. EFSA has re-evaluated other sweeteners since — acesulfame K and neotame in 2025 — and is currently re-evaluating the aspartame–acesulfame salt (E 962), but we found no announced plan for a fresh full aspartame re-evaluation. Claims that "EFSA rebutted IARC" appear to be unsupported: as of this writing EFSA's public position still rests on the 2013 opinion and does not mention the 2023 reviews.
