Heart, stroke and blood sugar

The strongest case against aspartame isn't cancer

It's this one. Here is the case against aspartame at its strongest — a 2025 mechanism study in monkeys, two cohorts that name aspartame directly, and a safety limit that was never built to detect what those studies describe. Then the answer to it.

Almost every popular argument against aspartame is about cancer, and cancer is where the critics are weakest — the rodent studies failed independent pathology review and the largest human cohorts found nothing. The serious argument is somewhere else, it is more recent, and hardly anyone making noise about aspartame has heard of it.

The case, made properly

1. A 2025 paper with a real mechanism

Wu and colleagues, in Cell Metabolism (2025), reported that aspartame triggers vagally-mediated insulin secretion, and that this accelerates atherosclerosis through a specific chemokine pathway — one they established genetically rather than inferred. The work was done in mice and monkeys, at a dose the authors put at roughly three cans of diet soda a day.

This is the most methodologically serious recent paper reporting harm, and it proposes a route that the absorption argument doesn't cover. The metabolism page sets out that aspartame is fully hydrolysed before absorption, so that nothing reaches the bloodstream to act on anything. A vagal, taste-mediated signalling route doesn't require anything to be absorbed at all. The sweetener acts on receptors and the nervous system carries the message.

2. Two large cohorts naming aspartame specifically

Most cohort studies measure "artificially sweetened beverages" and can't separate one sweetener from another. NutriNet-Santé is the exception — it used repeated 24-hour dietary records against a brand-level sweetener database, and reported aspartame by name:

OutcomeFinding
Cerebrovascular events (BMJ 2022)HR 1.17 (1.03–1.33)
Type 2 diabetes, higher sweetener consumers (Diabetes Care 2023)HR 1.63 (1.38–1.93)

A 63% higher relative risk of type 2 diabetes is not a trivial number, and it comes from a cohort with no industry funding and better exposure measurement than most.

3. The daily limit was never designed to catch this

This is the sharpest part of the argument, and it's structural rather than empirical.

The Acceptable Daily Intake is a classical toxicology construct: take the highest dose that did nothing in a 1981 rodent study, divide by 100. That approach bounds organ toxicity and cancer. It was not built to detect chronic low-dose endocrine or neural signalling, and it wouldn't. A compound could sit comfortably inside its ADI for forty years while doing something the ADI was never looking for.

No regulator has revisited the limit against that kind of question.

What answers it

That's the case. Here's why we don't think it currently overturns the safety position — while noting it's the part of this subject most likely to change.

The cohort signal is small, and behaves exactly like confounding

Pooled relative risks across the cardiovascular literature mostly run 1.06–1.26. The signal comes entirely from cohort studies. And the place it's easiest to check — randomised trials of intermediate endpoints like blood pressure and blood lipids — shows nothing. If diet drinks were driving cardiovascular disease, you'd expect some trace in the mechanisms that cause cardiovascular disease.

The reverse-causality problem is severe here specifically. People switch to diet drinks because they have a metabolic problem, and metabolic problems cause strokes and diabetes. A cohort finding that diet-soda drinkers get more type 2 diabetes is close to being a finding that people at risk of diabetes drink diet soda.

The famous dementia study is the weakest link, not the strongest

The 2017 Pase study in Stroke gets cited constantly. Look at it closely:

  • 97 strokes and 81 dementia cases in total — small numbers for the claims made of them.
  • Confidence intervals spanning 1.26 to 6.97. That's not a precise estimate of anything.
  • The dementia association disappeared once diabetes and waist-to-hip ratio were adjusted for.
  • Sugar-sweetened drinks — which by any reasonable theory should be worse — showed no association with either outcome.

That last point is the tell. When the supposedly harmful exposure shows an effect and the obviously worse one doesn't, the most likely explanation is that you're measuring who chooses which drink.

Blood sugar itself is about as settled as this literature gets

Aspartame contains no carbohydrate, and in controlled human trials it doesn't raise blood glucose or insulin. The acute evidence is null, the medium-term evidence is null, and the one long trial in people with diabetes using a near-limit dose for 18 weeks was null. Both the American Diabetes Association and the joint Diabetes UK/BDA/BNF statement say so.

The cephalic-phase insulin response — the small insulin blip some people show to sweet taste — is a real phenomenon. It doesn't matter here because the downstream effect it's supposed to produce, a measurable change in glucose or insulin after eating, doesn't appear.

Three limits on that answer

The null results are low-certainty. Cochrane's verdict on sweeteners in diabetes is formally "inconclusive," not "reassuring" — the trials are small and short. "No trial has found an effect" is accurate; "proven not to affect blood sugar" is not.

The ADA's position is more hedged than it's usually quoted. Its grade-A recommendation is for water over non-nutritively sweetened drinks. The permissive recommendation is grade B and carries "in moderation and for the short term."

Wu 2025 has not been answered. It's one paper, in animals, and animal mechanisms often fail to translate. But no regulator has assessed it, and nobody has tried to replicate it. Watch this one.

Erythritol and xylitol are a different subject

The 2023 and 2024 Cleveland Clinic papers on erythritol and xylitol were widely reported as news about "artificial sweeteners," and get attached to aspartame constantly. They shouldn't be.

The distinction isn't merely that they're different molecules. It's the exposure route. Those are sugar alcohols eaten in gram quantities that circulate in plasma at high concentrations — and the entire proposed mechanism runs through those circulating levels. Aspartame is consumed in milligrams, roughly a thousandth of the dose, and intact aspartame is undetectable in blood. The mechanism cannot apply to it, which is a stronger statement than "these are different compounds."

The mechanistic evidence against erythritol is genuinely more concerning than anything in the aspartame literature. That's a reason to look at erythritol carefully, not a reason to worry about aspartame.

Where this leaves it

An observational signal exists. It's small, it's cohort-only, it fails to appear in the randomised intermediate endpoints, and it has an obvious confounding explanation that has been directly measured in the same cohorts. One 2025 animal paper proposes a mechanism that wouldn't require absorption, and it hasn't been replicated or assessed.

That's not a reason to stop drinking diet soda. It is the part of this subject most likely to move — and if anything does move, it'll move here rather than on the cancer page.
