An evidence reference

What the research on aspartame actually says

Few food additives have been studied this heavily, and it clears every regulatory safety threshold at the amounts people actually consume. The evidence isn't unanimous, though, and this site prints the parts that aren't.

The short version

Every food-safety regulator that has looked at aspartame has concluded it's safe at real-world intakes. The European Food Safety Authority ran a 263-page review in 2013 and left the safe intake level where it was. The WHO's expert committee on food additives independently reaffirmed the same figure in July 2023. That number has held since 1980, and it already sits a hundredfold below the highest dose used in the long-term rat study it derives from.

None of which means every study came back clean. On the same day in July 2023, the WHO's cancer agency classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic on limited evidence. Observational studies have reported associations at intakes well below the safe limit. A 2025 paper in Neurology linked sweetener intake to faster cognitive decline. None of these establish cause, several have since weakened, and one has been corrected twice — but they exist, and a site that skipped past them wouldn't be worth reading.

What this site actually claims

Not that aspartame is proven harmless — nothing is, and nobody serious argues it. The narrower claim is this: at the amounts people actually consume, the evidence of harm is weak, confounded, and hasn't held up when others tried to replicate it, while the evidence of safety is unusually deep. Where that stops being true, the site says so.

Nobody here is telling you to drink it

Worth getting out of the way early, because it's the thing that makes the rest readable.

You do not have to consume artificial sweeteners. There's no nutritional case for diet soda. If you'd rather drink water, drink water — that's a perfectly sensible preference and nothing on this site argues against it. Two of the three public bodies that currently advise against non-sugar sweeteners are making a dietary-pattern argument, not a toxicity one, and that argument is reasonable.

What this site disputes is narrower: the claim that these things are poisoning you. Those are separate questions, and collapsing them is how the conversation goes wrong in both directions. You can think diet soda is a bit grim and still think the formaldehyde story is nonsense. Most people who look into it end up exactly there.

Who has actually assessed this

Not one agency, and not one country. Every national food-safety regulator that has examined aspartame has reached the same conclusion about safety at normal intakes — and they did it independently, in different decades, under different legal systems.

Why we don't call this "settled like climate change"

It's a tempting comparison and we're not going to make it. Climate science has an actual counted consensus — surveys of thousands of papers producing a percentage. No equivalent tally exists for aspartame, so any percentage you see quoted has been made up.

There's a second reason. Genuine open questions remain here — a leukaemia signal that hasn't been resolved, and three public bodies that currently advise against sweeteners for dietary reasons. Claiming the matter is as closed as the greenhouse effect would hand a well-informed critic an easy win, and the case doesn't need it. "Seven national regulators, independently, over forty years" is both true and enough.

How to read a scare headline about aspartame

Most viral claims fail on one of five points. Checking them takes about a minute.

1. Is this about hazard, or about risk?

They're different questions. Hazard asks whether something can cause harm under any conditions at all. Risk asks whether it does at the doses people actually encounter. the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) — the WHO's cancer arm — assesses hazard only, and says so in its own documentation. A headline that turns a hazard classification into a risk warning has made a mistake, not a discovery.

2. What was the dose?

The limit is 40 mg per kg of body weight, around 2,800 mg for a 70 kg adult. Studies reporting associations often involve a small fraction of that. The 2025 Neurology study's highest-intake group averaged 191 mg a day across seven sweeteners combined — under 7% of the limit even if all of it had been aspartame.

3. Observational or randomised?

People who drink diet soda differ from people who don't. They're heavier on average, more likely to be diabetic, and often switched because of a health concern in the first place. That's reverse causality, and it contaminates any cohort study that doesn't handle it carefully. An association in a cohort is a hypothesis worth testing, not a finding.

4. Was it actually aspartame?

A lot of research measures diet-drink consumption as a stand-in, or pools chemically unrelated compounds together. The 2025 study combined aspartame with sugar alcohols like erythritol and xylitol — different molecules, different metabolism, different evidence. Alarm about one routinely gets transferred to another.

5. Is the study new, or just the article?

Publication dates, print issues, press releases and correction notices all create fresh news pegs for research that's often a year or more old. A headline this week doesn't mean a finding this week.
