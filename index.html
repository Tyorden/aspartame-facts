An evidence reference

What the research on aspartame actually says

Aspartame has been assessed more thoroughly than almost any other food additive, and it clears every regulatory safety threshold at real-world intakes. That is the honest headline. The honest fine print is that the evidence is not unanimous, and this site prints the fine print too.

The short version

Two things are true at once, and most arguments about aspartame come from people holding only one of them.

First: every food-safety regulator that has assessed aspartame at real-world exposures has concluded it is safe at those exposures. The European Food Safety Authority ran a 263-page re-evaluation in 2013 and reconfirmed an Acceptable Daily Intake of 40 mg per kg of body weight. The WHO's Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives independently reaffirmed the same figure in July 2023. That number has stood since 1981, and it already contains a 100-fold safety margin below the highest dose ever tested in animals without an adverse effect.

Second: "safe at real-world exposures" is not the same as "every study is null." In the same July 2023 announcement, the WHO's cancer agency classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic on limited evidence. Observational cohorts have reported associations at intakes far below the ADI. A 2025 study in Neurology linked sweetener intake to faster cognitive decline. None of these establish cause, and several have since weakened — but pretending they do not exist is how a debunking site loses its credibility.

The site's actual claim

Not "aspartame is proven harmless." That is unprovable and nobody serious says it. The claim is narrower and better supported: at the amounts people actually consume, the evidence of harm is weak, confounded, and has not held up under replication — while the evidence of safety is unusually deep. Where that is not true, this site says so.

40 mg/kg body weight — the ADI, unchanged since 1981 and reaffirmed by JECFA in 2023

How to read a scare headline about aspartame

Nearly every viral aspartame claim fails on one of five points. Checking them takes about a minute and resolves most of what circulates.

1. Hazard or risk?

These are different questions. Hazard asks whether something can cause harm under any conditions. Risk asks whether it does at the doses people actually encounter. IARC assesses hazard only, and says so in its own documentation: its classifications "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level." A headline that converts a hazard classification into a risk statement has made an error, not a discovery.

2. What was the dose, relative to the ADI?

Aspartame's ADI is 40 mg/kg body weight per day, about 2,800 mg for a 70 kg adult. Studies reporting associations often involve intakes that are a small fraction of that. The 2025 Neurology study's highest-intake group averaged 191 mg/day across seven combined sweeteners — under 7% of the ADI even if every milligram had been aspartame.

3. Observational or randomised?

People who drink diet soda differ systematically from people who don't: they are heavier on average, more likely to be diabetic, and often switched because of a health concern. That is reverse causality, and it contaminates every cohort study that doesn't handle it well. An association in a cohort is a hypothesis; it is not a finding about aspartame.

4. Was it actually aspartame?

A great deal of "artificial sweetener" research measures diet-drink consumption as a proxy, or pools chemically unrelated compounds. The 2025 Neurology study combined aspartame with sugar alcohols like erythritol, xylitol and sorbitol — different molecules, different metabolism, different evidence bases. Alarm about one routinely gets transferred to another.

5. Is the study new, or is the article new?

Publication dates, print-issue dates, press releases and correction notices all generate fresh news pegs for research that is often a year or more old. A headline this week does not mean a finding this week.
