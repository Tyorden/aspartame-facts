Work it out yourself

What's actually a lot, for you?

Safety limits for aspartame are set per kilogram of body weight, so "a lot" is a different number for everyone. Put your weight in and find out what yours is.

Regulators set a number called the Acceptable Daily Intake — the amount you could have every single day for your whole life without expecting any harm. It's set per kilogram you weigh, which is why it's different for everyone, and it's set with a large deliberate safety margin: a hundred times below the largest amount that did nothing at all to animals in testing.

Europe and the World Health Organization use 40 mg per kilogram. The United States allows 50. Enter your weight and it'll do the rest.

    The thing most people don't expect

    Diet soda contains caffeine as well as aspartame, and caffeine has a much tighter daily ceiling. Work through the arithmetic and you run out of caffeine headroom well before you run out of aspartame headroom — for most adults, roughly half as many cans.

    If you drink diet soda all day and something starts to feel wrong, the ingredient with the documented dose-dependent effects at reachable amounts is the caffeine, not the sweetener. That's worth knowing before you go looking for an explanation.

    Why there's no height box

    Because height isn't in the formula. The ADI is defined purely as milligrams per kilogram of body weight — two people who weigh the same have the same limit regardless of how tall they are. Adding a height field would have made the calculator look more thorough without changing a single number, so it isn't here.

    This calculator does not apply to you if you have PKU

    People with phenylketonuria can't process phenylalanine normally, and aspartame is about 56% phenylalanine by weight. The ADI does not apply to them at all — the limit isn't a number, it's avoidance, and it extends far beyond aspartame to protein generally. See the caveats page.

    How to read your result

    The limit this gives you is not a cliff edge. It already sits a hundredfold below the highest dose used in the long-term rat study the limit is derived from. Going over the limit on one unusual day is not a meaningful event. The number describes a daily average across a lifetime, with a very large margin built in.

    Measured intake in the real world sits far below it. The WHO's own exposure modelling puts even high-consuming adults at 12 mg/kg/day or less — under a third of the limit. Most people are nowhere near.
