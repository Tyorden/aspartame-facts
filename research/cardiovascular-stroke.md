# Cardiovascular Disease, Stroke and Dementia Signals

*Research brief. Last updated 2026-08-05.*

## Bottom line

There is a real, reasonably consistent observational signal linking diet-drink and artificial-sweetener
intake to stroke, cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. It is small (pooled relative risks
mostly 1.06–1.26), it comes entirely from cohort studies, it is exactly the pattern reverse causation
would produce, and the one place it is easiest to check — randomised trials of intermediate endpoints
like blood pressure and blood lipids — shows nothing. The famous 2017 Pase study is the weakest link in
the chain, not the strongest: 97 strokes and 81 dementia cases total, confidence intervals spanning
1.26–6.97, **the dementia association vanished once diabetes and waist-to-hip ratio were adjusted for**,
and sugar-sweetened beverages — which should be worse — showed no association with either outcome. The
erythritol and xylitol papers are a separate matter entirely: those are sugar alcohols eaten in
*gram* quantities that circulate in plasma, and the mechanistic evidence against them is genuinely
stronger than anything that exists for aspartame. Aspartame is never absorbed intact, so no
"circulating aspartame" story is even available. Transferring erythritol alarm to aspartame is a
category error — but so is dismissing the erythritol work, which now includes a human intervention
trial and a Mendelian randomisation study.

## Key findings

**1. Pase 2017 is much weaker than its reputation.** 2,888 adults (stroke) and 1,484 (dementia) from
the Framingham Offspring Study, 10 years. Daily artificially-sweetened beverage intake vs none:
ischemic stroke HR **2.96 (1.26–6.97)**, Alzheimer's dementia HR **2.89 (1.18–7.07)**. Total events:
**97 strokes (82 ischemic) and 81 dementia cases (63 AD)**.
([Pase et al., *Stroke* 2017;48(5):1139–46](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28428346/)) — **moderate as
a finding, weak as evidence**

**2. The dementia result did not survive cardiometabolic adjustment — and this is in the paper.** In
Model 2 the daily-intake dementia HR was 2.47 (p=0.02); in Model 3, adding diabetes, waist-to-hip ratio
and other cardiometabolic covariates, it fell to **1.70 (p=0.17)** — not significant. The stroke
association did survive. ([PMC5405737](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5405737/)) — **strong /
verified in full text.** The widely repeated "diet soda triples your dementia risk" headline is based on
a model the authors themselves superseded.

**3. Sugar-sweetened beverages showed no association with stroke or dementia in the same study.** This
is the single most damaging internal fact about Pase 2017 for a causal reading. If soft drinks damaged
cerebral vasculature, sugar should be at least as bad as aspartame. It wasn't. — **strong**

**4. The authors' own limitations are explicit.** Verbatim: *"the observational nature of our study
precludes us from inferring causal links…"*, *"we cannot exclude the possibility of residual
confounding"*, and — critically — *"we did not adjust for multiple comparisons meaning that some
findings may be attributable to chance."* — **strong / verified primary**

**5. The accompanying editorial was cautious in both directions.** Wersching, Gardener & Sacco,
*"Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages in Relation to Stroke and Dementia: Are Soft
Drinks Hard on the Brain?"* ([*Stroke* 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28428347/)) concluded the
research is inconclusive on causation but that it "may not be advisable" to promote diet drinks as
healthy alternatives. Not a vindication for either side. — **strong**

**6. Larger cohorts replicate a much smaller effect.** Women's Health Initiative, 81,714 postmenopausal
women, mean 11.9 years: ≥2 ASB/day vs never/rarely — all stroke HR **1.23 (1.02–1.47)**, ischemic stroke
**1.31 (1.06–1.63)**, CHD **1.29 (1.11–1.51)**, all-cause mortality **1.16 (1.07–1.26)**. Only **5.1%**
of the cohort were high consumers. The authors state findings need replication.
([Mossavar-Rahmani et al., *Stroke* 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30802187/)) — **moderate**

**7. NutriNet-Santé is the only large cohort that measured aspartame specifically.** 103,388
participants, 2009–21, 904,206 person-years. Total artificial sweeteners → CVD HR **1.09 (1.01–1.18)**;
cerebrovascular disease **1.18 (1.06–1.31)**. **Aspartame specifically → cerebrovascular events HR 1.17
(1.03–1.33)**; acesulfame-K and sucralose were instead linked to coronary heart disease (1.40 and 1.31).
([Debras et al., *BMJ* 2022;378:e071204](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36638072/)) — **moderate.** A
site claiming "no cohort has ever linked aspartame itself to stroke" would be wrong.

**8. Pooled estimates are consistent but small.** Mortality meta-analysis: 11 cohorts, 2,196,503
participants, 235,609 deaths — all-cause mortality RR **1.13 (1.06–1.21)** highest vs lowest, **1.06
(1.02–1.09)** per serving/day; CVD mortality **1.26 (1.10–1.44)**; cancer mortality null, **0.99
(0.96–1.03)**. NutriGrade: moderate for mortality, low for cancer.
([Chen et al., *Nutrition Journal* 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11290234/)) —
**moderate**

**9. A 2025 umbrella review calls the ASB evidence "suggestive rather than conclusive."** Pooled: stroke
RR **1.09 (1.02–1.15)**, CHD **1.06 (1.02–1.11)**, CVD **1.14 (1.00–1.27)**, all-cause mortality **1.10
(1.07–1.12)**. Sensitivity analyses made the CVD and stroke associations non-significant when individual
studies were dropped — i.e. the results are fragile. Most included meta-analyses rated low-to-moderate on
AMSTAR 2. ([Jamali et al., *Nutrition Journal* 2025;24:173](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12613916/))
— **strong as a summary of the literature's quality**

**10. The dose-response curve in the biggest US cohorts is J-shaped, which is a confounding tell.**
Malik et al. pooled the Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study (118,363 people,
3.4 million person-years, 36,436 deaths). ASB total-mortality HRs across rising intake categories:
ref, **0.96 (0.93–0.99)**, 0.97, 0.98, **1.04 (1.02–1.12)**. Moderate diet-drink intake was associated
with *lower* mortality. CVD mortality behaved the same way (0.93 at low intake, 1.13 at highest).
([Malik et al., *Circulation* 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30882235/)) — **moderate**

**11. Randomised evidence on intermediate cardiovascular endpoints is null.** Azad et al. pooled 7 RCTs
(1,003 participants, median 6 months) against 30 cohorts (405,907 people, median 10 years). RCTs: no
significant effect on BMI (MD −0.37 kg/m², −1.10 to 0.36). Cohorts: associations with obesity,
hypertension, metabolic syndrome, diabetes and cardiovascular events. Publication bias was detected in
the diabetes analyses. ([Azad et al., *CMAJ* 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28716847/)) —
**strong.** The RCT/cohort divergence is the whole story in one paper.

**12. Substitution modelling reverses the sign.** Replacing one serving/day of sugar-sweetened beverage
with an artificially sweetened one was associated with **4–6% lower** all-cause and CVD mortality
(Chen 2024, above), consistent with the 14-cohort substitution analysis already covered in
`weight-obesity-rcts.md` (CHD RR 0.89, CVD mortality 0.95). — **weak-to-moderate**

**13. AHA's official position (2018) is agnostic.** The science advisory says low-calorie sweetened
beverages may be useful for adults as a replacement for sugar-sweetened beverages, advises against
long-term use in children, recommends water as the goal, and notes "a dearth of studies" on
cardiovascular harms or benefits.
([Johnson et al., *Circulation* 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30354445/)) — **strong**

### Erythritol and xylitol — different molecules, and the evidence is different too

**14. Erythritol (Cleveland Clinic, 2023).** Witkowski et al.: discovery cohort 1,157 cardiac patients,
validation in 2,149 US and 833 European patients. Top vs bottom quartile of plasma erythritol, adjusted
HRs for 3-year MACE **1.80** (US) and **2.21** (EU). Plus platelet aggregation in vitro, thrombosis in
mice, and 8 volunteers showing >2-day plasma elevations after ingestion.
([*Nature Medicine* 2023;29(3):710–18, published 27 Feb 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36849732/))
— **moderate**

**15. Erythritol has since been followed by a human intervention trial.** 20 healthy volunteers, 30 g
erythritol vs 30 g glucose. Erythritol raised plasma levels **>1,000-fold** and enhanced platelet
reactivity on multiple measures in all 10 subjects; glucose did nothing.
([Witkowski et al., *ATVB* 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11338701/)) — **moderate;**
funded by NIH, not industry. This is a genuinely stronger design than anything in the aspartame–CVD
literature.

**16. Xylitol (2024).** Same group: discovery 1,157, validation 2,149; tertile 3 vs 1 adjusted MACE HR
**1.57 (1.12–2.21)**. Intervention: 10 volunteers, **30 g xylitol** in water, raising platelet
responsiveness in all subjects.
([Witkowski et al., *Eur Heart J* 2024;45(27):2439](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38842092/)) —
**moderate**

**17. Both polyol papers have serious published rebuttals.** Valentine, Söderling & Milgrom
(*Eur Heart J* 2025;46(27):2705–6) argue: the cohorts were patients presenting for elective cardiac
evaluation, hence high-risk and unrepresentative; xylitol is produced endogenously via the pentose
phosphate pathway, which is upregulated in ischemia-reperfusion injury, so plasma xylitol may be *a
benign biomarker of the underlying disease*; and the 30 g bolus is unrealistic — therapeutic oral-health
use is <10 g/day in 1–2 g doses, so 30 g would mean chewing ~30 pieces of gum at once, a dose that
itself causes diarrhoea. ([PMC12257477](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12257477/)) — **strong
as a critique**

**18. The same "biomarker, not cause" argument applies to erythritol, and has independent support.**
Erythritol is synthesised in humans from glucose via the pentose phosphate pathway; it was identified as
a PPP metabolite associated with adiposity gain in young adults in *PNAS* in 2017, before the
sweetener-cardiology literature existed. Cramer, Gonder & Kofler (*Front Nutr*, 23 May 2023, no declared
commercial relationships) note the cohort blood samples largely predate widespread erythritol use as a
sweetener, and that no dietary data were collected in the days before sampling — so exogenous and
endogenous erythritol cannot be separated.
([PMC10242034](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10242034/)) — **strong**

**19. ARIC — the most careful replication — frames erythritol as a marker.** 4,006 older adults without
prior CVD, median 8.41 years. Erythronate (the metabolite) → CHD HR **1.30 (1.04–1.61)**, stroke **1.40
(1.08–1.83)**, HFrEF **1.38 (1.09–1.74)**. Authors' conclusion: circulating erythritol and erythronate
are *"markers of cardiometabolic health and cardiovascular outcomes."*
([*JACC: Advances* 2025, PMID 39983608](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39983608/)) — **strong**

**20. FDA published a formal evaluation of the erythritol paper.** FDA's document states the
observational studies "are incapable of establishing causation" and that "critical confounding
variables, such as diet, were not accounted for," while agreeing further research is warranted. *(The
document is indexed at https://www.fda.gov/media/182122/download; that URL returned HTTP 404 to me this
session and I am quoting the text as surfaced by search indexing — see "could not verify".)* — **moderate,
partially verified**

**21. Erythritol has an independent, non-cardiac regulatory problem.** EFSA's 2023 re-evaluation of
erythritol (E 968) set an ADI of **0.5 g/kg bw/day** based on the laxative threshold and concluded that
both acute and chronic exposure **exceed** that ADI in all population groups; the "excessive consumption
may produce laxative effects" warning was retained.
([EFSA Journal 2023;21:e8430](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8430)) —
**strong.** Nothing comparable is true of aspartame, whose measured intakes sit far below its ADI.

**22. The 2025 aspartame–atherosclerosis mouse paper is real and needs handling honestly.** Wu et al.,
*"Sweetener aspartame aggravates atherosclerosis through insulin-triggered inflammation"*, *Cell
Metabolism* 2025;37(5):1075–1088, online 19 Feb 2025. 0.15% aspartame **in food** for 12 weeks raised
insulin secretion in mice *and monkeys*; the effect was abolished by subdiaphragmatic vagotomy; ApoE−/−
mice developed larger plaques; the mechanism ran through CX3CL1 and was abolished by deleting *Cx3cr1*
in monocytes/macrophages. ([PubMed 39978336](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39978336/)) — **moderate as
mechanism, untested in humans.** The Cell Press release (19 Feb 2025) translates 0.15% as "about three
cans of diet soda each day for humans" — see caveats.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The signal is consistent across independent cohorts on three continents.** Framingham, WHI,
  NutriNet-Santé, EPIC, NHS/HPFS and the pooled meta-analyses all point the same direction for stroke
  and CVD/all-cause mortality. Consistency is not causation, but "one weird study" is not an honest
  description of this literature.
- **NutriNet-Santé found the association for aspartame *specifically*, not just "diet drinks."** HR 1.17
  (1.03–1.33) for cerebrovascular events. That is the strongest single human data point against
  aspartame in this domain, and the site must not pretend it does not exist.
- **EPIC is large and its point estimate is not small.** 451,743 Europeans: ≥2 glasses/day of
  artificially sweetened soft drinks vs <1/month gave all-cause mortality HR **1.26 (1.16–1.35)** and
  **circulatory disease mortality HR 1.52 (1.30–1.78)**.
  ([Mullee et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31479109/))
- **The erythritol/xylitol mechanistic work is better designed than anything on the aspartame side.**
  A randomised-ingestion human platelet study plus a Mendelian randomisation analysis is more than the
  aspartame–CVD literature has. Dismissing it as "just observational" is inaccurate.
- **There is now genetic evidence for erythritol.** Fan et al., *Am J Prev Cardiol* 2025, instrumented
  51 SNPs for circulating erythritol: CHD OR 1.0020 (1.0007–1.0034), MI 1.0015 (1.0004–1.0026), stroke
  1.0463 (1.0010–1.0937). Effect sizes are tiny and the authors cannot separate endogenous from dietary
  erythritol. ([PMC12547948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12547948/))
- **Aspartame has a mouse mechanism now, and it is not a crank one.** Insulin-driven CX3CL1 vascular
  inflammation, replicated across two species for the insulin step, with a clean genetic knockout
  showing necessity. Species and dose caveats are real, but "there is no proposed mechanism" is no
  longer true.
- **AHA does not endorse diet drinks.** Its advisory recommends water, not diet soda, as the target.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **Myth: "Diet soda triples your risk of dementia."** The 2.89 HR came from a model the paper's own
  fully-adjusted analysis replaced with 1.70 (p=0.17, non-significant) after accounting for diabetes and
  waist-to-hip ratio. It rested on 63 Alzheimer's cases in 1,484 people.
- **Myth: "Erythritol proves artificial sweeteners cause heart attacks — so does aspartame."** Erythritol
  and xylitol are **sugar alcohols (polyols)**, roughly 60–100% as sweet as sugar, therefore used in
  **gram** quantities, absorbed largely intact, and circulating in plasma at high concentrations.
  Aspartame is a **dipeptide methyl ester** ~200× as sweet as sugar, used in **milligram** quantities,
  and **completely hydrolysed before absorption — intact aspartame is undetectable in blood in rats,
  dogs, monkeys and humans** (see `metabolism-methanol.md`). The erythritol mechanism is "high plasma
  concentrations of the molecule activate platelets." That mechanism is structurally unavailable to
  aspartame, because there are no plasma concentrations of aspartame.
- **Myth: "Diet Coke contains erythritol."** Aspartame-sweetened colas are sweetened with
  aspartame and acesulfame-K, not polyols. Erythritol is characteristic of stevia and monk-fruit blends,
  "keto" products, and baking sweeteners — a different product category. *(I did not fetch specific
  brand ingredient panels this session; treat as a general statement, not a per-product claim.)*
- **Half-myth: "A Mendelian randomisation study proved artificial sweeteners cause stroke."** The 2025
  paper is titled "artificial sweeteners" but instruments **only erythritol**. No genetic instrument for
  aspartame intake was used, and none plausibly exists.
- **Myth (from the pro-sweetener side): "The cohort associations are entirely reverse causation."**
  Probably mostly, but not demonstrably entirely. WHI found elevated ischemic stroke risk in women with
  BMI ≥30 (HR 2.03, 1.38–2.98) and small-artery stroke risk of 2.44 (1.47–4.04) among women *without*
  prior CVD or diabetes — a subgroup chosen to blunt that objection. Underpowered subgroups, but not
  nothing.

## Corrections to the existing site

**No factual corrections required.** I found nothing published on the site that this research
contradicts. Cardiovascular is currently a declared gap (`index.html` "Site status" callout lists
"cardiovascular signals" as still to come), and the site makes no CV claims to be wrong about.

Two things to get right when this brief is turned into a page:

1. `index.html` currently says the 2025 *Neurology* study "combined aspartame with sugar alcohols like
   erythritol and xylitol — different molecules, different metabolism, different evidence." That is
   correct and this brief strengthens it substantially. It should be extended: the reason the
   distinction matters is not just chemistry but **plasma exposure** — the entire erythritol mechanism is
   a plasma-concentration story, and aspartame has no plasma concentration.
2. Do **not** write a page implying that no human study has linked aspartame itself to cardiovascular
   outcomes. NutriNet-Santé did, by name, for cerebrovascular events. Compare with the site's existing,
   correct handling of the same problem on `the-study.html` ("a site claiming no study has ever found an
   aspartame–cognition association would be wrong").

## What I could NOT verify

- **The FDA erythritol evaluation document itself.** https://www.fda.gov/media/182122/download returned
  HTTP 404 to automated fetch on 2026-08-05. The quoted phrases ("incapable of establishing causation";
  "critical confounding variables, such as diet, were not accounted for") came through search indexing of
  that document and are consistent across two independent search results, but **I did not read the PDF**.
  Do not present it as a verified FDA quotation without re-checking the live URL.
- **The "three cans of diet soda a day" human equivalence for the mouse atherosclerosis study.** The
  claim comes from the Cell Press release, not the abstract. cell.com returns HTTP 403 to automated
  fetch, so I could not read the Methods. My own arithmetic from 0.15% in chow gives ~180–250 mg/kg
  bw/day, which is 4–6× the human ADI on a straight mg/kg basis and would be ~80 cans/day for a 70 kg
  adult; "three cans" implies allometric body-surface-area scaling. **The two translations differ by more
  than an order of magnitude and I could not confirm which the authors used. Do not repeat "three cans"
  as fact.**
- **The monkey dose and duration** in the same paper — abstract-level only.
- **Whether erythritol appears in any specific aspartame-sweetened retail product.** I made no
  brand-level ingredient checks this session. The general product-category statement stands; per-product
  claims do not.
