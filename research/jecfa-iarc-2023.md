# The 2023 WHO Split: IARC Group 2B vs JECFA's Reaffirmed ADI

## Bottom line

On 14 July 2023 two WHO-affiliated bodies published on aspartame the same morning and appeared to contradict each other. They did not. **IARC** answered a *hazard* question — "is there evidence aspartame *can* cause cancer under some conditions?" — and answered "possibly, on limited evidence" (Group 2B, the weakest of IARC's three positive tiers). **JECFA** answered a *risk* question — "does it cause harm at the amounts people actually eat?" — and answered no, reaffirming the acceptable daily intake of 0–40 mg/kg body weight, unchanged since 1981. Both statements can be true simultaneously, and IARC's own documentation says explicitly that its groups "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level."

That said, the honest version is not "IARC found nothing." IARC found positive associations for liver cancer in all three human studies it could use, and it disagreed with JECFA on two of three evidence streams. The correct framing is: weak, confounded signal; no basis for changing intake advice; genuine open questions.

## Key findings

**1. Group 2B rested on three "limited" evidence streams, not one.** IARC classified aspartame 2B on limited evidence for cancer in humans (hepatocellular carcinoma), limited evidence in experimental animals, and limited mechanistic evidence. The "limited" human evidence for HCC is what drove the classification. ([IARC/JECFA Summary of Findings, PDF](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Summary_of_findings_Aspartame.pdf)) — **strong** (primary source)

**2. The three human studies were four cohorts, all using artificially sweetened beverages as a proxy for aspartame.** IARC names them: the EPIC cohort ([Stepien 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25528243/)), a pooled analysis of NIH-AARP and PLCO ([Jones 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35728406/)), and CPS-II ([McCullough 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107009/)). IARC's wording: "In all three studies, a positive association was observed ... either overall or in important subgroups ... but bias or confounding could not be ruled out as an explanation for the positive findings." — **strong**

**3. The underlying effect sizes are small and subgroup-dependent.** EPIC (477,206 participants, 11.4 y follow-up): HR 1.06 (95% CI 1.03–1.09) per serving increment of artificially sweetened soft drinks for HCC, n=101 cases. NIH-AARP/PLCO pooled: no significant association in people *without* diabetes; among people *with* diabetes, artificially sweetened beverages HR 1.13 (95% CI 1.02–1.25). *I could not verify a liver-specific ASB estimate for CPS-II from the freely available abstract*, which reports obesity-related cancers combined HR 1.05 (1.01–1.08, null after BMI adjustment) and pancreatic cancer HR 1.11 (1.02–1.20). — **weak/contested** (as evidence about aspartame specifically)

**4. JECFA's exposure numbers are the load-bearing safety fact.** JECFA estimated mean dietary exposure up to **10 mg/kg bw/day in children and 5 mg/kg bw/day in adults**, and high exposure up to **20 mg/kg bw/day in children and 12 mg/kg bw/day in adults** — all at or below the 40 mg/kg ADI. ([JECFA 96th meeting Summary & Conclusions, PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/food-safety/jecfa/summary-and-conclusions/jecfa96-summary-and-conclusions.pdf)) — **strong**

**5. The ADI's derivation.** 0–40 mg/kg bw was set at JECFA's 25th meeting (1981) from a NOAEL of 4000 mg/kg bw/day — the highest dose tested — in a 104-week rat feeding study (Ishii et al., 1981), with a 100-fold uncertainty factor. — **strong**

**6. JECFA's mechanistic argument.** Aspartame is fully hydrolysed in the gut to phenylalanine, aspartic acid and methanol; "no aspartame enters the systemic circulation," and at doses up to the ADI there were no increases in plasma concentrations of those metabolites. — **strong**

**7. FDA rejected the IARC conclusion outright.** FDA's 13 July 2023 statement: "The FDA disagrees with IARC's conclusion that these studies support classifying aspartame as a possible carcinogen to humans," noting FDA reviewed the same material in 2021 and "identified significant shortcomings in the studies on which IARC relied." FDA also disclosed that HHS had written to WHO arguing JECFA was better suited to conduct the review. ([FDA statement, PDF](https://www.foodpolitics.com/wp-content/uploads/FDA-statement-on-aspartame.pdf)) — **strong**

**8. The two bodies were deliberately coordinated, not rival camps.** Three JECFA members attended the IARC meeting as observers; both secretariats attended both meetings; IARC briefed JECFA on its outcome in confidence. IARC screened >7,000 references and made ~1,300 studies available to a working group of 25 experts from 12 countries. — **strong**

**9. Group 2B is crowded and heterogeneous.** As of the IARC classification list (volumes 1–142, updated 3 July 2026), Group 2B contains **329 agents**; Group 1 has 135, Group 2A 98, Group 3 498. Verified 2B companions include aloe vera whole leaf extract, traditional Asian pickled vegetables, gasoline, and radiofrequency electromagnetic fields. ([IARC list of classifications](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/agents-classified-by-the-iarc/)) — **strong**

**10. Post-2023 evidence has mostly gone against the liver-cancer signal.** A 2026 pooled analysis of 11 prospective cohorts (1,518,411 participants, median 17.8 y, 2,811 liver cancer cases) found sugar-sweetened beverages associated with HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, but "little evidence that ASB intake was associated with liver cancer risk overall or by subtype." ([Watling et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2026;9(6):e2617754](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2850133)) — **moderate-to-strong**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **"Limited" is not "none."** All three cohorts showed a positive association in some form. IARC's working group judged the evidence too confounded to be convincing, not absent.
- **IARC and JECFA genuinely disagree on two of three streams.** IARC: "limited evidence for cancer in experimental animals." JECFA: "no concern for carcinogenicity in animals from oral exposure." IARC: "consistent and coherent evidence that aspartame induces oxidative stress in experimental systems." JECFA: those studies "had limitations in their design." These are substantive scientific disagreements, not just vocabulary. Sites that say "the two agencies agreed completely" are overclaiming.
- **The margin for high-consuming children is 2×, not 100×.** JECFA's own high-exposure estimate for children (20 mg/kg bw/day) is half the ADI. The ADI already contains a 100-fold uncertainty factor, so this is not alarming — but "you'd need 14 cans a day" describes a 70 kg adult, not a 25 kg child.
- **The NOAEL is bounded by study design.** 4000 mg/kg bw/day was the *highest dose tested*; no adverse effect was seen at any tested dose. That is reassuring, but it means the true no-effect threshold was never located.
- **JECFA acknowledged associations at intakes far below the ADI.** It cited NutriNet-Santé (~100,000 French participants, 2009–2021; Debras et al. 2022, 2023), where associations appeared comparing higher consumers (mean 47.42 mg/day) with non-consumers — "at exposures 20 or 40 times lower than the current ADI." JECFA found this not convincing; it did not find it non-existent.
- **Regulators are not perfectly aligned on the number.** JECFA and EFSA use 40 mg/kg bw/day. FDA uses a higher ADI — its statement's "75 packets/day for a 132-pound person" figure corresponds to 50 mg/kg bw/day. *I was unable to fetch the current FDA sweeteners page in this session (404), so treat the 50 mg/kg figure as consistent-with-FDA's-own-arithmetic rather than directly quoted.*
- **EFSA appears not to have issued a formal public response to the 2023 IARC classification.** As fetched, EFSA's aspartame topic page still rests on the 2013 re-evaluation (ADI 40 mg/kg bw/day, "no reason to revise") and makes no mention of the 2023 IARC or JECFA evaluations. Claims that "EFSA rebutted IARC" are, as far as I can verify, unsupported.
- **A 2026 cohort paper found positive associations.** In CPS-II Nutrition Cohort (100,004 adults, 21,356 cancers through June 2017), aspartame consumption was associated with higher risk of obesity-related and non-obesity-related cancers — but with **no dose-response**, which the authors themselves flag as a reason for caution. ([*J Nutr*, online 27 Feb 2026](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(26)00109-4/abstract) — abstract text accessed via search index; full text was paywalled)
- **A separate WHO position is unfavourable to sweeteners generally.** WHO's guideline (reaffirmed in the July 2023 Q&A) "suggests that non-sugar sweeteners not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases (conditional recommendation)." That is a WHO statement about aspartame's *category* that a safety-focused site should not omit.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| "WHO declared aspartame a carcinogen." | IARC placed it in Group 2B, *possibly* carcinogenic — the weakest positive tier, shared with 328 other agents. IARC's own text: classifications "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level." |
| "Aspartame was reclassified in 2023." | It had never been evaluated by IARC before. Volume 134 (meeting 6–13 June 2023) was the first-ever IARC evaluation of aspartame. |
| "WHO lowered / questioned the safe limit." | The ADI was reaffirmed unchanged at 0–40 mg/kg bw. It has stood since 1981. |
| "The two agencies contradicted each other." | They answered different questions and coordinated deliberately. But see caveats: they *do* differ on the animal and mechanistic evidence. |
| "Group 2B means it's as dangerous as [alarming thing]." | Group membership encodes evidence strength, not potency or exposure. IARC: "the cancer risk ... associated with two agents classified in the same IARC Group may be very different." |
| "IARC found aspartame causes liver cancer." | IARC found three studies with positive associations in which "bias or confounding could not be ruled out." A 2026 pooled analysis of 11 cohorts found no ASB–liver cancer association. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- **14 July 2023** — joint IARC/JECFA release date (Lancet Oncology summary online 23:30 BST, 13 July 2023).
- **IARC Monographs Meeting 134**: 6–13 June 2023, Lyon; **25 experts from 12 countries**; **>7,000 references screened, ~1,300 studies reviewed**. Volume 134 published **2024**.
- **JECFA 96th meeting**: 27 June – 6 July 2023, Geneva; **13 members and 13 experts from 15 countries**.
- **ADI: 0–40 mg/kg bw/day** (JECFA, EFSA), unchanged since **1981**; derived from **NOAEL 4000 mg/kg bw/day** (highest dose tested) ÷ **100-fold uncertainty factor**.
- **9–14 cans/day**: a 70 kg adult would need >9–14 cans of diet soft drink containing **200–300 mg aspartame** each to exceed the ADI.
- **FDA equivalent**: 75 tabletop sweetener packets/day for a 132 lb (60 kg) person.
- **Actual exposure (JECFA)**: mean ≤10 mg/kg bw/day (children), ≤5 (adults); high ≤20 (children), ≤12 (adults).
- **Group counts (IARC, vols 1–142, updated 3 July 2026)**: Group 1 = 135; Group 2A = 98; **Group 2B = 329**; Group 3 = 498.
- **Effect sizes**: EPIC HR 1.06 (1.03–1.09) per serving increment, 101 HCC cases; NIH-AARP/PLCO HR 1.13 (1.02–1.25), diabetics only.
- **2026 pooled cohorts**: 1,518,411 participants, 2,811 liver cancers (1,699 HCC, 444 ICC), median 17.8 y — no ASB association; SSB +10% HCC, +15% ICC per daily serving.
- **12 oral carcinogenicity studies** reviewed by JECFA; all but the four **Soffritti/Ramazzini** studies (2005, 2006, 2007, 2010) were negative.
