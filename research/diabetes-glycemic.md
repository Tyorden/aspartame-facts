# Diabetes and glycaemic effects: does aspartame move blood glucose or insulin?

## Bottom line

Aspartame contains no carbohydrate and, in controlled human trials, does not raise blood glucose
or insulin. That much is about as well established as anything in this literature: the acute-trial
evidence is null, the medium-term evidence is null, the one long trial in people with diabetes that
used a near-ADI dose for 18 weeks was null, and both the American Diabetes Association and the
UK's joint Diabetes UK/BDA/BNF statement say so. The honest qualifications are three. First, the
*certainty* ratings on these null results are low or very low — Cochrane's review of sweeteners in
diabetes is formally "inconclusive," not "reassuring," because the trials are small and short.
Second, the cephalic-phase insulin response is a real physiological phenomenon and some people do
show a small insulin blip to sweet taste; the reason this does not matter is that the downstream
outcome it is supposed to cause — a measurable change in postprandial glucose or insulin — does not
appear. Third, prospective cohorts really do find higher type 2 diabetes incidence in diet-drink
drinkers, including one 2023 cohort with an aspartame-specific hazard ratio of 1.63. That signal is
badly confounded by the fact that people at risk of diabetes are the people who switch to diet
drinks, and the leading meta-analysis says so in its own text — but it exists and should not be
waved away.

## Key findings

**1. The largest aspartame-specific synthesis finds no effect on glucose or insulin — at very low
certainty.** Boxall et al. screened 101 articles describing 100 experiments (79 acute ≤1 day, 8
medium-term 2–30 days, 13 long-term >30 days). Pooled cross-over results, aspartame alone vs.
vehicle: **glucose SMD −0.47 (95% CI −1.07 to 0.12)**; **insulin SMD 0.04 (−0.42 to 0.49)** — both
null. Aspartame *with* a nutritive element vs. vehicle: glucose 0.01 (−0.26 to 0.27), insulin 0.03
(−0.24 to 0.30). Aspartame vs. sugars/carbohydrate: **glucose SMD −0.83 (−1.20 to −0.47)** and
**insulin SMD −1.70 (−2.52 to −0.87)**, i.e. substantially lower than sugar. Heterogeneity I² = 50%
(glucose) and 84% (insulin); funnel plots showed asymmetry "suggestive of publication bias"; and
**the authors judged the certainty of evidence for all outcomes in all populations to be "very
low."** ([Boxall et al., *Adv Nutr* 2025;16(7):100449](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205327/))
— **moderate**, downgraded by the authors' own GRADE call.

**2. Five acute experiments in people with diabetes: nothing.** Within the same review: "no effects
of aspartame were found when compared with vehicle or saccharin, for blood glucose, insulin,
glucagon, or for adverse events," and the findings "mirror those in healthy participants." — **moderate**

**3. The broader low-energy-sweetener meta-analysis is also null, and in people with type 2 diabetes
the point estimate goes the *helpful* way.** Greyling et al. pooled 34 RCTs: 55 comparisons (452
participants) for postprandial glucose, 50 comparisons (394 participants) for postprandial insulin.
Random-effects results: **PPG −0.02 mmol/L (95% CI −0.09 to 0.05)**, **PPI −2.39 pmol/L (−11.83 to
7.05)**. Aspartame contributed 9 comparisons. In the type 2 diabetes subgroup, PPG was **−0.3
mmol/L (−0.53 to −0.07)** — a small statistically significant *reduction* — vs. −0.01 (−0.07 to
0.06) in healthy participants. I² 44.7% / 48.3%; Egger's test negative (p = 0.48 and 0.83).
([Greyling et al., *AJCN* 2020;112(4):1002–1014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32672338/))
— **moderate.** *Disclosure: funded by Unilever, a commercial food and beverage manufacturer.
Greyling is a Unilever employee, Mela was one during protocol development, and Appleton and Raben
received financial support from Unilever. This is an industry-funded meta-analysis and must be
labelled as such wherever it is used. Note that Appleton is also an author of finding 1.*

**4. The longest trial in people with diabetes used a dose close to the ADI and found nothing.**
62 people with insulin-dependent or non-insulin-dependent diabetes completed a randomised
double-blind trial: 29 took **2.7 g of aspartame per day in capsules with meals for 18 weeks**,
33 took placebo. No change in fasting glucose, 2-hour postprandial glucose, or glycohaemoglobin in
either arm; adverse reactions were no more common on aspartame.
([Nehrling et al., *Diabetes Care* 1985;8(5):415–417](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3902420/))
— **strong for the null**, weak for precision (n = 62, 1985 methods).

**5. The classic acute diabetes trial — with one small honest anomaly.** Twelve people without
diabetes and 10 with NIDDM drank, on separate occasions, an unsweetened beverage, one with 400 mg
aspartame, and one with 135 mg saccharin; glucose, insulin and glucagon were followed for 3 hours.
Glucose: no differences. Peak insulin: no effect in either group. **Insulin AUC was statistically
higher after aspartame than after saccharin or unsweetened beverage — in the non-diabetic subjects
only.** The authors judged the magnitude small and physiologically unimportant, and noted it was
largely driven by insulin *falling* after the other two drinks rather than rising after aspartame.
([Horwitz et al., *Diabetes Care* 1988;11(3):230–234](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3046854/))
— **moderate.** This is the study a fair-minded critic would cite; the site should cite it too.

**6. Twelve weeks of two cans a day did not move insulin sensitivity.** Non-diabetic adults (mean
age 31) who were not habitual sweetener users drank 2 × 330 mL/day of a carbonated beverage
containing aspartame and acesulfame K, or an unsweetened no-calorie carbonated beverage, for 12
weeks each in cross-over with a 4-week washout. Change in the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index did
not differ and non-inferiority was demonstrated; insulinogenic, disposition and Stumvoll indices did
not differ. ([Bonnet et al., *J Nutr* 2018;148(8):1293–1299](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)16379-0/fulltext))
— **moderate.** *I could not open the paper to read its funding statement; treat funding as unverified.*

**7. The famous "sweeteners wreck your blood sugar" trial exonerated aspartame.** Suez et al.
randomised 120 healthy adults to two weeks of saccharin, sucralose, aspartame or stevia sachets at
doses **below the ADI**, vs. vehicle-glucose and no-supplement controls. All four altered the stool
and oral microbiome and the plasma metabolome. But at group level, **"saccharin and sucralose
significantly impaired glycemic responses"** — aspartame and stevia did not.
([Suez et al., *Cell* 2022;185(18):3307–3328.e19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35987213/))
— **strong as a description of the study.** The same transfer error applies to
[Dalenberg et al., *Cell Metab* 2020;31(3):493–502](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131(20)30057-7),
which found impaired insulin sensitivity from **sucralose** consumed with carbohydrate — and which
was itself challenged by a published re-analysis arguing the carbohydrate, not the sweetener,
explained it ([Cell Metab 2020](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131(20)30593-3)).

**8. Cochrane's verdict on sweeteners in diabetes is "inconclusive," not "safe."** Nine RCTs, 979
people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, 4–10 months. NNS vs. placebo: **HbA1c mean difference 0%
(95% CI −0.1 to 0.1; p = 0.99; 4 trials, 360 participants; very low certainty)**. NNS vs. sugar:
HbA1c 0.4% higher on NNS (−0.5 to 1.2; p = 0.44; 72 participants; very low certainty). Conclusion:
"inconclusive evidence of very low certainty" on HbA1c, body weight and adverse events.
([Lohner et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2020;5:CD012885](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012885.pub2/full))
— **strong as a description of the evidence base.** A debunking site must quote this honestly: the
best answer is "no detectable effect in trials that were too small to detect a small effect."

**9. Guidance. ADA (2025 and 2026 Standards of Care):** Recommendation **5.21 (grade A)** — "Counsel
people with prediabetes and diabetes that water is recommended over nutritive and nonnutritive
sweetened beverages." Recommendation **5.22 (grade B)** — "Counsel people with diabetes and those at
risk for diabetes that nonnutritive sweeteners can be used instead of sugar-sweetened products if
consumed in moderation and for the short term to reduce overall calorie and carbohydrate intake."
The accompanying text states NNS "do not appear to have a significant effect on glycemic
management." ([ADA, *Diabetes Care* 2025;48(Suppl 1):S86–S127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11635047/);
[2025 Summary of Revisions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11635056/)) — **strong (primary source).**
Note that the *water-first* recommendation carries the higher evidence grade.

**10. Guidance. Diabetes UK, jointly with the BDA and the British Nutrition Foundation, 24 October
2025:** "Evidence shows that the use of approved low or no calorie sweeteners may support a
reduction in free sugar intake, weight management and blood glucose control," and such drinks "may
also support individuals at increased risk of, or living with diabetes to transition from
sugar-sweetened versions." With the caveat that switching "should not be the primary dietary
intervention (and [sweeteners] are not recommended for younger children)."
([BDA summary, 24 Oct 2025](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/low-or-no-calorie-sweeteners-deemed-safe-in-new-position-statement.html);
[Diabetes UK position page](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/about-us/about-the-charity/our-strategy/position-statements/use-of-low-or-no-calorie-sweetners);
[Mellor et al., *Nutrition Bulletin* 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nbu.70044))
— **strong (primary source)**, though I could not read the full statement or its funding disclosures.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The cohort signal is real.** Imamura's BMJ meta-analysis found artificially sweetened beverages
  associated with **25% higher type 2 diabetes incidence per serving/day (RR 1.25, 1.18–1.33)**
  before adjustment for adiposity. NutriNet-Santé reported an **aspartame-specific HR of 1.63
  (1.38–1.93)** for incident type 2 diabetes, which the authors state was independent of BMI.
  E3N-EPIC reported HR 2.21 (1.56–3.14) for women drinking >603 mL/week vs. non-consumers. These are
  serious cohorts run by serious groups.
- **Confounding is the best explanation, but it is an explanation, not a disproof.** Imamura's
  adiposity-adjusted estimate falls to **1.08 (1.02–1.15)**, and after calibration for publication
  bias the estimate becomes **1.22 (0.98 to 1.52)** — no longer statistically significant. The
  authors write that "residual confounding in the finding for artificially sweetened beverages is
  also plausible because adults at high risk of type 2 diabetes preferentially consumed more
  artificially sweetened beverages." Greenwood's dose–response meta-analysis (SRR 1.13 per 330
  mL/day) made the same point: BMI adjustment behaved differently for artificially sweetened than
  for sugar-sweetened drinks, which "may indicate an alternative explanation, such as ... reverse
  causality." A Mendelian randomisation analysis found a nominal effect for artificial sweetener in
  coffee (OR 1.265, 1.035–1.545) but not cereal or tea, **and its reverse analysis found type 2
  diabetes predicting higher sweetener intake in all three** — which is what reverse causality looks
  like. None of this proves the null; it shows the observational signal is not interpretable as
  causal.
- **The randomised evidence is thin where it matters most.** Cochrane's HbA1c comparison rests on
  four trials and 360 people at very low certainty. Nehrling 1985 had 62 participants. There is no
  large, long, modern RCT of aspartame in people with diabetes, and there probably never will be.
- **Cephalic-phase insulin release is not a myth.** A meta-analysis of studies measuring insulin
  within 10 minutes of a sensory food stimulus found a pooled effect size of **0.47 (0.36–0.58),
  p < 0.0001** — CPIR is real in humans. Some studies find it for non-nutritive sweeteners
  specifically: sip-and-spit saccharin produced a significant insulin rise in one study, and
  sucralose produced one in "responders" when delivered in solid rather than beverage form. What is
  *not* supported is the leap from "some people show a small transient insulin blip" to "diet drinks
  spike your insulin": Teff's sip-and-spit study, which included aspartame, found no significant
  insulin rise, and the postprandial meta-analyses in findings 1 and 3 find no downstream effect on
  either glucose or insulin.
- **The GRADE ratings on the reassuring results are low.** Boxall: very low for everything.
  Cochrane: very low. Greyling is the strongest of the three and is Unilever-funded. A site that
  says "proven safe for blood sugar" is overclaiming; "no effect has been detected in any controlled
  trial, at low certainty" is the defensible sentence.
- **ADA's higher-graded recommendation is water, not diet soda.** Rec 5.21 (water over both nutritive
  and nonnutritive sweetened beverages) is grade A; the permissive rec 5.22 is grade B and is hedged
  with "in moderation and for the short term." Quoting only 5.22 would misrepresent the ADA.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **"Aspartame spikes your insulin."** Not supported. Nine RCT comparisons in Greyling, 79 acute
  experiments in Boxall, and the 1988 diabetes trial all fail to find a meaningful insulin rise.
  The only positive acute result I found is Horwitz's insulin-AUC difference in non-diabetic
  subjects — small, and attributed by its own authors to insulin falling in the comparator arms.
- **"Sweet taste alone triggers an insulin response that causes insulin resistance."** The first
  clause is sometimes true and person-specific; the second does not follow and is not observed.
  Postprandial glucose and insulin — the things a CPIR would have to change — do not change.
- **"The 2022 Cell microbiome study showed sweeteners raise blood sugar."** It showed saccharin and
  sucralose did, at group level. Aspartame and stevia did not. Aspartame did alter the microbiome
  and metabolome, which is a separate and unresolved question (see the planned `gut-microbiome`
  brief).
- **"Diet drinks cause diabetes — the cohorts prove it."** The cohorts show an association that
  shrinks by roughly two-thirds when adiposity is accounted for, loses significance when calibrated
  for publication bias, and runs backwards in Mendelian randomisation. In NutriNet-Santé the
  "high" exposure group was defined by a median total sweetener intake of **16–19 mg/day** — under
  1% of the ADI. A 1.6-fold risk gradient across a contrast that small is far more consistent with
  the sweetener marking a dietary or behavioural pattern than with a pharmacological effect.
- **"Aspartame is fine for diabetics — the ADA says so."** Overstated. The ADA says water is
  preferred (grade A) and that NNS *can be used* instead of sugar-sweetened products, in moderation,
  short term (grade B).

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- Aspartame alone vs. vehicle, pooled: glucose SMD **−0.47 (−1.07 to 0.12)**; insulin SMD **0.04
  (−0.42 to 0.49)**. Certainty: **very low**. (Boxall 2025; 100 experiments, 101 articles)
- Aspartame vs. sugars: glucose SMD **−0.83 (−1.20 to −0.47)**; insulin SMD **−1.70 (−2.52 to −0.87)**.
- All low-energy sweeteners, postprandial: glucose **−0.02 mmol/L (−0.09 to 0.05)**; insulin
  **−2.39 pmol/L (−11.83 to 7.05)**; 34 RCTs, 452 and 394 participants. (Greyling 2020, Unilever-funded)
- Type 2 diabetes subgroup, postprandial glucose: **−0.3 mmol/L (−0.53 to −0.07)** — about −5.4 mg/dL.
- **2.7 g of aspartame per day for 18 weeks** in 29 people with diabetes: no change in fasting
  glucose, 2-h postprandial glucose or glycohaemoglobin. For a 70 kg adult that is **~38.6 mg/kg/day
  — 96% of the EU ADI of 40 mg/kg and 77% of the US ADI of 50 mg/kg.** (Nehrling 1985)
- **400 mg aspartame** (roughly two cans' worth) vs. 135 mg saccharin vs. unsweetened, 3-hour
  follow-up, 12 non-diabetic and 10 NIDDM subjects: no glucose difference, no peak-insulin
  difference. (Horwitz 1988)
- **2 × 330 mL/day for 12 weeks**, aspartame + acesulfame K: no change in Matsuda insulin
  sensitivity index; non-inferiority demonstrated. (Bonnet 2018)
- HbA1c, NNS vs. placebo in diabetes: **mean difference 0.0% (−0.1 to 0.1), p = 0.99**, 4 trials,
  360 participants, **very low certainty**. (Cochrane 2020; 9 RCTs, 979 participants, 4–10 months)
- ASB and incident type 2 diabetes, per serving/day: **RR 1.25 (1.18–1.33)** unadjusted for
  adiposity → **1.08 (1.02–1.15)** adjusted → **1.22 (0.98–1.52)** after calibration for publication
  bias. (Imamura, BMJ 2015)
- NutriNet-Santé: 105,588 participants, median 9.1 years, 972 incident cases, 946,650 person-years.
  Aspartame **HR 1.63 (1.38–1.93)**; total sweeteners HR 1.69 (1.45–1.97). Sex-specific median
  intakes defining the "higher consumer" group: **16.4 mg/day (men), 18.5 mg/day (women)**.
- Cephalic-phase insulin release, pooled across human studies: effect size **0.47 (0.36–0.58),
  p < 0.0001**. (Wiedemann, *Appetite* 2020)
- ADA Standards of Care recommendations **5.21 (grade A)** and **5.22 (grade B)**, unchanged in the
  2026 edition.
- Joint Diabetes UK / BDA / British Nutrition Foundation position statement: **24 October 2025**.

## Corrections to the existing site

I found **no factual claim on the published pages that this research contradicts** — the site
currently carries almost nothing on glycaemia. Two smaller points:

1. **`caveats.html` heading framing.** The section headed "The WHO advises against sweeteners for
   weight control" quotes the recommendation correctly in the body (it covers "reducing the risk of
   noncommunicable diseases" too), but the heading is narrower than the recommendation. WHO's
   evidence review explicitly flagged cohort associations with **type 2 diabetes**, not only weight.
   Suggest widening the heading, or adding a clause.
2. **Pre-emptive warning for whatever page this brief feeds.** Do not write "the ADA says aspartame
   is fine for people with diabetes." The ADA's grade-A recommendation is *water over sweetened
   beverages of either kind*; the permissive statement is grade B and hedged. And do not write
   "proven not to affect blood sugar" — Cochrane's own verdict on the HbA1c evidence is
   "inconclusive ... very low certainty."

  on it. Treat as unverified.
- **Funding for Bonnet et al. 2018.** Full text 403; the study appears on the International
  Sweeteners Association's site, which is suggestive but not evidence.
- **The full text and funding of the joint Diabetes UK / BDA / BNF position statement.** The
  *Nutrition Bulletin* methods paper returned HTTP 402 (paywall). I have the BDA's public summary
  and quoted from it, but not the statement itself or its industry-involvement disclosures.
- **Whether recommendations 5.21 and 5.22 are worded identically in the 2026 Standards of Care.**
  The 2026 Summary of Revisions returned 403. The 2026 press release and a guideline-summary site
  reproduce the same substance, but I quoted the 2025 wording, which I verified directly.
- **Volume and page numbers for Greenwood et al. 2014** (*Br J Nutr*) — I verified journal, year,
  title and the 1.13 per-330 mL summary relative risk, but not the volume/pages.
- **Whether the Debras 2023 aspartame HR survives sensitivity analyses excluding early cases**
  (the standard reverse-causality check). The paper is behind Silverchair; I have only the abstract.
- **Exact phenylalanine/aspartate contribution to gluconeogenesis at real intakes.** Aspartic acid
  is glucogenic in principle, but I found no human study quantifying it at dietary aspartame doses,
  so I have not asserted "contributes zero glucose" — only that no measurable glycaemic effect
  appears in trials.
- **Any RCT of aspartame specifically (rather than mixed sweeteners) with HbA1c as the primary
  endpoint in people with type 2 diabetes, published after 1985.** I could not find one.
