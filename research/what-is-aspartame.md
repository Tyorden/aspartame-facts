# What aspartame actually is: chemistry, degradation, and where it turns up

*Research brief — chemistry and basics. Compiled 5 August 2026.*

## Bottom line

Aspartame is the methyl ester of a two-amino-acid peptide — aspartic acid joined to phenylalanine, with a methyl group capping the phenylalanine end. There is nothing exotic in it: hydrolyse it and you get two ordinary dietary amino acids plus a small amount of methanol. It is roughly 200 times sweeter than sucrose, so a 12 oz can needs under 200 mg of it, contributing well under one calorie.

Its one genuine technical weakness is real and is not a conspiracy: **in water it slowly falls apart.** The peptide bond and the methyl ester both hydrolyse, and the molecule can also cyclise into a ring compound called diketopiperazine (DKP). This is why tabletop packets in the US must legally carry an instruction not to use them in cooking or baking. But the consequence of that degradation is *loss of sweetness*, not the generation of anything toxic — the products are DKP (which has its own, separately derived ADI), aspartyl-phenylalanine, the two amino acids, and methanol. The methanol was going to be released in your gut anyway; storage degradation front-loads it, it does not add to it. No formaldehyde forms in a warm can.

## Key findings

**1. Structure and identity are unambiguous.** Aspartame is N-L-α-aspartyl-L-phenylalanine 1-methyl ester. Molecular formula **C₁₄H₁₈N₂O₅**, molecular weight **294.30**, CAS **22839-47-0**, EU additive number **E 951**. IUPAC name: (3S)-3-amino-4-[[(2S)-1-methoxy-1-oxo-3-phenylpropan-2-yl]amino]-4-oxobutanoic acid. ([PubChem CID 134601](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/134601); [IARC Monographs Vol. 134, 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/)) — **strong.**

**2. Sweetness is ~200× sucrose, but the honest number is a range.** FDA states "about 200 times sweeter than table sugar." IARC's 2024 monograph gives "approximately 160–200 times as sweet as sucrose." Schlatter's original 1970 patent claimed 100–200×. The spread is not sloppiness: relative sweetness of a high-intensity sweetener falls as concentration rises, so the multiple depends on the reference sucrose solution. ([FDA, Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [IARC Vol. 134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/)) — **strong.**

**3. It is discovered by accident in December 1965 by James M. Schlatter at G.D. Searle.** Schlatter was synthesising a tetrapeptide corresponding to the C-terminus of gastrin, as part of an anti-ulcer drug programme. The aspartyl-phenylalanine methyl ester was an intermediate. The flask bumped while being heated with methanol, the material got on his fingers, and he noticed an intense sweet taste when he licked a finger to pick up a piece of weighing paper. He filed **US Patent 3,492,131, "Peptide sweetening agents," on 18 April 1966**; it granted **27 January 1970**, assigned to G. D. Searle & Co. ([US3492131A, Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3492131A/en)) — patent details **strong**; the finger-licking narrative **moderate** (widely and consistently retold, but I could only reach secondary retellings, not a first-person publication by Schlatter).

**4. It degrades by two competing routes, and pH decides which.** Below about pH 3–4, ester hydrolysis dominates, giving **α-L-aspartyl-L-phenylalanine + methanol**. At pH 5 and above, intramolecular cyclisation dominates, giving **DKP (5-benzyl-3,6-dioxo-2-piperazineacetic acid) + methanol**. Both routes eliminate the methyl group as methanol. DKP itself hydrolyses further to aspartic acid and phenylalanine. Maximum stability sits at **pH 4.3**, where the half-life in solution is about **260 days at 25 °C**; at pH 7 the half-life collapses to roughly **1 day**. ([IARC Vol. 134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/); [Prudel et al., *J Food Sci* / degradation-vs-pH literature summarised in IARC](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jo00247a018)) — **strong.**

**5. Dry aspartame is far more heat-stable than the folklore suggests.** IARC records that under **dry storage at 105 °C for 100 hours, conversion to DKP stays below 5%**. The instability is a wet-chemistry problem: it needs water, time, and unfavourable pH. ([IARC Vol. 134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/)) — **strong.** This matters because the viral "breaks down above 86 °F" claim implies a temperature threshold; the real variable is water plus time.

**6. Measured degradation in real soft drinks is slow and produces DKP, not poison.** Van Vliet et al. bought **111 soft drinks from 10 European countries** and measured aspartame plus its degradants by LC-MS/MS. In their deliberate stability experiment, samples held at **~37 °C** retained **77–79% of aspartame at day 6 and 64–69% at day 15**, with DKP rising to as much as **375% of baseline**; at room temperature (~22 °C) 86–93% remained at day 15; refrigerated and frozen samples barely moved. Free phenylalanine stayed below the limit of detection throughout. ([van Vliet et al., *Nutrients* 2020;12(6):1887, PMID 32599819](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599819/)) — **strong.** Longer-timescale storage work agrees: an orange-flavoured soft drink held at 30 °C retained **93.3% at 1 month and 60.3% at 5 months**, and a lime-lemon carbonated beverage lost **29.5% over 60 days at 37 °C**. ([*Int J Food Properties* 2011](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10942912.2011.565903); [*J Food Quality* 2002](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-4557.2002.tb01009.x)) — **moderate** (abstract-level access only for these two).

**7. DKP has its own ADI, derived independently.** JECFA allocated an ADI of **0–7.5 mg/kg bw/day** for the diketopiperazine at its **24th meeting in 1980**, alongside the 0–40 mg/kg bw ADI for aspartame itself. The basis was a **115-week rat study with a no-toxicological-effect level of 750 mg/kg bw/day**, divided by the standard 100-fold factor. ([JECFA monograph 497, WHO Food Additives Series 16](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v16je03.htm)) — **strong.**

**8. The FDA label rule about baking is real, and narrower than people think.** 21 CFR 172.804 requires that when aspartame is sold as a table-use sugar substitute, the label bear **"instructions not to use in cooking or baking."** But the same regulation *permits* aspartame in baked goods and baking mixes at up to **0.5% by weight** of the ready-to-bake product, provided other approved ingredients are used with it "to ensure its functionality as a sweetener in the final baked product" — i.e. encapsulation or co-sweeteners. FDA even specifies an analytical method for measuring aspartame *and DKP* in baked goods. ([21 CFR 172.804, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/172.804)) — **strong.**

**9. Real-world concentrations vary enormously between products and countries.** Van Vliet found aspartame from **30 to 527 mg/L** across the aspartame-containing drinks, and total phenylalanine from **20 to 332 mg/L** — with the "same" branded drink differing markedly between countries. IARC's occurrence tables give beverage means of **91–277 mg/L**, chewing gum **1,369–1,922 mg/kg**, and tabletop sweeteners **100,000–500,000 mg/kg**. The EU maximum permitted level for flavoured drinks is **600 mg/L** (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008, Annex II). ([van Vliet 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599819/); [IARC Vol. 134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/); [EUR-Lex Reg. 1333/2008](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2008/1333/oj/eng)) — **strong.**

**10. Which products.** WHO's own list: diet drinks, chewing gum, gelatin, ice cream, yoghurt and dairy products, breakfast cereal, toothpaste, cough drops and chewable vitamins. Tabletop brands named by FDA: **NutraSweet, Equal, Sugar Twin** (Canderel in Europe). Formulations differ: Diet Coke is sweetened with aspartame alone; Coca-Cola Zero Sugar uses an aspartame + acesulfame-K blend (Ace-K and aspartame are synergistic — the blend tastes sweeter than the sum of its parts and improves stability). ([WHO news release, 14 July 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-07-2023-aspartame-hazard-and-risk-assessment-results-released); [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [The Coca-Cola Company ingredient FAQ — **company source, treat as advocacy** but authoritative for its own labels](https://www.coca-colacompany.com/about-us/faq/ingredients)) — **strong** for the categories; **moderate** for the blend/synergy framing.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The degradation-products data gap is real and was flagged by regulators, not activists.** During the 2013 re-evaluation, EFSA concluded there were **too few data** on DKP and other potential degradants formed in foods and beverages under real storage conditions, and the European Commission issued a call for additional data. EFSA ultimately concluded exposures were below the respective ADIs, but the point that the degradant literature is thinner than the parent-compound literature is fair.
- **Aspartame really is unstable in a way most additives are not.** A site that says "heat does nothing" is wrong. A third of the aspartame in a bottle can be gone after two weeks at body temperature. The correct rebuttal is about *what* it turns into, not *whether* it changes.
- **Degradation is invisible to the consumer.** Drinks lose sweetness gradually and the label still says "aspartame." For people counting phenylalanine (PKU), van Vliet's finding that identical-looking products differ several-fold between countries — and that the amount is usually not declared — is a legitimate practical complaint, and the authors call for mandatory quantitative labelling.
- **"200× sweeter" is a slogan, not a constant.** It ranges roughly 160–200× and falls at higher concentrations.
- **It is not calorie-free in the strict sense.** Aspartame yields 4 kcal/g like any protein; a can containing ~180 mg contributes about **0.7 kcal**, which is why it is legally and practically treated as non-nutritive. Saying "zero calories" is a rounding, not a chemical fact.
- **Aspartame is being removed from some products for reasons unrelated to evidence.** PepsiCo pulled it from Diet Pepsi in August 2015 in response to consumer sentiment, sales fell, and aspartame versions returned from 2016. Retailer reformulation announcements in 2025–26 continue this pattern. Market withdrawal is a consumer-preference signal, not a safety signal — but the site should not pretend the withdrawals aren't happening.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | What's actually true |
|---|---|
| "Above 86 °F aspartame converts to formaldehyde in the bottle" | No formaldehyde forms in the container. Above ~30 °C aspartame slowly hydrolyses/cyclises to aspartyl-phenylalanine, DKP, the two amino acids and methanol. At 37 °C, ~21–23% is gone in six days. |
| "Aspartame loses all its sweetness above 86 °F" | No. At 30 °C an orange soft drink still had 93% of its aspartame after a month. Loss is gradual, over months. |
| "Heat degradation yields DKP" (the common debunker's shorthand) | Only partly. In acidic drinks — cola sits near pH 2.5–3.5 — the dominant route is ester hydrolysis to aspartyl-phenylalanine + methanol. DKP dominates above about pH 5. |
| "Degrading in the can creates extra methanol" | It liberates the same methanol your gut esterases would have liberated from the same molecule minutes after you drank it. The dose is set by how much aspartame you ingest, not by where it broke down. |
| "Aspartame is never permitted in baked goods" | FDA permits it in baked goods and baking mixes up to 0.5% by weight, with functionality aids. The "do not use in cooking or baking" instruction applies to *tabletop* packets. |
| "It's a synthetic chemical unlike anything in food" | It's a dipeptide methyl ester. Aspartyl-phenylalanine is an ordinary product of protein digestion; the only non-dietary feature is the methyl cap. |

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`origins.html`, myths table row on the 86 °F claim** — currently reads "Heat degradation yields DKP; formaldehyde is a transient metabolic intermediate, not a storage product." The second half is right; the first half is **incomplete and pH-wrong for soft drinks**. Cola is acidic (~pH 3), where the dominant degradation route is ester hydrolysis to **aspartyl-phenylalanine + methanol**; DKP only dominates above about pH 5. Suggested rewrite: *"Heat degradation yields aspartyl-phenylalanine, DKP and methanol — losing sweetness. No formaldehyde forms in the container."* The same wording appears in `research/nancy-markle-hoax.md` (key finding on the 86 °F claim) and should be fixed there too.

2. **`calculator.html` and `caveats.html`: "about half / about 50% phenylalanine by weight."** The stoichiometric figure is **56%** (165.19 / 294.30). Note that `caveats.html` already quotes ~104 mg of phenylalanine per can, which implies 56% of ~185 mg — so the site is internally inconsistent. "About half" understates it; "roughly 56%" or "over half" is correct.

3. **`calculator.html` assumptions table: "Aspartame per 12 oz can — 200 mg — Typical diet cola."** The largest concentration *measured* across 111 European soft drinks was 527 mg/L, which is 187 mg per 355 mL; the EU legal ceiling of 600 mg/L gives 213 mg. So 200 mg is at the top of the plausible range rather than "typical." This errs in the conservative direction (a higher mg/can produces a *lower* cans-to-ADI figure), so no headline number needs changing — but the word "typical" should become something like "top of the measured range; WHO uses 200–300 mg."

4. **`index.html` site-status callout** lists "the chemistry basics" as still to come. That should be updated when this brief is published.

## What I could NOT verify

- **A primary, first-person Schlatter account of the 1965 discovery.** Every version I reached (ACS, Encyclopedia.com, Wikipedia) is secondary. The details are consistent across them and consistent with the patent's filing date and assignee, but the "licked his finger to pick up a piece of paper" specific should be attributed as the standard retelling, not quoted as documented fact.
- **EFSA's high-level DKP exposure estimate of 5.5 mg/kg bw/day.** This figure sits in the existing `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md` brief citing the draft opinion §5607–5611. The EFSA Journal returned HTTP 402 from this workstation, and the supporting publication EN-523 (on degradation products) could not be retrieved either. Treat as unconfirmed at second hand.
- **A manufacturer-published mg-of-aspartame figure for any specific US branded product.** Neither Coca-Cola nor PepsiCo publishes per-can aspartame quantities; the ~180–190 mg figures circulating are from secondary blogs and news. The defensible route is the measured van Vliet range plus the WHO 200–300 mg working figure. Do not cite "184 mg in a can of Diet Coke" — I could not source it to anything primary.
- **The exact aspartame content of an Equal or Canderel packet.** The 40 mg figure on the site is a sound back-calculation from FDA's own "75 packets for a 60 kg adult at 50 mg/kg" statement, but I found no manufacturer disclosure to confirm it directly.
- **"Used in more than 6,000 products in over 90 countries."** This traces to the **Calorie Control Council — an industry trade association, i.e. advocacy.** No regulator publishes such a count. Do not use it as a factual claim on the site.
- **The precise pH of specific commercial colas.** The ~2.5–3.5 range I used is widely reported but I did not verify it against a primary measurement in this pass.
- **2025–26 retailer reformulation announcements** (Sam's Club / Walmart removing aspartame from private-label ranges). Only low-quality secondary sources were reachable; if this is used on the site, it needs a corporate press release first.
