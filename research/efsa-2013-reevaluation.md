# EFSA's 2013 Full Re-evaluation of Aspartame (E 951)

*Research brief. Compiled 2026-08-04. Primary sources prioritised; industry and activist sources labelled inline.*

## Bottom line

EFSA's December 2013 opinion is the single most exhaustive risk assessment ever performed on aspartame: a 263-page document covering ADME, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity, epidemiology, the three breakdown products (phenylalanine, aspartic acid, methanol) and the degradant DKP, with a new dietary exposure assessment across 17 European countries. It reconfirmed the Acceptable Daily Intake of **40 mg/kg body weight/day**, found no genotoxic or carcinogenic concern, found no convincing evidence of neurotoxicity, and stated explicitly that aspartame at or below the ADI is safe for pregnant women (excluding PKU). Its conclusions have been independently reproduced by JECFA in 2023.

The honest caveats are real but narrow: (1) EFSA *did not* rest on animal reproductive data — it found it could not exclude developmental toxicity below 4000 mg/kg bw/day in animals, and switched the basis of assessment to human phenylalanine kinetics, a defensible but assumption-heavy move; (2) the ADI is explicitly not applicable to people with phenylketonuria; (3) EFSA's own worst-case exposure model put high-consuming toddlers at up to 36.9 mg/kg bw/day — about 92% of the ADI; (4) a peer-reviewed academic critique (Millstone & Dawson 2019) alleges asymmetric study appraisal, and EFSA's rebuttal disputes their arithmetic without fully resolving it; (5) conflict-of-interest concerns about EFSA's food-additives panel are documented and predate the opinion.

## Key findings

**The scope of the review.** EFSA's ANS Panel evaluated the original authorisation dossier, prior SCF/JECFA evaluations, the published literature, and unpublished data obtained through two public calls. In November 2011 EFSA proactively published the **112 original studies** submitted to support aspartame's European authorisation in the early 1980s, and stated that **over 600 datasets** were available for the re-evaluation. [EFSA news, 24 Nov 2011](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/111124-0) — **strong** (regulator primary).

**The final opinion is 263 pages, published 10 December 2013.** The public-consultation draft (245 pp., Jan 2013) that I read directly lists **173 separate unpublished study documents** supplied by Ajinomoto and NutraSweet under the call for data, plus a reference list of roughly 300 published citations (my own count from the draft PDF, not an EFSA-stated figure). [EFSA draft opinion PDF, 8 Jan 2013](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong** for the document itself, **moderate** for my counts.

**ADI reconfirmed at 40 mg/kg bw/day.** The published abstract: *"The Panel concluded that aspartame was not of safety concern at the current aspartame exposure estimates or at the ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day. Therefore, there was no reason to revise the ADI of aspartame."* [EFSA Journal 2013;11(12):3496 abstract via US EPA HERO](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/5920329/) — **strong**.

**Genotoxicity: no concern, but with a stated data gap.** The Panel concluded aspartame is not mutagenic in bacterial systems and that in vivo systemic genotoxicity studies were mostly negative. It explicitly noted that in mammalian systems, apart from one valid (negative) UDS study, *"no conclusion could be drawn at the gene and chromosomal level, as no studies dealing with these endpoints were available."* Overall verdict: *"the available data do not indicate a genotoxic concern for aspartame."* [EFSA draft opinion, summary §72–81](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong** (with the gap conceded).

**Carcinogenicity: no aspartame-related tumour increase; NOAEL 4000 mg/kg bw/day.** Three chronic rat studies and one mouse study *"revealed no aspartame-related increase in any type of neoplasms at all doses tested."* Intracranial neoplasms seen in some studies fell within the spontaneous range for the rat strain used. Several US NTP 9-month studies in genetically modified mice (Tg.AC hemizygous, p53 haploinsufficient, Cdkn2a deficient) showed no treatment-related lesions. The Panel derived a **NOAEL of 4000 mg/kg bw/day** — 100× the ADI. [EFSA draft opinion, summary §82–93](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**.

**The Ramazzini (Soffritti) studies were rejected on quality grounds.** EFSA considered the 2006/2007 rat studies methodologically flawed, citing high background chronic inflammatory disease in lungs and other organs and uncertainty about tumour diagnoses. It noted US EPA had concluded many "malignant neoplasms" and lymphoid dysplasias from that laboratory were hyperplasias linked to unknown chronic infection. For the 2010 mouse study, hepatic and pulmonary tumour incidences fell within the Ramazzini institute's *own* historical control range. [EFSA draft opinion §94–105](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf); prior [EFSA 2009 opinion on the 2nd ERF study](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2009.945) — **strong** as a description of EFSA's reasoning; the underlying dispute is **contested**.

**Neurotoxicity: no convincing evidence.** On aspartic acid (a neurotransmitter convertible to glutamate): *"The Panel did not see any convincing evidence of neurotoxicity associated with aspartame,"* and concluded aspartic acid from aspartame was not of safety concern at current exposures or at the ADI. EFSA's press release put it plainly: aspartame *"does not harm the brain, the nervous system or affect behaviour or cognitive function in children or adults."* [EFSA press release, 10 Dec 2013](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/131210) — **strong**.

**Reproductive/developmental toxicity — the pivot.** This is the most technically interesting part and is usually misreported. From chronic studies the NOAEL was 4000 mg/kg bw/day, **but EFSA stated it could not exclude developmental toxicity at lower doses in animals** (a NOAEL of 1000 mg/kg bw/day was identified for maternal and developmental toxicity in one rabbit study). Through a formal Mode-of-Action and weight-of-evidence analysis, the Panel attributed those animal effects to **phenylalanine** released from aspartame — supported by the observation that equimolar L-phenylalanine produced similar effects — and therefore based the risk characterisation on **human** phenylalanine data rather than animal studies. [EFSA abstract](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/5920329/); [draft opinion §106–199](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong** documentation, **moderate** confidence in the inference itself.

**The explicit pregnancy finding, with the actual numbers.** Clinical guidelines put the maternal plasma phenylalanine ceiling for fetal protection at **360 µM**. EFSA assumed a worst case in which a meal already contributes 120 µM, leaving a 240 µM headroom for aspartame. Its concentration-response model found 240 µM would require a **bolus of 103 mg/kg bw** in a normal subject (lower-bound distribution 88 mg/kg, 95% CI 59–125) and **59 mg/kg bw** in a PKU heterozygote (lower bound 50 mg/kg, CI 28–69). To exceed 240 µM on repeated dosing, a normal individual would need a 40 mg/kg bw bolus **every hour**. Conclusion: aspartame up to the ADI, on top of dietary phenylalanine, would not push peak plasma phenylalanine above the fetal-protection guideline. [EFSA draft opinion §200–225](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**.

**Methanol: not the smoking gun.** Aspartame releases ~10% of its weight as methanol. EFSA reviewed methanol genotoxicity (limited data, no genotoxic potential indicated), carcinogenicity (concluded that neither the Apaja 1980 mouse study nor Soffritti 2002 rat study contributed usefully), and development (mouse LOAEL 4000 mg/kg bw/day), concluding no risk from aspartame-derived methanol at the ADI. EFSA also noted methanol occurs naturally in fruit and vegetables at levels dwarfing the aspartame contribution. [EFSA draft opinion §149–165](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf); [EFSA press release](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/131210) — **strong**.

**Cancer epidemiology: negative.** *"The Panel considered there was no epidemiological evidence for possible associations of aspartame with various cancers in the human population."* — **strong** for the 2013 evidence base; note this predates the 2023 IARC review.

**Post-consultation reports did not change the conclusion.** After the 15 Feb 2013 literature cut-off, two papers were assessed in a separate statement: the Gift et al. (2013) evaluation of ERF studies (found consistent with EFSA's conclusions) and the US EPA Toxicological Review of Methanol (Noncancer). Neither altered the risk assessment. [EFSA Journal 2013;11(12):3504](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/3504) — **strong**.

**Independently corroborated in 2023.** JECFA reaffirmed the 0–40 mg/kg bw ADI on 14 July 2023; IARC separately classified aspartame Group 2B on limited evidence for hepatocellular carcinoma. [WHO, 14 July 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-07-2023-aspartame-hazard-and-risk-assessment-results-released) — **strong**.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

1. **EFSA could not exclude developmental toxicity below 4000 mg/kg bw/day in animals.** This is EFSA's own wording in the published abstract. The site must not say "no adverse effects were found in animals." What EFSA did was argue those effects were phenylalanine-mediated and therefore better modelled in humans. That is a mechanistic judgement, not a null result.

2. **The pregnancy conclusion rests on a model, not on a trial.** EFSA's safety margin for pregnancy comes from concentration-response modelling of plasma phenylalanine, with acknowledged uncertainties from *"the limited kinetic data in animals and in the human aspartame dose-phenylalanine concentration response data."* For PKU heterozygotes, EFSA reported that the 95th-percentile lower-bound dose exceeded 40 mg/kg bw in **82% of simulations** — i.e. in 18% it did not, and EFSA relied on the layered conservatism of its assumptions to bridge that. This is a legitimate approach; it is not the same as direct evidence.

3. **The ADI does not apply to people with PKU.** EFSA said so explicitly, and EU law requires a "source of phenylalanine" label. Any page claiming aspartame is safe "for everyone" is wrong.

4. **High-consuming toddlers get close to the ADI under EFSA's conservative model.** EFSA's MPL-based estimate for 95th-percentile toddlers (12–35 months) reached **36.9 mg/kg bw/day**, and 32.9 for children 3–9 — 92% and 82% of the ADI respectively. EFSA regards these as substantial overestimates (they assume every relevant food is consumed at the maximum permitted level), and the reported-use-level estimates were only marginally lower (36.0 and 32.4). Still: the honest framing is "high-percentile young children under worst-case assumptions approach but do not exceed the ADI," not "everyone is far below the ADI."

5. **The Millstone & Dawson critique is real, peer-reviewed, and not fully answered.** Erik Millstone and Elisabeth Dawson (University of Sussex), writing in *Archives of Public Health* (15 July 2019, 77:34), reported that of 154 empirical studies EFSA appraised, **all 73 studies indicating possible harm were judged unreliable, while 62 of 81 (76%) studies showing no harm were judged reliable.** They argued EFSA applied a low bar to negatives and an unreachable bar to positives, breached its own 2009 transparency guidance by leaving key assumptions undocumented, and failed to engage with documented flaws in early Searle studies. They called for suspension of EU authorisation pending review by a conflict-free panel. [Millstone & Dawson 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6628497/)

6. **EFSA's rebuttal disputes the numbers but on a different denominator.** George Kass and Federica Lodi (both EFSA staff — this is the regulator defending its own work) replied in the same journal, presenting a table showing EFSA deemed **21 of 37 "positive" studies reliable** and over a third of "negative" studies unreliable, and arguing the selection criteria were pre-specified and documented. [Kass & Lodi 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7114778/). Note honestly: **the two sides count different study sets (154 vs. 37+), and neither published a reconciliation.** I could not verify which denominator is the correct one from the sources available. Do not present EFSA's rebuttal as a clean refutation.

7. **Conflict-of-interest concerns about the ANS Panel are documented.** Corporate Europe Observatory (an anti-corporate-lobbying NGO — treat as activist advocacy) reported in June 2011 that 11 of 20 ANS Panel members had conflicts of interest under the OECD definition, including Dominique Parent-Massin, who had consulted for Ajinomoto (the largest aspartame manufacturer) from 2005–2008 and for Coca-Cola in 2009. [CEO report, 15 June 2011](https://corporateeurope.org/sites/default/files/publications/efsa_ans_panel.pdf). Two important corrections to how this is usually cited: that report describes the **2008–2011** panel, not identical to the 2011–2015 panel that authored the opinion; and the 2013 opinion's own footnote states that *"member of the Panel did not participate in the discussion on the subject referred to above because of potential conflicts of interest identified in accordance with the EFSA policy on declarations of interests."* Parent-Massin is nonetheless listed among the 2011–2015 ANS Panel membership; **I could not verify from primary sources which member was recused.**

8. **The 2013 opinion is now over a decade old** and predates the 2023 IARC 2B classification and subsequent large cohort analyses. EFSA has re-evaluated other sweeteners since (acesulfame K and neotame in 2025) but I found no announced plan for a full aspartame re-evaluation; EFSA states it is currently re-evaluating the aspartame–acesulfame salt (E 962). [EFSA aspartame topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/aspartame)

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **Myth: "EFSA just rubber-stamped industry studies."** Partly false, partly fair. False in that EFSA obtained and *published* the 112 original 1980s dossier studies, ran two public data calls, held a public consultation drawing 200+ comments, and produced a 263-page opinion with an original exposure assessment and de novo dose-response modelling. Fair in that the unpublished study set was overwhelmingly sponsor-supplied (Ajinomoto/NutraSweet), and that Millstone & Dawson's asymmetry allegation targets exactly this. Concede the second half.
- **Myth: "EFSA ignored the Ramazzini cancer studies."** False. EFSA assessed them in 2006, 2009 and again in 2013, and rejected them on specified, published methodological grounds (chronic infection confounding, diagnostic uncertainty, and tumour rates within the institute's own historical controls). Whether that rejection was correct is contested; that it was reasoned and documented is not.
- **Myth: "EFSA said aspartame is safe in pregnancy without looking at it."** False. The pregnancy question was the analytical centrepiece of the opinion — the entire phenylalanine mode-of-action pivot exists because of developmental toxicity. EFSA also addressed the Danish preterm-birth cohort directly (see below).
- **Myth: "EFSA covered up the Danish preterm birth finding."** False. EFSA discussed it in the summary: a large Danish prospective cohort found a small but significant elevation in **medically induced** preterm delivery with higher artificially sweetened drink consumption — *"but not aspartame specifically"* — while a Norwegian prospective study found a much weaker association, applying more to spontaneous deliveries, and exceeded by the association with **sugar**-sweetened soft drinks. That is a fair and non-dismissive summary of genuinely weak, confounded evidence.
- **Myth: "The 40 mg/kg ADI was invented by EFSA to protect industry."** False. The 40 mg/kg bw/day ADI was set by JECFA and the EU Scientific Committee for Food long before EFSA existed; EFSA reviewed and declined to change it, and JECFA independently reaffirmed it in 2023.
