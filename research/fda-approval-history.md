# FDA Approval History and Current Position: The Regulatory Mechanics

*Research brief. Compiled 2026-08-05. Primary sources prioritised: FDA's own published timeline, the Federal Register, and GAO/HRD-87-46. Deliberately does **not** duplicate `rumsfeld-conspiracy.md`, which covers the 1974–1987 corruption allegation, the Public Board of Inquiry politics, the grand jury and the Hayes decision. This brief covers what the regulatory machinery actually did: the sequence of petitions and rulemakings, how the US ADI was derived and why it is 50 rather than 40, what FDA says today, and what the adverse-event record actually contains.*

## Bottom line

Aspartame is not a substance FDA approved once and forgot. It is the most procedurally contested food additive in FDA history: approved in 1974, stayed in 1975, litigated through a Public Board of Inquiry, re-approved in 1981, extended by roughly a dozen separate rulemakings between 1983 and 1993, consolidated into a single general-purpose listing in 1996, and revisited by FDA at least four times since (2007, 2014, 2021, 2023). FDA's position today is unambiguous and recent: aspartame is "one of the most studied food additives in the human food supply," FDA "scientists do not have safety concerns," and FDA publicly **disagreed with IARC** in July 2023.

Two things a scrupulous site must concede. First, **the US ADI of 50 mg/kg bw/day was not derived the way the international 40 mg/kg figure was.** FDA's original ADI was 20 mg/kg (1974). It was raised 2.5-fold to 50 mg/kg in July 1983 — in the same action that approved aspartame for carbonated beverages, after FDA projected that high-consuming 2–4-year-olds could reach almost 50 mg/kg under the new use. That sequence looks exactly as bad as critics say it looks, and the defence is on the merits of the clinical data, not on the optics. Second, **the "10,000 consumer complaints" figure that circulates cannot be sourced to any FDA document I could reach**, and neither can the claim that FDA "stopped collecting" complaints in 1995. The best-documented FDA figure is about 3,100 reports as of January 1987.

## Key findings

**The 1974 approval was for dry foods only, and set an ADI of 20 mg/kg.** Searle petitioned in February 1973; FDA approved in July 1974 for tabletop use, chewing gum, cold breakfast cereals, and dry bases for beverages, instant coffee and tea, gelatins, puddings, fillings and dairy toppings. GAO records the number precisely: *"CFSAN established the Acceptable Daily Intake for aspartame at 20 mg/kg. For a 132-pound person, this would represent an intake of 1,200 milligrams (equivalent to 60 teaspoons of sugar) of aspartame per day."* FDA explicitly told Searle aspartame was **not** approved for products with appreciable breakdown to diketopiperazine (DKP) — i.e. carbonated beverages — because no chronic DKP studies had been submitted. ([GAO/HRD-87-46, ch.2, pp.24–25](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf); [FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame)) — **strong**

**The 1975 stay came from a data-integrity investigation, and the studies were then audited by two independent teams.** Task force appointed July 1975; on-site investigation 6 October – 19 December 1975; regulation stayed December 1975. FDA then selected **15 of 144 studies** for audit — three audited by FDA itself (April–July 1977: a DKP rat tumorigenicity study, and rat and mouse embryotoxicity/teratogenicity studies), twelve by UAREP (August 1977 – December 1978). March 1979: FDA concluded the deficiencies did not invalidate the studies. ([FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame)) — **strong**

**The 1981 decision restored the 1974 dry-foods regulation; it did not create a new ADI.** Hayes revoked the 1975 stay (46 FR 38285, 24 July 1981) and imposed a novel condition: Searle had to report actual post-market consumption data. FDA notes those reports continued **through 2002**. The consumption estimates the Commissioner relied on were 99th-percentile figures. ([FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame); [GAO/HRD-87-46 ch.7 fn.3](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf)) — **strong**

**The 50 mg/kg ADI was created in July 1983, by raising it from 20, in the carbonated-beverage rulemaking (48 FR 31376, 8 July 1983).** GAO documents the reasoning in full: *"CFSAN projected that at the highest daily intake, 2- to 4-year-olds could potentially consume aspartame at almost 50 mg/kg of body weight … CFSAN reviewed five clinical studies submitted by Searle and five additional studies published in the literature … Based on these clinical studies, CFSAN determined the regular consumption of aspartame at very high levels above 50 mg/kg/day did not result in any adverse effects … Therefore, CFSAN concluded that the Acceptable Daily Intake could be raised from 20 to 50 mg/kg. At this higher level, a 132-pound person could drink about 15 cans of diet soda per day."* GAO's footnote: *"50 mg/kg of body weight was 2.5 times the established Acceptable Daily Intake of 20 mg/kg."* ([GAO/HRD-87-46, ch.6, p.60](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf); [FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame)) — **strong**

**The mechanism that allows 50 > 40 is a documented FDA rule, not an ad-hoc move.** GAO states FDA's method plainly: *"food additive regulations require FDA to use a 100-fold safety factor when applying animal experimentation results to humans … When sufficient clinical studies are conducted, the 100-fold safety factor is not required."* JECFA and EFSA anchored their 40 mg/kg on the rodent NOAEL of 4,000 mg/kg ÷ 100. FDA, having ten human clinical studies, anchored on human data instead. That is why the two numbers differ. ([GAO/HRD-87-46, ch.2, p.22](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf)) — **strong**

**Between 1983 and 1996 FDA issued roughly a dozen further use approvals, then consolidated them.** Multivitamins (May 1984); fruit juices, stick-type confections, breath mints and iced tea (November 1986); ready-to-serve gelatin desserts and clearance as an inactive drug ingredient (December 1986); six final rules in June 1988; prebaked cookie fillings (October 1988); an amendment to 21 CFR 172.804(b) (December 1988); frozen cheesecake, fruit toppings and frostings (June 1989); fruit spreads, toppings and syrups (1989); bulk packaging, hot and instant cereals, refrigerated puddings (January 1992); hard and soft candy, additional non-alcoholic beverages, and beer under 3% alcohol (April 1993). The **June 1996 general-purpose approval (61 FR 33654, 28 June 1996)** was administrative consolidation: it *"simplified the existing regulation by replacing the 23 listed uses as a single category."* The current listing is 21 CFR 172.804. ([FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame); [61 FR 33654](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1996-06-28/pdf/96-16522.pdf)) — **strong**

**FDA has kept reviewing after 1996 — four documented times.** 2007 (Ramazzini's first bioassay, the 2005 NTP transgenic-mouse studies, the 2006 NIH-AARP epidemiology study of >500,000 people, and a two-year rat study of aspartame and its decomposition product); October 2014 (denial of two citizen petitions seeking revocation); March 2021 (review of the Ramazzini Institute's 2020 haematopoietic-tumour paper); and 2021/2023 (review of the material IARC relied on, and the July 2023 public statement). FDA also notes that ERF **declined to supply complete study reports** for both its first and second bioassays when FDA requested them. ([FDA timeline](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame)) — **strong**

**FDA's current position, verbatim.** From the July 2023 statement and the standing web page: *"Aspartame is one of the most studied food additives in the human food supply. FDA scientists do not have safety concerns when aspartame is used under the approved conditions."* And: *"The FDA disagrees with IARC's conclusion that these studies support classifying aspartame as a possible carcinogen to humans. FDA scientists reviewed the scientific information included in IARC's review in 2021 when it was first made available and identified significant shortcomings in the studies on which IARC relied."* On the number of studies: *"the FDA has reviewed more than 100 studies designed to identify possible toxic effects, including studies that assess effects on the reproductive and nervous systems, carcinogenicity, and metabolism."* On the dose: *"for aspartame, a person who is 132 pounds would need to consume 75 packets of this sweetener per day to reach the FDA's ADI."* ([FDA, Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [FDA Statement on the Safety of Aspartame, 13 July 2023](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)) — **strong**

**The US formally lobbied WHO about the review itself.** FDA's July 2023 background note states: *"On behalf of the United States, the Department of Health and Human Services sent a letter to the World Health Organization (WHO) outlining our concerns with IARC and JECFA, both part of the WHO, conducting reviews of aspartame. In that letter, we recommended that JECFA was better suited to conduct the review."* This is FDA's own disclosure, not an allegation — and it is a fact a critic will use. — **strong**

**The adverse-event record, as FDA actually documented it.** Reports rose from 108 in the first half of 1983 to 248 in the second half — i.e. immediately after the beverage approval and the accompanying media coverage. FDA asked CDC to evaluate them in February 1984; CDC's November 1984 report analysed **231 fully worked-up cases** out of roughly 600 complainants. As of **January 1987, CFSAN reported about 3,100 aspartame adverse-reaction reports** (excluding those CDC reviewed), of which about 2,900 had been reviewed and **about 9% were classed as severe**; the 3,100 reports contained 4,423 symptoms: neurological 1,045 (23.6%), headache 983 (22.2%), other 736 (16.7%), gastrointestinal 668 (15.1%), allergic 457 (10.3%), behavioural 263 (5.9%), seizures 175 (4.0%), unspecified 96 (2.2%). CFSAN's conclusion: it *"cannot definitely state whether aspartame is or is not associated with the reported reactions."* ([GAO/HRD-87-46, ch.7, pp.66–68 and Table 7.1](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf)) — **strong**

**Consumption never approached the ADI, and the highest-exposed group was infants.** At the 99th percentile, the 0–23-month group had the highest body-weight-adjusted intake at about **20 mg/kg — roughly 40% of the 50 mg/kg ADI.** GAO's illustration: for a 22-pound child, the ADI is equivalent in sweetness to 25 teaspoons of sugar daily. ([GAO/HRD-87-46, ch.7](https://www.gao.gov/assets/hrd-87-46.pdf)) — **strong**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

1. **The ADI was raised at exactly the moment the new use would have breached it.** This is the single most uncomfortable fact in the US regulatory record, and no honest page should omit it. FDA projected high-consuming toddlers at "almost 50 mg/kg" under the beverage use, then set the ADI at 50 mg/kg in the same rulemaking. The defence — that the ten clinical studies supported it, that FDA's own rules permit dropping the 100-fold animal factor when human data exist, and that JECFA independently landed at 40 on animal data alone — is a good defence. It is not a denial.
2. **FDA's 50 rests partly on Searle's own clinical studies.** Five of the ten studies CFSAN relied on to justify the increase were submitted by the petitioner. This was standard practice and remains so, but "the manufacturer's data supported raising the manufacturer's limit" is a fair characterisation.
3. **CDC did not give aspartame a clean bill of health.** The published version of the CDC analysis (Bradstock et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 1986) says: *"we found no clear symptom complex that suggests a widespread public health hazard associated with aspartame use; **however, we identified some case reports in which the symptoms may be attributable to aspartame in commonly-consumed amounts.**"* CDC's report to FDA used similar language about *"some as yet undefined sensitivity of some individuals to aspartame in commonly consumed amounts."* Sites that cite CDC as a flat exoneration are misquoting it.
4. **Passive surveillance is weak in both directions.** ~3,100 reports cannot establish causation; they also cannot exclude a rare idiosyncratic response. FDA said so explicitly. Do not deploy the complaint numbers as evidence of safety.
5. **The complaint surge tracked publicity, not exposure.** Reports more than doubled in the six months after the beverage approval and the associated press coverage. That is a strong argument that the reports are not a clean signal — but it is an argument about the reports' *interpretation*, and it cuts against using them either way.
6. **FDA's "more than 100 studies" is not a claim about independence.** It counts studies FDA reviewed for toxic effects, most of them from the petitioner's dossiers. See `industry-funding.md`.
7. **The US government lobbied WHO on the framing of the 2023 review.** Disclosed by FDA itself. It does not show the outcome was influenced, but "FDA independently disagreed with IARC" is a slightly cleaner story than the record supports.
8. **FDA's own timeline is not neutral prose.** It is the agency's account of its own conduct, published 30 May 2023, six weeks before the IARC decision. Corroborate it against GAO and the Federal Register where it matters — which is what this brief does.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Circulating claim | What the record shows |
|---|---|
| "Aspartame was approved in 1981." | The first approval was **July 1974**. 1981 revoked a stay and restored the 1974 regulation. The 1999 CRS report gets this wrong too, saying FDA "first approved aspartame for use as a food additive in 1981" — a widespread error. |
| "The FDA never reviewed it again after approval." | FDA's timeline records roughly 30 discrete actions from 1969 to 2021, including ~12 separate food-additive rulemakings, two citizen-petition denials in 2014, and literature reviews in 2007, 2021 and 2023. |
| "The FDA received 10,000 consumer complaints." | **Unverified from any FDA source.** The documented figure is ~3,100 as of January 1987 (GAO). The 10,000 number appears only in activist secondary sources (Mission Possible, Aspartame Consumer Safety Network and derivatives) attributed to a 1995 FOIA release I could not obtain. See "What I could NOT verify." |
| "The FDA's own list of 92 symptoms proves harm." | The list, if genuine, is a printout of *what people reported*, not of *what aspartame causes*. FDA's contemporaneous verdict on the same database was that it could not state whether aspartame was or was not associated with the reports. A symptom appearing on a passive-surveillance list carries no causal weight whatsoever. |
| "The FDA stopped collecting aspartame complaints in 1995 to hide the problem." | **I could not verify that FDA made any such decision in 1995.** What is documented: the 1985 computerised Adverse Reaction Monitoring System was superseded by the CFSAN Adverse Event Reporting System (CAERS), announced around 2002. FDA today explicitly invites adverse-event reports for high-intensity sweeteners by phone, email and post. |
| "Aspartame accounts for 75% of all food-additive complaints to FDA." | This figure circulates without a traceable primary source. It is plausible for the mid-1980s peak — aspartame was the most publicised additive of the era — but I could not source it to FDA. If used at all, it should be attributed to activist secondary sources and flagged as unverified. |
| "The FDA and WHO agree on the safe limit." | They do not. FDA: 50 mg/kg bw/day (since 1983). JECFA and EFSA: 40 mg/kg bw/day. The gap is real and has a documented cause. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- **Feb 1973** petition filed → **July 1974** approval, dry foods only, **ADI 20 mg/kg bw/day**.
- **Oct 6 – Dec 19 1975** on-site task force investigation → **Dec 1975** stay.
- **15 of 144** studies selected for audit: **3** by FDA (Apr–Jul 1977), **12** by UAREP (Aug 1977 – Dec 1978). **March 1979**: deficiencies did not invalidate the studies.
- **24 July 1981**, 46 FR 38285 — stay revoked, dry foods restored, post-market consumption reporting required. Reporting continued **through 2002**.
- **8 July 1983**, 48 FR 31376 — carbonated beverages, and **ADI raised from 20 to 50 mg/kg** (a **2.5-fold** increase), based on **10 clinical studies (5 from Searle, 5 published)**. Trigger: projected 99th-percentile intake in **2–4-year-olds of almost 50 mg/kg**.
- At 50 mg/kg, GAO's illustration: a **132-pound person could drink about 15 cans of diet soda per day**. FDA's 2023 illustration: **75 tabletop packets per day at 132 pounds**.
- **28 June 1996**, 61 FR 33654 — general-purpose sweetener; **23 listed uses** consolidated into one category. Current listing: **21 CFR 172.804**.
- Adverse reactions: **108** reports (H1 1983) → **248** (H2 1983). CDC analysed **231 cases** from ~**600** complainants (Nov 1984). **~3,100** reports as of **January 1987**, **~2,900** reviewed, **~9% severe**, **4,423** symptoms recorded. **251** seizure reports analysed by FDA staff (Tollefson & Barnard 1992).
- Highest body-weight-adjusted consumers at the 99th percentile: **0–23-month-olds at ~20 mg/kg, ~40% of the FDA ADI**.
- FDA today: **"more than 100 studies"** reviewed; ADI **50 mg/kg bw/day**; position last updated **13 July 2023** (statement) and **30 May 2023** (timeline page).

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`the-evidence.html`, "The dose arithmetic": "The ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day was set at JECFA's 25th meeting in 1981."** WHO's own JECFA database records the ADI of **0–40 mg/kg bw allocated at the 24th meeting in 1980**, and *confirmed* at the 25th meeting in 1981. (FDA's timeline says "April 1981," which appears to refer to the confirmation.) Suggested fix: "set at JECFA's 24th meeting in 1980 and confirmed in 1981." This also strengthens the site's own argument on `origins.html`, which correctly says 1980.
2. **`myths.html`: "It has stood since 1981"** (of the 0–40 mg/kg ADI). Same issue — **1980**.
3. **Material omission, `the-evidence.html` and `caveats.html`: the site never says where FDA's 50 mg/kg came from.** `the-evidence.html` explains the 40 mg/kg derivation (NOAEL 4,000 ÷ 100) and then lists FDA's 50 in the same table without explanation, which implies the same derivation. It is not the same derivation. FDA's figure was 20 mg/kg from 1974 to 1983 and was raised to 50 in the beverage rulemaking on the basis of human clinical data, under a rule that waives the 100-fold animal factor when clinical studies are sufficient. `caveats.html` currently says only "That gap is not scientifically dramatic" — true, but the site should say what the gap *is*, because a well-informed critic will otherwise land the "they raised the limit to fit the product" point unanswered.
4. **`caveats.html` / `origins.html` framing: the site nowhere mentions the ~3,100 documented adverse-event reports.** This is a gap rather than an error, but the "10,000 complaints / 92 symptoms" claim is one of the most-cited anti-aspartame talking points and the site has no answer to it. The honest answer is strong: the documented number is far smaller, FDA said it could not establish causation either way, and the reports spiked with publicity.
5. **Not an error, but worth checking:** `origins.html` says a "Public Board of Inquiry unanimously refused approval." FDA's own timeline says the PBOI "revoked the 1974 aspartame approval." Both are accurate; no change needed.

## What I could NOT verify

- **The "~10,000 consumer complaints" figure.** Every version I could reach traces to activist sources (Mission Possible / Betty Martini, Aspartame Consumer Safety Network, and sites reprinting them) describing a 1995 FOIA release. I could not obtain the underlying FDA document, and no FDA or GAO source I reached states a total above ~3,100. **Do not put 10,000 on the site as a fact, in either direction.** The defensible sentence is: "FDA's documented total was about 3,100 reports as of January 1987; the widely-cited 10,000 figure has no primary source I could locate."
- **The claim that FDA decided in 1995 to stop collecting aspartame complaints.** Not verified. I found no FDA statement to that effect. The documented institutional change is the replacement of the 1985 Adverse Reaction Monitoring System by CAERS, announced around 2002. FDA currently accepts adverse-event reports for sweeteners by phone (240-402-2405), email (CAERS@cfsan.fda.gov) and mail.
- **The "75% of all food-additive complaints" statistic.** No primary source located.
- **The FDA's own count of how many times it has reviewed aspartame.** FDA gives no number. It says only that its scientists reassessed the science "each time the agency filed a food additive petition, received relevant information from other regulatory agencies … and responded to objections." The ~30 discrete actions figure in this brief is my count from FDA's published timeline, not FDA's own.
- **The Bressler Report.** This is a genuine gap. I could not locate the document, or any description of it, from a primary or neutral source. Every account I found is activist (Betty Martini / Mission Possible, Activist Post, LinkedIn reposts, WNHO) or a student paper relying on those. Notably, **neither GAO/HRD-87-46 nor FDA's own timeline mentions Bressler by name at all** — both describe the same 1977 FDA investigation of three aspartame studies (April–July 1977) without attaching his name. So the *investigation* is fully documented from primary sources; the *report as characterised by activists* — "98 of 196 mice died," "animals recorded dead then alive weeks later," "the worst 20% never released" — is not something I could confirm from anything neutral. **The site should describe the April–July 1977 FDA validation of three studies using FDA/GAO wording, and should not repeat the activist characterisations of the Bressler Report as established fact — nor dismiss them, since I could not disprove them either.**
- **The exact toxicological basis stated in the 1983 Federal Register for the 50 mg/kg figure.** The FDA-hosted scan of 48 FR 31376 is a poor multi-column OCR and I could not extract the ADI passage from it directly. The derivation given in this brief comes from GAO's 1987 summary of that rulemaking, which is explicit and detailed. Worth confirming against a clean copy of 48 FR 31376 if precision matters.
- **Whether FDA formally responded to the WHO's May 2023 non-sugar-sweetener guideline.** Not checked in this brief; see `weight-obesity-rcts.md`.
- **CDC's full November 1984 report to FDA.** The MMWR summary (MMWR 1984;33(43):605–7) and the CDC stacks PDF both returned 403 to automated fetching. The quotations here come from GAO's verbatim reproduction and from the peer-reviewed Bradstock 1986 paper, which agree.
