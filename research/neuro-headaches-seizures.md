# Headaches, seizures, mood and behaviour: what the blinded trials actually found

*Research brief. Compiled 5 August 2026. Extends `nancy-markle-hoax.md` (which touches Van den Eeden in one line) and `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md` (which covers EFSA's neurotoxicity conclusion). Everything below is new detail.*

## Bottom line

This is the one symptom area where the anti-aspartame case has real, peer-reviewed, blinded-trial support — and it is much narrower than either side usually admits. **Headache is the only outcome with positive randomised evidence in humans**, and even there the trials conflict, the effect appears confined to a small self-selected subset, and the largest and best-controlled challenge study (n=96, government-funded) found nothing. **Seizures have three RCTs: two clearly null, one positive on a subclinical EEG measure and never replicated.** **Behaviour and cognition in children — including ADHD — is as close to a settled null as nutrition science gets.** Mood has one small trial stopped early by an ethics board and one small trial finding worse mood at high dose; neither has been replicated, and both come with serious caveats. The honest summary is not "myth debunked" — it is "no effect at population level; a possible idiosyncratic headache trigger in a minority; regulators say the headache evidence is too thin to conclude either way."

## Key findings

**Headache: the positive trial is real.** Van den Eeden et al., *Neurology* 1994;44(10):1787–93 — double-blind, randomised, four-period (7 days each) crossover, ~30 mg/kg/day (~2,100 mg for a 70 kg adult) in 32 volunteers who self-identified as getting headaches from aspartame. Headaches on **33% of aspartame days vs 24% of placebo days (p = 0.04)**. The effect concentrated in the subgroup who were "very sure" aspartame was their trigger (0.37 vs 0.18 headache-days, p < 0.001); the "somewhat sure" (p = 0.51) and "not sure" (p = 0.51) subgroups showed nothing. No difference in headache duration or intensity. ([PubMed 7936222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7936222/)) — **moderate, but see caveats: only 18 of 32 completed.**

**Headache: an earlier trial found the opposite.** Schiffman et al., *NEJM* 1987;317(19):1181–5 — 40 subjects with a history of aspartame-associated headache, 30 mg/kg, crossover. Headache incidence **35% after aspartame vs 45% after placebo (p < 0.50, not significant)**. ([NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM198711053171903); characterisation confirmed in [DGAC Appendix E-2.41](https://odphp.health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Appendix-E-2.41.pdf)) — **moderate**.

**Migraine: one small positive crossover.** Koehler & Glaros, *Headache* 1988;28(1):10–14 — double-blind randomised crossover in diagnosed migraineurs, reporting a significant increase in headache **frequency** but not intensity or duration. ([PubMed 3277925](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3277925/)) — **weak** (see "could not verify" — the sample size is reported inconsistently across secondary sources).

**The best sensitivity challenge study is null.** Sathyapalan et al., *PLOS ONE* 2015;10(3):e0116212 — 48 self-reported aspartame-sensitive adults and 48 matched non-sensitive controls, double-blind randomised crossover, 100 mg aspartame in a cereal bar vs control bar, 14 symptoms rated repeatedly over 4 hours plus biochemistry and metabonomics. **"None of the rated symptoms differed between aspartame and control bars."** Sensitive participants reported more symptoms overall — but equally after both bars. Funded by the **UK Food Standards Agency** (a government regulator, not industry). ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0116212)) — **strong for the acute, low-dose question.**

**Seizures: two null RCTs.** Rowan et al., *Epilepsia* 1995;36(3):270–5 — 18 people (16 adults, 2 children) who reported aspartame-provoked seizures, admitted to epilepsy monitoring units with 5 days of continuous EEG; 50 mg/kg or identical placebo in divided doses on days 2 and 4. **No clinical seizures and no adverse events after aspartame.** ([PubMed 7614911](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7614911/)) Shaywitz et al., *Annals of Neurology* 1994;35(1):98–103 — 10 children aged 5–13 with documented seizure disorders, 34 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks per arm, standard and 24-hour EEG: **no effect on seizures or epileptiform discharges.** ([PubMed 7506878](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7506878/)) — **strong**.

**Seizures: one positive RCT on an EEG surrogate.** Camfield et al., *Neurology* 1992;42(5):1000–3 — 10 children with newly diagnosed, untreated generalised absence epilepsy, double-blind, 40 mg/kg aspartame vs a sucrose-sweetened drink on consecutive days, ambulatory cassette EEG. Number of spike-wave discharges per hour and mean discharge length rose but not significantly; **total duration of spike-wave discharge per hour rose significantly, by 40% ± 17% (SEM), p = 0.028**. ([Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.42.5.1000)) — **weak-to-moderate; never replicated; not a clinical seizure endpoint.**

**Children's behaviour and ADHD: consistently null.** Wolraich et al., *NEJM* 1994;330(5):301–7 — 25 normal preschoolers (3–5) and 23 school-age children (6–10) whose own parents called them sugar-sensitive; three consecutive 3-week diets (high sucrose 4,500–5,600 mg/kg/d; aspartame 32–38 mg/kg/d; saccharin placebo); **39 behavioural and cognitive measures**. Among the parent-designated sugar-sensitive children: **no significant differences on any of the 39 measures.** ([NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199402033300501)) Shaywitz et al., *Pediatrics* 1994;93(1):70–5 — 15 unmedicated children meeting DSM-III criteria for attention deficit disorder, 34 mg/kg/day (≈10× typical intake) for 2 weeks, randomised double-blind crossover: **no effect on any behavioural rating or cognitive test**, and no biochemical change except the expected ~40% rise in plasma phenylalanine, within the normal postprandial range. ([PubMed 7505423](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7505423/)) Kruesi 1987 (30 preschool boys) and Roshon & Hagen 1989 (12 preschoolers) also found nothing. — **strong**.

**Regulator verdict on headache is "cannot conclude," not "no effect."** The 2015 US Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee evidence portfolio states: *"Due to very limited evidence it is not possible to draw any conclusions on the relationship between aspartame consumption and headaches. DGAC Grade: Limited."* On seizures it is firmer: *"the available data do not provide evidence for a relationship between aspartame consumption and seizures."* On behaviour: *"no evidence that aspartame affects behavior or cognitive function in adults"* and none in children. ([DGAC Appendix E-2.41, 2015](https://odphp.health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/Appendix-E-2.41.pdf)) — **strong**.

**EFSA's position is stronger than DGAC's.** EFSA's 2013 press release: aspartame *"does not harm the brain, the nervous system or affect behaviour or cognitive function in children or adults."* ([EFSA, 10 Dec 2013](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/131210)) — **strong, but note EFSA states more confidence on headache than the DGAC does from the same evidence base.**

**Genetic evidence finds nothing.** Li, *Clinical Nutrition Open Science* 2026 — Mendelian randomisation found **no causal association between genetically predicted artificial sweetener intake and migraine**, while noting the instruments do not isolate aspartame specifically. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667268526000586)) — **weak-to-moderate** (MR on a dietary exposure with non-specific instruments).

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The positive headache trial and the null sensitivity trial used wildly different doses.** Van den Eeden used ~30 mg/kg/day (~2,100 mg); Sathyapalan used **100 mg** — roughly one can, about 1/20th the dose. The null study cannot rule out a dose-dependent effect it never tested. Anyone citing Sathyapalan as a clean refutation of Van den Eeden is overclaiming.
- **Van den Eeden's dropout rate was severe:** 32 randomised, 18 completed the full protocol, 7 withdrew citing adverse effects. Differential dropout in a crossover trial can bias in either direction, and 7 withdrawals *for adverse effects* is itself a datum.
- **Camfield 1992 is a real positive finding that has never been replicated or refuted.** It used sucrose as the comparator rather than a true placebo, measured an EEG surrogate rather than clinical seizures, and had n=10 — but it is a blinded controlled study in a genuinely vulnerable group, and no one has repeated it in 34 years. "Aspartame does not cause seizures" is defensible; "no controlled study has ever found a neurophysiological effect" is not.
- **Walton 1993 was stopped early, and that fact is not nothing.** Walton, Hudak & Green-Waite, *Biological Psychiatry* 1993;34(1–2):13–17: a crossover trial of 30 mg/kg/day vs placebo for 7 days, designed for 40 unipolar-depressed patients plus a similar number of controls, was **halted by the Institutional Review Board after 13 subjects had completed, because of the severity of reactions in the depressed group**. Patients with depression reported greater symptom severity on aspartame; non-depressed matched subjects reported no difference. ([Biological Psychiatry](https://www.biologicalpsychiatryjournal.com/article/0006-3223(93)90251-8/abstract); [PubMed record of the reply letter](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7948460/))
  - Against it: n=13 total, never replicated in 33 years, no depression-specific challenge trial has been run since, and the sole published response was a letter from H.H. Butchko — then a NutraSweet Company medical affairs employee, i.e. **an industry source** (*Biol Psychiatry* 1994;36(3):206–8). The author, Ralph Walton, is also the source of the discredited "92% of independent studies found problems" funding survey that the site's own caveats page correctly calls junk. That is a reason for caution about his advocacy, not a reason to pretend the IRB termination didn't happen.
- **Lindseth 2014 found mood effects at high dose.** Lindseth et al., *Research in Nursing & Health* 2014;37(3):185–93 — 28 healthy adults, within-subject, 8 days on 25 mg/kg/day vs 8 days on 10 mg/kg/day with a 2-week washout. On the high-aspartame diet participants had **more irritable mood, more depression, and worse spatial orientation performance**; working memory was unaffected. ([PubMed 24700203](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24700203/)) Caveats: the comparator was a *lower dose*, not placebo; 25 mg/kg/day is ~1,750 mg/day, near the ADI and far above typical intake; n=28; not replicated.
- **The entire headache/seizure trial literature is small, old and short.** The DGAC's own words: the studies were "approximately 20–30 years old, all had small sample sizes, and all were conducted over the short-term (1 day to 3 weeks)." Nobody has run a modern, adequately powered, dose-ranging headache challenge trial. The confident null is partly an absence of evidence.
- **Self-reported sensitivity is common and hard to recruit against.** Sathyapalan's team had 268 self-reported sensitive people enquire and only 53 attend and consent — the authors attribute this to genuine fear of the challenge, which means the trial may have systematically excluded the most strongly affected people.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Aspartame causes seizures" | **Not supported for clinical seizures.** Two blinded RCTs at 34–50 mg/kg, including one in people who specifically reported aspartame-triggered seizures with 5 days of continuous EEG, found nothing. FDA's own analysis of 251 seizure complaints found the medical records "did not support the claim" (Tollefson & Barnard, *J Am Diet Assoc* 1992;92(5):598–601, [PubMed 1573143](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1573143/)). **But** Camfield 1992 found a 40% increase in spike-wave discharge duration in absence-epilepsy children — concede it. |
| "Aspartame makes kids hyperactive" | **False.** This is the sugar-hyperactivity myth transplanted. Wolraich 1994 tested 39 outcomes at 32–38 mg/kg/day in children their own parents called sugar-sensitive and found no consistent effect; Shaywitz 1994 found none in diagnosed ADD at 10× typical intake. |
| "Aspartame causes depression" | **Unproven, and the one supportive trial is n=13.** Walton 1993 is genuinely suggestive for people with a history of unipolar depression and has never been replicated either way. Nobody should state this as fact in either direction. |
| "Aspartame is a proven migraine trigger" | **Overstated.** Two small trials positive (Koehler 1988, Van den Eeden 1994), one negative (Schiffman 1987), the best-designed challenge null (Sathyapalan 2015), Mendelian randomisation null. Regulators say "cannot conclude." |
| "Thousands of FDA complaints prove harm" | **False as evidence of causation.** By 1988 FDA had ~4,000 complaints via its passive Adverse Reaction Monitoring System — headache 19%, dizziness/balance 8%, mood change 7%, nausea/vomiting 7%. CDC reviewed 517 cases in 1984 and did not identify a specific symptom complex attributable to aspartame. Passive surveillance measures attention, not incidence, and 1983–84 was the peak of aspartame press coverage. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- **33% vs 24%** headache-days, p = 0.04 — Van den Eeden 1994, 30 mg/kg/day, 32 randomised / 18 completed.
- **0.37 vs 0.18** headache-days, p < 0.001 — the "very sure it's aspartame" subgroup; **p = 0.51** in both other subgroups.
- **35% vs 45%**, p < 0.50 (n.s.) — Schiffman 1987, 40 subjects, 30 mg/kg.
- **100 mg** — the aspartame dose in Sathyapalan 2015 (48 sensitive + 48 controls); **no symptom differed** between bars.
- **40% ± 17% increase** in spike-wave discharge seconds per hour, p = 0.028 — Camfield 1992, 10 children, 40 mg/kg.
- **50 mg/kg, 5 days of continuous EEG, 18 self-reported aspartame-seizure patients, zero seizures** — Rowan 1995.
- **39 behavioural/cognitive measures, zero significant differences** in parent-identified sugar-sensitive children — Wolraich 1994.
- **13 subjects, IRB termination** — Walton 1993, protocol had called for ~80.
- **251 seizure complaints** analysed by FDA; records did not support a link (Tollefson & Barnard 1992). **517 complaints** reviewed by CDC in 1984.
- DGAC 2015 grade on aspartame and headaches: **Limited** — *"not possible to draw any conclusions."*

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`research/nancy-markle-hoax.md`, seizures row** currently reads: *"Aspartame causes seizures | **Not supported.** Epilepsy Foundation of America and the Epilepsy Institute (NY) considered it safe for people with epilepsy (per CTAHR 2001)."* Two problems. (a) The sourcing is a 2001 university extension bulletin quoting advocacy-adjacent patient organisations, when **three actual randomised controlled trials exist** (Camfield 1992, Shaywitz 1994, Rowan 1995) and should be cited instead. (b) The flat "Not supported" omits Camfield 1992, a blinded controlled trial that found a statistically significant EEG effect at the ADI. Recommended replacement: "Not supported for clinical seizures — two blinded RCTs null — but one 1992 blinded trial found a 40% increase in spike-wave discharge duration in children with absence epilepsy, never replicated."
2. **`caveats.html`, "Genuinely open questions" list** should gain two entries it currently lacks: (a) a subset of self-identified sensitive people reported more headaches under blinded challenge at 30 mg/kg (Van den Eeden 1994), and no one has run a modern dose-ranging replication; (b) Camfield 1992 has never been replicated in 34 years. The page's stated purpose — conceding what survives scrutiny — is undermined if the *only* symptom with positive RCT evidence is missing from it.
3. **`myths.html`** lists "headaches and seizures" as deliberately absent pending research. That is now researchable; the callout should be updated when this material is published.
4. **Not a site error, but worth knowing:** the US government's own DGAC Appendix E-2.41 misdescribes two studies. It calls Walton 1993 a **"case-control study … with 40 adults with unipolar depression and a similar number of subjects without a psychiatric history"** — that is the *planned* protocol; the trial was a crossover RCT stopped at 13 completers. It also says Camfield's aspartame dose "exacerbated the number of EEG spike waves per hour for these children **without a history of seizures**" — the children had newly diagnosed generalised absence epilepsy, and the *number* per hour was the measure that did **not** reach significance. Do not quote the DGAC's study descriptions; quote its conclusion statements only.

## What I could NOT verify

- **Koehler & Glaros 1988 sample size and dose.** PubMed carries no abstract. Secondary descriptions conflict: the Wiley abstract page describes a 13-week crossover; the DGAC portfolio describes "10 subjects … over 4 weeks." I could not obtain the primary text and therefore cannot state n or dose. Do not publish a number for this study.
- **Walton 1993's exact subject split** (commonly reported as 8 depressed / 5 controls) and the specific adverse events that prompted the IRB to stop it. The publisher page and ScienceDirect returned 403/paywall. The "13 completed" figure and the IRB-termination fact are well-corroborated across independent secondary summaries including the DGAC portfolio; the breakdown is not.
- **Funding sources for Schiffman 1987, Koehler 1988, Rowan 1995 and Shaywitz 1994.** Several of these are widely asserted online to have been manufacturer-funded. I could not confirm any of them from the primary papers and am not asserting it. This matters: if the null headache and seizure trials were industry-funded and the positive ones were not, that asymmetry belongs on the page — but only if verified.
- **The Levy 1995 and Schiffman 1995 critiques of Van den Eeden** cited in the site's own `nancy-markle-hoax.md` (the claim that one subject largely drove the difference). I could not retrieve those letters and did not independently confirm that claim.
- **CDC MMWR 1984 primary text** — cdc.gov returned HTTP 403. The 517-complaint figure and symptom breakdown come from contemporaneous *Washington Post* and UPI reporting (2 November 1984) and should be re-verified against the MMWR before publication.
- **Whether any systematic review or meta-analysis specific to aspartame and headache exists.** I searched and found none. The closest things to a net verdict are the DGAC 2015 evidence portfolio and EFSA 2013, both narrative expert syntheses rather than formal meta-analyses. No pooled effect estimate for aspartame and headache appears to exist.
