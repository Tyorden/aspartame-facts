# Multiple Sclerosis, Lupus, and "Aspartame Disease": the Medical Evidence

*Research brief. Compiled 2026-08-05. Companion to `nancy-markle-hoax.md`, which covers where these claims came from. This brief covers only the medical question: is there any evidence, of any kind, that aspartame causes, triggers, worsens or mimics MS or lupus — and does the syndrome "aspartame disease" exist as a medical entity? Advocacy sources labelled inline.*

## Bottom line

There is no clinical evidence that aspartame causes, triggers or worsens multiple sclerosis or systemic lupus erythematosus. The unusual thing about this area is not that the evidence is negative — it is that there is almost **no evidence at all, in either direction**, because nobody with research funding has ever thought the hypothesis worth testing. A PubMed search for `aspartame AND "multiple sclerosis"` returns **one** record, a 2008 nursing review; `aspartame AND lupus` returns **four**, of which the only relevant one is a 2023 *Lupus* paper cataloguing aspartame as an example of lupus **misinformation**. There are zero clinical trials, zero cohort studies and zero case-control studies of aspartame in either disease.

That absence cuts both ways, and an honest site should say so. The correct sentence is not "studies show aspartame doesn't cause MS." It is: **no peer-reviewed research supports the link, the diseases' own diagnostic criteria and epidemiology contradict it, and the specialist charities in both diseases reject it — but the specific question has never been directly studied, and the most candid MS centre on the subject says exactly that.**

"Aspartame disease" is not a diagnosis. It has no ICD code, no MeSH heading, no diagnostic criteria, and no PubMed phrase index entry. It is the title of a 2001 self-published book by one Florida physician, H.J. Roberts, whose entire PubMed-indexed output on aspartame — all eleven items — consists of letters to the editor, comments and single-patient case reports. Not one is an original research article.

## Key findings

**1. The published literature is essentially empty.** NCBI E-utilities searches run 2026-08-05: `aspartame AND "multiple sclerosis"` → **Count = 1** (PMID 18604921, Whitehouse et al., *AAOHN J* 2008, a general review of sweetener toxicity). `aspartame AND lupus` → **Count = 4**, and three are irrelevant (a 1984 chromatography methods paper, a 1997 erythema nodosum review, the same 2008 review). Broadening to `("artificial sweeteners" OR "non-nutritive sweeteners" OR "diet soda" OR "soft drinks") AND "multiple sclerosis"` → **Count = 4**, none of which studies sweeteners as an MS exposure. ([PubMed E-utilities](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/esearch.fcgi?db=pubmed&term=aspartame+AND+%22multiple+sclerosis%22)) — **strong** (this is a verifiable, reproducible count, not an interpretation).

**2. The only peer-reviewed paper that connects aspartame and lupus classifies it as misinformation.** Porter E, Murphy M, O'Connor C. "Crying Wolf: A Qualitative Review of Misinformation and Conspiracy Theories in Lupus Erythematosus." *Lupus* 2023;32(7):887–892. Its themes of online lupus misinformation include "suggestion of incorrect causes such as infection or aspartame consumption." ([PubMed 37171120](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37171120/)) — **strong**.

**3. MS Trust (UK):** "There has been no published, peer-reviewed research that supports the link between aspartame and MS." Page last updated **22 December 2022**. ([MS Trust](https://mstrust.org.uk/a-z/aspartame)) — **strong**.

**4. Multiple Sclerosis Foundation (US):** "The MSF neither condemns nor endorses aspartame, and has never filed suit against the FDA" — a direct denial of the chain letter's claim that MSF was suing the FDA. The article, by Ellen Whipple Guthrie PharmD, last reviewed **July 2009**, attributes the MS claims to the "Nancy Merkle hoax" circulating since 1995. ([MS Focus Magazine](https://msfocusmagazine.org/Magazine/Magazine-Items/Examining-the-Safety-of-Aspartame.aspx)) — **strong**.

**5. Lupus Foundation of America:** its current Lupus Nutrition FAQs list aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame potassium and sucralose as FDA-approved and **safe for people with lupus**, while adding that long-term consumption of artificially sweetened beverages may increase overweight and obesity risk and that all such products should be used in moderation. ([LFA](https://www.lupus.org/resources/lupus-nutrition-faqs)) — **strong** for the current wording. *The older, more-quoted LFA line attributed to Dr Evelyn Hess — "no specific proof of an association with aspartame as a cause or worsening of SLE" — is **not on the current page**; see Corrections.*

**6. The most honest institutional statement is also the most useful one.** The Rocky Mountain MS Center (Colorado) states: "There is no evidence that MS or MS symptoms are worsened by aspartame," but adds, "we are not aware of any formal clinical studies that have specifically investigated the effects of aspartame on the course of MS." It also notes that limited studies suggest aspartame may affect immune-system cells, and that "the significance of these findings for MS is not known." Posted 20 Aug 2015, updated 25 May 2021. ([Rocky Mountain MS Center](https://mscenter.org/education/complementary-medicine/aspartame/)) — **strong**. This is the sentence a scrupulous site should adopt.

**7. "Aspartame disease" has no standing.** PubMed reports the exact phrase is **not in its phrase index**; a `"aspartame disease"[Title/Abstract]` search returns **0**. The single PubMed-indexed use of the term is in the title of a letter by Roberts himself: "Aspartame disease: a possible cause for concomitant Graves' disease and pulmonary hypertension," *Tex Heart Inst J* 2004;31(1):105–6, publication types **Comment; Letter**. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15061638/)) — **strong**.

**8. Roberts' aspartame bibliography contains no original research.** All 11 PubMed records for `Roberts HJ[Author] AND aspartame` are letters, comments or single case reports — six of them in *Southern Medical Journal*'s correspondence pages, plus letters to *Lancet* (1997), *Neurology* (1995), *Arch Intern Med* (1996) and *N Engl J Med* (1995). His book *Aspartame Disease: An Ignored Epidemic* (Sunshine Sentinel Press, 2001) rests on his own case series of ~1,200 self-selected patients, uncontrolled and unblinded. ([PubMed author search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Roberts+HJ%5BAuthor%5D+AND+aspartame)) — **strong**.

**9. The "MS is really methanol poisoning" claim fails on radiology and pathology.** Methanol neurotoxicity has a specific, well-described signature: bilateral haemorrhagic putaminal necrosis plus optic neuropathy/optic-nerve atrophy, with subcortical white-matter change, in the setting of acute severe metabolic acidosis. MS is diagnosed by dissemination of demyelinating lesions in space and time — periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial and spinal-cord lesions — typically with CSF oligoclonal bands. The two look nothing alike on MRI, and MS does not produce metabolic acidosis. ([Spectrum of MRI findings in 58 methanol-intoxication patients, *Egypt J Radiol Nucl Med* 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378603X16300936); [AJNR 2006;27(2):452](https://www.ajnr.org/content/27/2/452)) — **strong** on the imaging contrast; **moderate** as a formal differential-diagnosis argument, since no paper has been written specifically to compare them.

**10. Neither disease is new, and neither shows an aspartame-shaped epidemic curve.** MS was defined as a distinct entity by Charcot in 1868 and its latitude gradient was already being described in the 1870s — more than a century before aspartame's 1981 approval. The best US prevalence estimate (Wallin et al., *Neurology* 2019;92(10):e1029–e1040, US MS Prevalence Workgroup) is **309.2 per 100,000 adults / 727,344 cases** for 2010, cumulated over ten years — a number higher than older estimates chiefly because it used a validated claims algorithm across private, military and public datasets, not because incidence jumped. For lupus, the largest long-run dataset runs the wrong way for the myth entirely: UK CPRD data show SLE **incidence declining** across 1990–2020 (peak 6.96/100,000 person-years in 2002, down to 4.04 by 2019) while prevalence rose fivefold (21.4 → 107.14 per 100,000) — the classic signature of better detection and longer survival, not a new environmental cause. ([Wallin, PMID 30770430](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30770430/); [Ellis et al., *Lupus Sci Med* 2024;11(2):e001213](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11284910/)) — **strong**.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **"No evidence of harm" is not "evidence of no harm."** There has never been a randomised trial, cohort study or case-control study of aspartame in MS or lupus patients. The Rocky Mountain MS Center says so explicitly. Anyone who writes "studies have shown aspartame doesn't cause MS" is overclaiming — no such study exists.
- **Sweeteners *can* modulate immunity in animals — just not in the direction the myth needs.** Zani et al., "The dietary sweetener sucralose is a negative modulator of T cell-mediated responses," *Nature* 2023 (615:705–711), showed high-dose **sucralose** impairs T-cell receptor signalling and calcium mobilisation in mice, reducing T-cell proliferation. Crucially, the effect **suppressed** T-cell-mediated autoimmunity (and tumour immunity) — the opposite of provoking an autoimmune disease. It is sucralose, not aspartame, and the doses are far above human intake. But "artificial sweeteners can't possibly touch the immune system" is not a defensible line. ([Nature 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05801-6))
- **The rodent immune data on aspartame specifically are mixed and low-quality.** Choudhary & Devi, *Endocrinol Nutr* 2015;62(3):114–22, gave Wistar rats 40 mg/kg/day for 90 days and reported reduced IL-2, TNF-α and IFN-γ, increased IL-4, raised corticosterone and lipid peroxidation, concluding aspartame acts as a "chemical stressor." He et al., *Food Chem Toxicol* 2023;178:113931, gave Sprague-Dawley rats 40 mg/kg for 1–8 weeks and reported delayed proinflammatory cytokine rises in CSF. Against this, Moshirian Farahi et al., *Tissue Cell* 2025;93:102657, ran a tiered immunotoxicity battery in BALB/c mice at **400 and 2,000 mg/kg** (10–50× the higher figures above) — haemagglutinating antibody titre, IFN-γ, IL-4, delayed-type hypersensitivity, spleen and bone-marrow histopathology, T-lymphocyte proliferation — and found nothing: "Aspartame was found to be safe to BALB/c mice immune system." Note the awkward pattern: the two positive studies used lower doses than the null study. None of them is an autoimmune-disease model, none has been replicated, and none involves MS or lupus. Report them; don't hide them; don't inflate them either.
- **Drug-induced lupus is a real phenomenon.** More than 100 drugs have been implicated — hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, quinidine, TNF-α inhibitors, PPIs, terbinafine. So "a chemical you ingest can trigger a lupus-like syndrome" is not an absurd premise in principle. Aspartame appears on none of these lists, in any review, ever. The premise is sane; the specific accusation is unsupported. ([StatPearls, Drug-Induced Lupus Erythematosus](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441889/))
- **There is one published abstract linking aspartame to an MS relapse.** Ross & Koffman, CMSC 2015 annual meeting: a 35-year-old woman with 13 years of MS who developed four active MRI lesions after two weeks of heavy aspartame intake. n = 1, conference abstract, never published as a full paper. It is not evidence of a link, but "there is literally nothing in the literature" is an overstatement. (Also noted in `nancy-markle-hoax.md`.)
- **The MS Foundation's own hedge.** MSF stops short of declaring aspartame 100% safe; its position is that it "neither condemns nor endorses" it. Don't quote MSF as an endorsement.
- **The LFA's caveat is about weight, and it is a real one.** LFA's current page pairs its safety statement with a warning about artificially sweetened beverages and obesity risk. That is consistent with the WHO 2023 NSS guideline covered in `weight-obesity-rcts.md`, and should not be omitted when quoting LFA.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Aspartame causes multiple sclerosis | **Unsupported.** No peer-reviewed research supports it (MS Trust); one PubMed record exists for the two terms combined, and it is a general review. |
| Many people diagnosed with MS actually have methanol poisoning from diet soda | **False.** Methanol neurotoxicity has a distinct imaging signature (bilateral putaminal necrosis + optic neuropathy) and presents with acute metabolic acidosis. MS requires demyelinating lesions disseminated in space and time. |
| Stopping aspartame reverses MS | **Unsupported.** No trial has ever tested it. MS is relapsing-remitting in ~85% of cases at onset, so unaided remissions are routine and are the obvious explanation for testimonials. |
| Aspartame causes or worsens lupus | **Unsupported.** LFA lists aspartame as safe for people with lupus; the only peer-reviewed paper connecting the two classifies the claim as misinformation. |
| The MS Foundation sued the FDA over aspartame | **False.** MSF: "has never filed suit against the FDA." |
| "Aspartame disease" is a recognised syndrome | **False.** No ICD code, no MeSH heading, not in PubMed's phrase index; the term originates in a 2001 self-published book. |
| Dr H.J. Roberts published extensive research documenting it | **Misleading.** His 11 PubMed-indexed aspartame items are all letters, comments or case reports — no original research articles, no controlled studies. |
| MS and lupus are epidemics that began with aspartame | **False.** MS described 1868, latitude gradient described 1877. UK SLE incidence *fell* between 1990 and 2020; rising prevalence reflects survival and ascertainment. |

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`origins.html`, claims table:** the row "MS is often 'really' methanol toxicity from aspartame — False. Rejected by the MS Foundation, MS Trust, National MS Society and FDA" attributes a position to the **National MS Society** that I could **not verify from any nationalmssociety.org page**. Their site returned 404 on the pages I tried, no Wayback snapshot was available for the obvious URL, and every "National MS Society says aspartame is a disproven theory" quote traces to third parties (HowStuffWorks, msviewsandnews.org). Either drop NMSS from that list, or attribute it as reported second-hand. MS Trust, MS Foundation and Rocky Mountain MS Center are all directly verifiable and are enough.
2. **`origins.html`:** the row "Aspartame causes or worsens lupus — False. Rejected by the Lupus Foundation of America" is accurate but should be softened from "False" to **"Unsupported"** in the same way the MS row is, and the LFA's own weight/obesity caveat should be acknowledged wherever LFA is quoted. LFA does not say aspartame is harmless in every respect; it says it is safe for lupus and should be consumed in moderation.
3. **`research/nancy-markle-hoax.md`, finding #5 and source #5:** the Evelyn Hess quote ("no specific proof of an association with aspartame as a cause or worsening of SLE") is cited to `https://www.lupus.org/resources/lupus-nutrition-faqs`. **That quote is not on that page.** The current page's aspartame text is different (see finding 5 above). The Hess wording appears only on secondary and activist pages. If the site ever surfaces the Hess quote, it needs a verifiable primary or archived source first.
4. **General framing across the site:** where MS is concerned, prefer "no published, peer-reviewed research supports the link" (MS Trust's exact wording) over "studies show no link." No study has looked. The stronger-sounding phrasing is the one that is false.

## What I could NOT verify

- **Any National MS Society statement, from the Society's own site.** `nationalmssociety.org` returned 404 on the URLs tried and its search endpoint is not fetchable; the Wayback availability API had no snapshot for the obvious aspartame URL. The widely-repeated "the National MS Society calls it a disproven theory" is reported only second-hand. See Corrections #1.
- **The Evelyn Hess / Lupus Foundation of America quotation.** Not present on the current LFA nutrition FAQ page. Not located on any lupus.org URL or archive I could reach. Treat as unverified.
- **The exact LFA wording**, verbatim. The fetch returned a paraphrase plus a truncated 125-character quote fragment; the substance ("safe for people with lupus", plus an obesity caveat and a moderation recommendation) is clear, but I do not have a full verbatim sentence to quote. Re-read the page before putting quotation marks around anything.
- **Whether ICD-10 or ICD-11 contains any aspartame-related entity.** The WHO ICD-11 browser is a JavaScript application and its search could not be run through WebFetch. My claim that "aspartame disease" has no ICD code is an inference from its total absence from indexed medical literature, not from a direct ICD query. State it as "not a recognised diagnosis" rather than "has no ICD code" unless someone checks directly.
- **The Wallin 2019 paper's own discussion** of ascertainment vs true increase. The *Neurology* full text returned HTTP 403 twice; the prevalence figures come from the PubMed abstract, which is reliable, but the attribution of the increase to methodology is my reading of the abstract's framing plus the Society's summary, not a quoted sentence from the paper.
- **The Zani 2023 *Nature* paper's methods in detail** (exact sucralose dose in human-equivalent terms, which autoimmune models were used). Taken from the abstract and secondary coverage, not the full text.
- **The full text of any of the three rodent immune studies.** Doses, durations and headline conclusions come from PubMed abstracts. I have not assessed their internal validity, group sizes, or multiplicity corrections — and the Choudhary & Devi output in particular is one of a long series of similar papers from a single laboratory, which is a pattern worth checking before citing.
- **Whether MS *incidence* (as opposed to prevalence) has risen since 1981.** I did not research this; the lupus incidence data are solid, the MS equivalent is not covered here. A future brief should fill this in, because "MS incidence has been flat/declining in country X" would be a stronger rebuttal than the prevalence argument.
