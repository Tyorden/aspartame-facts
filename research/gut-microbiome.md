# Aspartame and the gut microbiome

*Research brief. Written 2026-08-05. Covers the 2014 Suez/Elinav *Nature* paper, the 2022 Suez/Elinav *Cell* trial, and the human evidence for aspartame specifically.*

## Bottom line

The two famous "sweeteners wreck your gut" papers are real, well-conducted, and **do not say what the headlines said about aspartame**. In the 2014 *Nature* paper, aspartame appeared in exactly one mouse experiment; every human experiment in that paper, and every mechanistic experiment that established the microbiome as the cause, used **saccharin only**. In the 2022 *Cell* trial — the best human evidence in this area — aspartame was one of four sweeteners tested in 20 people each, and it was one of the **two that did *not* impair glycaemic response**. Saccharin and sucralose did; aspartame and stevia did not.

The honest residue is not nothing. The 2022 trial did find that aspartame, like all four sweeteners, measurably shifted stool and oral microbiome composition and the plasma metabolome relative to controls, at a dose of roughly one can of diet soda a day. Those shifts were small, largely reversible, and not accompanied by any glycaemic change — but "aspartame is completely inert to the gut" is an overclaim the site should not make. The defensible statement is: **at real-world intakes, aspartame produces detectable but small microbiome shifts with no demonstrated metabolic or clinical consequence, and the glucose-intolerance finding that made these papers famous does not extend to it.**

## Key findings

**1. The 2014 *Nature* paper tested aspartame in mice only — never in a human.** Suez et al. gave 10-week-old C57Bl/6 mice commercial saccharin, sucralose or aspartame formulations in drinking water for 11 weeks, against glucose, sucrose and plain-water controls; all three sweetener arms developed glucose intolerance. Every subsequent experiment in the paper — antibiotic reversal, germ-free faecal transplant, 16S/metagenomic sequencing — used **saccharin alone**. The human work was (a) a cross-sectional analysis of **381 non-diabetic adults** in an ongoing nutrition cohort, with sweetener intake from a food-frequency questionnaire, and (b) an intervention in **7 healthy volunteers given saccharin at 5 mg/kg/day for one week**, of whom 4 were "responders" and 3 were not. Aspartame was not administered to a single human subject. [Nature 2014;514:181–186](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25231862/) — **strong (for what it actually shows); the aspartame-in-humans claim is unsupported**

**2. The mouse doses in the 2014 paper were far above human intakes.** An industry-funded review calculated the aspartame arm at roughly **1,333 mg/kg/day — about 30× the 40 mg/kg EFSA ADI**. The formulations used were commercial table-top products (e.g. Sweet'N Low Gold, ~4% aspartame), the rest being glucose bulking agent. I could **not** independently verify the 1,333 mg/kg figure against the *Nature* methods section, which is paywalled — treat it as a derived estimate from a source with a conflict of interest. [Lobach, Roberts & Rowland, *Food Chem Toxicol* 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30557670/) — **moderate; industry-funded (Calorie Control Council paid Intertek to prepare the manuscript), flagged as such by [Marion Nestle](https://www.foodpolitics.com/2019/03/industry-funded-study-of-the-week-artificial-sweeteners-and-the-microbiome/)**

**3. The 2022 *Cell* trial is the key human evidence, and aspartame came out clean on glycaemia.** Suez et al. screened ~1,400 people for strict non-consumption of sweeteners, randomised **120 into six arms of 20**: saccharin, sucralose, aspartame, stevia, a glucose-vehicle control, and a no-supplement control. Two weeks of supplementation. **Saccharin and sucralose significantly impaired glycaemic responses; aspartame and stevia did not.** [Cell 2022;185(18):3307–3328.e19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35987213/) — **strong for a 20-per-arm RCT; small and short**

**4. But all four sweeteners, aspartame included, shifted the microbiome and metabolome.** The paper's own summary: "each administered NNS distinctly altered stool and oral microbiome and plasma metabolome." In the aspartame arm specifically, oral *Porphyromonas* fell, plasma **kynurenine** (a metabolite associated with diabetes risk) rose, and *Bacteroides fragilis* and *B. acidifaciens* correlated positively with glucose AUC while *B. coprocola* correlated negatively. Most microbiome changes appeared within the first week and **reverted to baseline after supplementation stopped**. [Cell 2022, as reported by Healio, 31 Oct 2022](https://www.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20221031/nonnutritive-sweeteners-alter-microbiome-composition-glycemia-in-healthy-adults) — **moderate; concede this**

**5. The one dedicated aspartame RCT with microbiome endpoints was null.** Ahmad, Friel & Mackay gave 17 healthy adults **425 mg/day aspartame (14% of ADI)** and 136 mg/day sucralose (20% of ADI) for 14 days each in a double-blind crossover with a 4-week washout, with 16S rRNA (V4) sequencing and gas-chromatography SCFAs. Aspartame produced **no change in diversity (Shannon index −0.24%, p = 0.96)**, no change in taxa, no change in community structure, and no change in faecal SCFAs. The authors themselves note the trial was powered for blood glucose, not microbiome. [Nutrients 2020;12(11):3408](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7694690/) — **moderate; underpowered null, one author disclosed a paid PepsiCo-sponsored speaking engagement**

**6. The 2014 saccharin result itself failed to replicate.** Serrano et al. randomised 54 healthy adults (46 analysed, 10–13 per arm) to placebo, saccharin at **400 mg/day — the full 5 mg/kg ADI** — lactisole, or both, for two weeks, alongside a 10-week mouse study at 250 mg/kg/day. Neither humans nor mice showed any change in glucose or hormonal response to an OGTT, microbial diversity or composition at any taxonomic level, faecal metabolites or SCFAs. NIH-funded, no competing interests declared. This matters for aspartame indirectly: the study that generalised to aspartame in the press did not hold up even for its own lead compound. [Microbiome 2021;9:11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7802287/) — **strong**

**7. There is a clean mechanistic reason to expect aspartame to behave differently from sucralose, saccharin and acesulfame-K.** Aspartame is hydrolysed completely in the gut lumen and gut wall before absorption; EFSA states intact aspartame in blood is "undetectable in several studies conducted in various species, including rats, dogs, monkeys and humans." Essentially none of the intact molecule reaches the colon, where the bulk of the microbiome lives. Sucralose, saccharin and acesulfame-K are poorly metabolised and largely excreted unchanged, so a substantial fraction *does* transit the colon. (See the sibling brief `metabolism-methanol.md` for the primary EFSA citations.) — **strong mechanistically; note it does not fully explain the 2022 *Cell* aspartame-arm findings**

**8. Large real-world metabolomics shows no disruption at heavy aspartame intakes.** In CARDIA, 2,160 young adults had 549 plasma metabolites profiled by LC-MS. Heavy aspartame consumption (**≥5 servings/day**) was associated only with caffeine metabolites and plasma saccharin — i.e. with what people drink alongside it — and with **no** sugar, carbohydrate, lipid, amino-acid or other metabolite signatures of metabolic disruption. NHLBI-funded. [Steffen et al., *Journal of Diabetes* 2025;17(8):e70138](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12340425/) — **moderate (cross-sectional, but large and directly on the metabolite readouts a microbiome effect would be expected to move)**

**9. The 2023 acesulfame-K/sucralose work is real but is not about aspartame.** Two small pilots of thrice-daily diet soda containing sucralose and acesulfame-K (n = 17 for one week, n = 8 for eight weeks) found increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria/Enterobacteriaceae at eight weeks and increased *Bacteroides* taxa at one week versus sparkling water. Separately, Schiffman et al. reported that **sucralose-6-acetate** — an impurity and gut metabolite of sucralose, not of aspartame — was clastogenic in vitro and that both it and sucralose impaired intestinal barrier integrity in human colon epithelium. Neither line of work involves aspartame, and the sucralose-6-acetate paper is in vitro only. [Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 2023, doi:10.1139/apnm-2022-0471](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/apnm-2022-0471); [J Toxicol Environ Health B, 29 May 2023, doi:10.1080/10937404.2023.2213903](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10937404.2023.2213903) — **weak-to-moderate; irrelevant to aspartame except as a contrast**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **Aspartame is not microbiome-inert.** The best-designed human trial in the field found statistically significant stool and oral microbiome changes and plasma metabolome changes in the aspartame arm, at ~240 mg/day. The site must not say "aspartame does not affect gut bacteria." It should say the changes were small, reversible, and unaccompanied by any glycaemic effect.
- **Kynurenine rose in the aspartame group.** Kynurenine is associated with diabetes risk in observational data. This is a single finding in a 20-person arm over two weeks, with no accompanying glucose signal, and I have not seen it replicated — but it is a real reported result and should not be omitted.
- **20 per arm, 14 days.** Absence of a glycaemic effect for aspartame in the 2022 trial is a null in a very small, very short study, not a demonstration of safety. The Ahmad trial (n = 17) was explicitly underpowered for microbiome endpoints. Both nulls are weak nulls.
- **The 2022 cohort was unusually unrepresentative** — healthy, metabolically normal, and screened for having consumed *no* sweeteners for six months, median age ~30, 65% women. Whether the same holds in people with prediabetes, obesity or long-term habitual use is untested.
- **The mechanistic argument has a hole.** If aspartame is fully hydrolysed before the colon, the 2022 stool microbiome findings need another explanation — sweet-taste-receptor signalling, upper-GI effects, or chance in a multi-comparison design. I could not find a resolution of this tension in the literature, and the site should not pretend there is one.
- **The strongest reassuring reviews are industry-linked.** The Lobach review was paid for by the Calorie Control Council (advocacy). The 2023 Conz/Salmona/Diomede *Nutrients* review, which is the most careful compound-by-compound summary, discloses funding and a 30-year collaboration with confectionery manufacturer Perfetti Van Melle. Neither is disqualifying, but neither should be cited without the label.
- **The field's own 2026 verdict is agnostic.** A 2026 *Nutrients* review of eight sweeteners concludes that "current research evidence does not support a class-wide conclusion that NAS are uniformly harmful or safe" — which cuts against confident claims in either direction. [doi:10.3390/nu18142402](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18142402)

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

**Myth: "A Nature study proved artificial sweeteners including aspartame give you glucose intolerance by wrecking your gut bacteria."**
Aspartame appeared only in the initial 11-week mouse drinking-water experiment, at a dose an industry review estimates at ~30× the human ADI. Everything that established microbiome causation — antibiotics, germ-free transfer, sequencing — used saccharin. The human intervention was 7 people on saccharin. Aspartame was never given to a human in that paper. Media coverage collapsed "saccharin in 7 people" into "artificial sweeteners."

**Myth: "The 2022 Cell study showed sweeteners raise blood sugar."**
It showed *saccharin and sucralose* raised glycaemic responses. Aspartame and stevia did not. Headlines of the form "Non-nutritive sweeteners alter microbiome composition, glycemia in healthy adults" are the generalisation step in action: true of the paper as a whole, false of two of its four arms.

**Myth: "Sucralose damages the gut lining, so aspartame must too."**
The gut-barrier findings are about sucralose and its acetate impurity, in vitro. Aspartame is a dipeptide methyl ester broken into phenylalanine, aspartate and methanol before absorption; the intact molecule does not reach the colon in meaningful quantity.

**Myth (the mirror image, which the site must also avoid): "Aspartame has no effect on the gut microbiome."**
The 2022 trial says otherwise. The correct claim is that the detected effects were small, reverted after exposure ended, and produced no measurable glycaemic consequence.

**Myth: "The saccharin finding is settled science."**
A better-powered NIH-funded replication at the full saccharin ADI (Serrano 2021) found nothing — no glucose effect, no microbiome effect, no SCFA effect, in humans or mice.

## What I could NOT verify

- **The exact aspartame dose given to mice in 2014.** *Nature* full text is paywalled and both nature.com and ScienceDirect refused fetches. The ~1,333 mg/kg/day (~30× ADI) figure comes from a Calorie Control Council–funded review and is a derived estimate. It is directionally consistent with the paper's use of concentrated commercial table-top formulations, but the site should either attribute it explicitly or not use it.
- **Whether the aspartame arm's glucose-tolerance impairment in the 2014 mouse experiment was individually statistically significant**, or only significant as part of a pooled sweetener comparison. I could not read the figure legends.
- **The stevia dose in the 2022 *Cell* trial.** The saccharin (180 mg), sucralose (102 mg) and aspartame (240 mg + 5.76 g glucose) doses are documented; I found no source giving the stevia dose.
- **The magnitude of the glycaemic effect in the saccharin and sucralose arms.** One SMC expert described the changes as "discrete" and "small," but I could not obtain the incremental-AUC numbers, confidence intervals or p-values from the paper.
- **Whether the 2022 aspartame-arm microbiome changes survive correction for multiple comparisons at the individual-taxon level.** The paper's group-level claim is clear; the per-taxon statistics are not visible to me.
- **The SWEET study (source 15) in any detail** — sample size (~340 adults + 38 children), design (2-month low-calorie diet then 10 months of sweetener-containing vs sugar-containing products) and headline result (slightly better weight maintenance, increased SCFA- and methane-producing bacteria) are second-hand from a press summary. Crucially, it used **mixtures** of sweeteners and sweetness enhancers, so nothing in it can be attributed to aspartame. Funding (EU Horizon 2020 consortium; I believe it includes industry partners) is unverified. **Do not put this on the site without reading the paper.**
- **The Frankenfeld 2015 cross-sectional study (n = 31)** — I have it only via the Conz review's summary, which describes both "no difference in bacterial abundance" and a diversity change, a combination I could not check against the original.
- **Any regulator statement specifically addressing aspartame and the microbiome.** EFSA's 2013 re-evaluation predates both papers and I found no JECFA/EFSA/FDA document that assesses microbiome endpoints for aspartame. If the site wants to say "no regulator has treated this as an adverse effect," that is an argument from silence and should be phrased as such.
