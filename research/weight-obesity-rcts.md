# "Diet Soda Makes You Fat": Cohorts vs Randomized Trials, Reverse Causality, and the WHO 2023 Guideline

*Research brief. Last updated 2026-08-04. Scope: body weight, adiposity and obesity only. Cancer/JECFA/IARC is covered in `jecfa-iarc-2023.md`; the ADI and EFSA's toxicological review in `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md`; cognition in `elsa-brasil-cognition-2025.md`.*

## Bottom line

This is the cleanest example in the whole aspartame debate of observational studies and randomized trials pointing in opposite directions — and of the observational direction being the less trustworthy one. **Every meta-analysis of RCTs finds that substituting low/no-calorie sweeteners (LNCS) for sugar produces modest weight *loss*: roughly 0.5–1.4 kg, or about 0.3 BMI units. Every meta-analysis of prospective cohorts finds the opposite association.** The best-supported explanation for the discordance is reverse causality — people who are gaining weight, or trying to stop gaining, switch to diet drinks — supported by direct evidence that prior intent to lose weight predicts sweetener use.

But the honest version has three parts the advocacy version omits. First, **the effect is small**: about 1 kg. Diet soda is not a weight-loss intervention; it is a way of not drinking sugar. Second, **the two trials that most strongly favour diet beverages over plain water were both fully funded by the American Beverage Association**, and one of them (SWITCH) missed its own pre-specified clinical-significance threshold. The best-known independently-run trial (CHOICE) found **no significant difference** between diet beverages, water and control. Third, **three separate public bodies — WHO (2023), the UK's SACN (2025) and the US Dietary Guidelines (Jan 2026) — currently advise against or advise minimising non-sugar sweeteners**, and two of them did so *while agreeing* that the RCT evidence shows a weight benefit. A site cannot truthfully say "the authorities agree diet soda is fine."

## Key findings

**1. The flagship substitution meta-analysis: LNCS beverages beat sugar-sweetened beverages by ~1 kg.** McGlynn et al. pooled 17 RCTs (24 trial comparisons, 1,733 adults, mean age 33.1, 77.4% women, overweight/obese or at diabetes risk). Substituting LNCS beverages for sugar-sweetened beverages: **body weight −1.06 kg (95% CI −1.71 to −0.41)**, BMI −0.32 (−0.58 to −0.07), body fat −0.60% (−1.03 to −0.18), intrahepatocellular lipid SMD −0.42 (−0.70 to −0.14). Water vs SSBs: −0.01 kg (−0.95 to 0.98). LNCS beverages vs water: −1.07 kg (−1.95 to −0.19). GRADE: moderate certainty for the LNCSB-vs-SSB weight result; low for LNCSB-vs-water. ([McGlynn et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2022;5(3):e222092](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9907347/)) — **strong for the sugar-substitution result; weak for the "better than water" result.** *Disclosure: this is the Toronto 3D unit (Sievenpiper group); multiple authors report sweetener- and sugar-industry funding, including personal fees from the International Sweeteners Association — an industry body.*

**2. An independent meta-analysis of substitution trials with ≥6 months follow-up agrees, at a smaller magnitude.** Tobiassen & Køster-Rasmussen pooled 6 randomized substitution trials, 1,729 participants: replacing SSBs with non-caloric alternatives gave a long-term **BMI reduction of 0.31 kg/m² (95% CI 0.17–0.44)**, roughly 0.5 kg in children and ~1 kg in adults. They also report that in one trial BMI regressed toward baseline after the intervention stopped. ([Tobiassen & Køster-Rasmussen, *Obes Rev* 2024;25(2):e13652](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37880814/)) — **moderate**

**3. The energy-intake mechanism checks out and shows no compensatory overeating in trials.** Rogers et al.'s systematic review found short-term energy intake reduced by **−94 kcal (95% CI −122 to −66)** for LES vs sugar, and essentially identical intake for **LES vs water: −2 kcal (−30 to 26)** — i.e. no evidence that sweetness without calories drives compensatory eating in controlled conditions. Sustained-intervention trials: **−1.35 kg (−2.28 to −0.42)** for LES vs sugar in adults, and −1.24 kg (−2.22 to −0.26) vs water. ([Rogers et al., *Int J Obes* 2016;40(3):381–94](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786736/)) — **moderate.** *Disclosure: the expert group was funded by the ILSI Europe Eating Behaviour and Energy Balance Task Force — an industry-supported body; several authors were employees of sweetener/food manufacturers.*

**4. An overview of 20 systematic reviews quantifies the RCT/cohort split directly.** Across 75 RCTs, 42 prospective cohorts and 10 cross-sectional studies: RCT-based meta-analyses gave mean body-weight differences of **−1.41 kg to −0.45 kg**, and "results from MA of RCT consistently yielded the same direction of effect." Cohort-based reviews gave weight differences of **−0.12 kg to +0.02 kg** and obesity risk ratios of **1.21 to 1.76**. The authors name reverse causality — people adopting LCS "in response to weight gain" — as the primary driver of the discordance. ([Higgins et al., *Adv Nutr* 2024, overview of reviews](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11705604/)) — **strong.** *Disclosure: one author is at Exponent (a consultancy); another received an honorarium from IAFNS, which pools industry funding.*

**5. Direct evidence for reverse causality: dieting predicts sweetener use.** In 22,231 US adults across five NHANES cycles (1999–2008), people who reported trying to lose weight in the previous 12 months were **64% more likely to consume any LCS (prevalence ratio 1.64, 95% CI 1.54–1.75)**; for LCS beverages specifically PR 1.72 (1.57–1.88); for LCS foods 1.93 (1.60–2.33). The association persisted after adjustment for BMI, i.e. it tracked *dieting behaviour*, not just body size. ([Drewnowski & Rehm, *Nutr Diabetes* 2016;6:e202](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4817079/)) — **moderate** (cross-sectional, single 24-h recall). *Disclosure: "Analyses of federal data supported by funds from the Calorie Control Council" — an industry trade association. Drewnowski reports extensive food and beverage industry funding. This finding should never be the sole support for the reverse-causality claim; use it alongside finding 4 and finding 10.*

**6. Cohort analyses that model *substitution* rather than *level* of intake lose the harm signal.** Pooling 14 prospective cohorts (416,830 participants, ≥1 y follow-up), substituting LNCS beverages for SSBs was associated with weight change of about **−0.12 kg/year**, lower obesity incidence (RR 0.88), lower CHD (0.89), CVD mortality (0.95) and total mortality (0.96). No adverse associations appeared under change or substitution modelling. Certainty: **low to very low** (imprecision, indirectness, inconsistency). ([Lee et al., *Diabetes Care* 2022;45(8):1917–1930](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35901272/)) — **weak-to-moderate.** *Same Toronto 3D group; a published Comment by Buziau et al. (Diabetes Care 2023;46:e97–e98) disputes it — I could not read the substance of that comment.*

**7. WHO's May 2023 recommendation is real, is conditional, and rests on low-certainty evidence — including its own RCT results that went the other way.** Verbatim: *"WHO suggests that non-sugar sweeteners (NSS) not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases (conditional recommendation)."* Released **15 May 2023**. WHO's own reading of the evidence: in RCTs, NSS **reduced body weight and BMI** (low certainty); in prospective cohorts (average ~13 y follow-up), higher NSS intake was associated with **increased BMI, obesity, type 2 diabetes, CVD and mortality** (very low to low certainty). WHO downgraded to *conditional* because of "lack of certainty about the overall balance of desirable and undesirable effects associated with long-term NSS use." ([WHO guideline, recommendation chapter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK592246/); [WHO news release, 15 May 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2023-who-advises-not-to-use-non-sugar-sweeteners-for-weight-control-in-newly-released-guideline)) — **strong (primary source)**

**8. WHO's own stated reasons for discounting the trials are substantive, not hand-waving.** From the guideline: "Most RCTs comparing intake of NSS with intake of free sugars did not explicitly assess the replacement of free sugars with NSS"; short-term weight loss "without additional evidence of long-term impact, does not represent a health benefit"; and the "discordant results between RCTs and prospective cohort studies" limit real-world relevance. WHO also acknowledged that reverse causation may have contributed to the cohort associations. The underlying systematic review was Rios-Leyvraz & Montez (WHO, 2022), covering 283 unique studies. ([WHO/IRIS](https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/353064)) — **strong**

**9. The most cited criticism of the WHO guideline is real but comes from an industry-connected group.** Khan, Lee, Ayoub-Charette, Noronha, McGlynn, Chiavaroli & Sievenpiper argue that WHO "disregarded the trial evidence and solely relied on the prospective cohort studies, ignoring the established hierarchy of evidence as described by GRADE," and that WHO "did not consider prospective cohort studies using change and substitution analysis." ([*Eur J Clin Nutr* 2023, DOI 10.1038/s41430-023-01314-7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10630128/)) — **moderate as argument; label the source.** *Disclosures include grants/honoraria from IAFNS, the International Food Information Council, the Calorie Control Council, the American Beverage Association, Tate & Lyle and the Canadian Sugar Institute. Never cite this paper as the sole rebuttal to WHO.*

**10. The strongest *independent* criticism of the WHO guideline is the UK SACN position statement — and it is the single most quotable non-industry source in this area.** Published **April 2025**. SACN states explicitly: *"SACN gives greater weight to RCTs of adequate quality and duration and less weight to PCS evidence… because quality RCTs minimise the potential for selection bias and confounding and facilitate assignment of a causal interpretation. WHO gave greater weight to PCS evidence over RCT evidence in reaching conclusions for this guideline."* And: *"SACN conclude that evidence from shorter… and longer term… RCTs consistently suggest NSS, compared with free sugars, reduce energy intake and therefore body weight."* On the cohorts: *"Given concerns about potential for confounding and reverse causality in PCS, SACN would give less weight to this evidence."* ([SACN statement on the WHO guideline on non-sugar sweeteners, April 2025, PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67ea97b3ea9f8afd8105627d/sacn-position-statement-on-non-sugar-sweeteners.pdf)) — **strong (government advisory committee, no industry funding)**

**11. But SACN still recommends minimising sweeteners.** Same document, Recommendations: *"SACN recommends that intake of NSS be minimised."* For younger children: no drinks sweetened with sugar **or** NSS; unsweetened food. For older children and adults: "swapping sugars for NSS may help reduce sugar intake from foods and drinks (and so reduce energy intake), at least in the short term - the long-term goal is to limit both sugar and NSS intake." SACN calls its own recommendations "precautionary… because evidence on NSS and health outcomes is inconsistent." — **strong; this is a genuine caveat and must not be omitted**

**12. The largest broadly independent systematic review found mostly nothing.** Toews et al. (Cochrane Germany group) reviewed randomized, non-randomized and observational evidence: "most health outcomes did not seem to have differences between the NSS exposed and unexposed groups." For adults, a small BMI benefit (−0.6, 95% CI −1.19 to −0.01) from two small studies (n=174), very low to low certainty. Lower vs higher NSS dose: −0.09 kg (−0.13 to −0.05; n=17,934), very low certainty. In children, a smaller rise in BMI z-score for NSS vs sugar (−0.15, −0.17 to −0.12; n=528), **moderate** certainty. In overweight/obese adults actively losing weight: "no evidence of any effect." ([Toews et al., *BMJ* 2019;364:k4718](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30602577/)) — **strong as a source; its content is a caution against overclaiming**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The effect size is about one kilogram.** Across nine RCT meta-analyses the range is −1.41 to −0.45 kg. That is a real but trivial amount for an individual. "Diet soda helps you lose weight" is an overclaim; "diet soda is a way of not drinking sugar, and doesn't cause weight gain" is defensible.
- **The two trials showing diet beverages *beat plain water* were both fully funded by the American Beverage Association**, and CHOICE — the trial without a verified industry sponsor — found no significant difference between any arm. Any site quoting Peters or SWITCH must disclose the funder in the same sentence.
- **The Peters design was tilted.** Participants were *habitual NNS consumers*; the water arm required them to abandon an existing habit, while the NNS arm did not. That is a plausible mechanism for a 3.8 kg difference that has nothing to do with the sweetener's physiology.
- **SWITCH's authors themselves said the difference was not clinically significant.** Quoting the 1.4 kg without that sentence is misleading by omission.
- **Cohort findings are not all explainable by reverse causality.** Fowler et al. (San Antonio Heart Study; 3,682 adults re-examined after 7–8 years) adjusted for **baseline BMI** and still found a dose-response: >21 artificially sweetened beverages/week vs none gave OR 1.93 (p=0.007) for becoming overweight/obese among 1,250 baseline normal-weight participants, and OR 2.03 (p=0.0005) for obesity among 2,571 with baseline BMI <30. Adjusting for baseline BMI does not eliminate reverse causality (people can be on a weight *trajectory* at any BMI), but it does defeat the crudest version of the argument. ([Fowler et al., *Obesity* 2008;16(8):1894–1900](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18535548/))
- **There is real observational evidence of food compensation.** In NHANES 1999–2010, overweight and obese diet-beverage drinkers consumed significantly **more calories from solid food** at meals and snacks than same-weight-status SSB drinkers. Overall daily intake: 2,351 kcal (SSB consumers) vs 2,203 kcal (diet-beverage consumers). ([Bleich et al., *Am J Public Health* 2014;104(3):e72–8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3953764/)) This is cross-sectional and self-reported, and controlled trials find no compensation (finding 3) — but it is the strongest empirical form of the "diet soda makes you eat more" claim and should be conceded, not dismissed.
- **The benefit appears contingent on continued substitution.** Tobiassen & Køster-Rasmussen report BMI regressing toward baseline after intervention ceased.
- **WHO's structural objection is fair**: most RCTs did not explicitly test *replacement* of free sugars with NSS, and short-term weight loss is not by itself a demonstrated long-term health benefit.
- **Trials are short, small, and heterogeneous.** Toews et al. found "no evidence of any effect" in overweight/obese adults actively losing weight, at very low to moderate certainty. The whole RCT literature is ~75 trials, most under 3 months.
- **Three public bodies currently advise against or advise minimising.** WHO (May 2023, conditional); SACN (April 2025, "intake of NSS be minimised", precautionary); and the **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans**, released **7 January 2026**, which state that "no amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended or considered part of a healthy or nutritious diet." ([ADA News, 7 Jan 2026](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2026/january/hhs-usda-release-2025-2030-dietary-guidelines/)) The DGA position appears to be driven by a food-processing framework rather than a weight-specific evidence review — but it exists, it is official US policy, and a debunking site cannot pretend otherwise.
- **Weight is not the whole question.** Cohort associations with type 2 diabetes, CVD and mortality remain unresolved and are outside this brief.
- **Almost every meta-analysis favourable to sweeteners in this area has industry fingerprints.** McGlynn/Lee/Khan (Toronto 3D), Rogers (ILSI Europe), Higgins (IAFNS honoraria), Drewnowski (Calorie Control Council). The independent sources — SACN, Toews/Cochrane Germany, Tobiassen — are more cautious. That pattern is itself evidence and should be stated plainly.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "Diet soda makes you fat." | Not supported by any RCT evidence. Randomized substitution of LNCS for sugar produces ~1 kg *loss* (−1.06 kg, 95% CI −1.71 to −0.41). The cohort association exists but is the study design least able to separate cause from effect. |
| "Artificial sweeteners trick your body into eating more later." | Controlled intake studies find no compensation: LES vs water, **−2 kcal (95% CI −30 to 26)**. Cross-sectional NHANES data do show heavier diet-drink consumers eating more solid food — real, but self-reported and confounded. |
| "WHO said sweeteners cause weight gain." | WHO's own RCT meta-analysis found NSS **reduced** body weight and BMI. WHO's recommendation is *conditional*, rests on **low-certainty** evidence, and says NSS are not a *means of achieving weight control* — not that they cause weight gain. |
| "WHO banned / warned against aspartame for obesity." | No ban, no warning. A conditional guideline suggestion about the whole NSS category, with WHO explicitly noting the recommendation is not a comment on safety. |
| "The WHO guideline was debunked." | It was contested. The best-known critique (Khan/Sievenpiper, *EJCN* 2023) comes from a group with dense industry ties. The most credible independent critique (SACN, April 2025) agrees WHO underweighted the RCTs — and still recommends minimising sweetener intake. |
| "Diet drinks are better than water for weight loss." | The pooled estimate (−1.07 kg, 95% CI −1.95 to −0.19) is **low certainty**, and the two trials driving it were fully ABA-funded. CHOICE found no significant difference. Treat diet drinks and water as equivalent. |
| "The RCTs prove sweeteners are good for you." | They show a small weight benefit versus sugar over short periods. They do not establish long-term health benefit, and WHO's objection on that point is legitimate. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- **−1.06 kg (95% CI −1.71 to −0.41)** — pooled body-weight effect, LNCS beverages substituted for sugar-sweetened beverages; 17 RCTs, 24 comparisons, 1,733 adults; GRADE moderate (McGlynn, *JAMA Netw Open* 2022).
- **BMI −0.32 (−0.58 to −0.07)**; body fat **−0.60% (−1.03 to −0.18)**; intrahepatocellular lipid SMD **−0.42 (−0.70 to −0.14)** — same analysis.
- **BMI −0.31 kg/m² (95% CI 0.17–0.44)** — 6 substitution RCTs ≥6 months, 1,729 participants (Tobiassen 2024).
- **−94 kcal (−122 to −66)** short-term energy intake, LES vs sugar; **−2 kcal (−30 to 26)** LES vs water (Rogers 2016).
- **−1.41 kg to −0.45 kg** — range of pooled body-weight effects across nine RCT meta-analyses; versus **−0.12 kg to +0.02 kg** and obesity RR **1.21–1.76** across cohort meta-analyses (Higgins 2024 overview of 20 reviews; 75 RCTs, 42 cohorts).
- **PR 1.64 (95% CI 1.54–1.75)** — people who tried to lose weight in the past year were 64% more likely to use any LCS; 22,231 US adults, NHANES 1999–2008 (Drewnowski & Rehm 2016).
- **15 May 2023** — WHO guideline release date. Recommendation is **conditional**, evidence certainty **low**.
- **April 2025** — SACN position statement. **7 January 2026** — 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans released.
- **CHOICE, 6 months, n=318**: −2.5% (diet beverage), −2.03% (water), −1.76% (control); no significant between-group difference; OR 2.07 (1.02–4.22) for reaching 5% loss with beverage replacement.
- **Peters, 52 weeks, n=303**: −6.21 ± 7.65 kg (NNS) vs −2.45 ± 5.59 kg (water), p<0.001. **Fully funded by the American Beverage Association.**
- **SWITCH, 52 weeks, n=493**: −7.5 kg (NNS) vs −6.1 kg (water); difference 1.4 kg (90% CI −2.6 to −0.2); below the authors' 1.5 kg clinical-significance threshold. **Funded by the American Beverage Association.**
- **Fowler 2008, San Antonio Heart Study, n=3,682 followed 7–8 years**: OR 1.93 (p=0.007) for overweight/obesity at >21 ASB/week vs none, adjusted for baseline BMI.
- **2,351 vs 2,203 kcal/day** — total intake of SSB vs diet-beverage consumers, NHANES 1999–2010 (Bleich 2014).

- **A "2022 Harrold/Halford" study does not appear to exist.** The SWITCH trial's protocol is 2017 (*Contemp Clin Trials*); its results papers are both 2023. If the brief's "2022" referred to something else, I did not find it.
- **CHOICE trial funding source.** The AJCN full text returned HTTP 403. CHOICE is widely described as an academic (UNC Chapel Hill) trial and I found no industry-funding claim, but I could not read the funding statement.
- **Tobiassen & Køster-Rasmussen funding/COI** — not present in the abstract I could access.
- **The 12-week SWITCH numbers** (Obesity 2023, DOI 10.1002/oby.23796) — the Wiley full text returned 403. Only the 52-week figures below are verified.
- **The substance of Buziau et al.'s published Comment on Lee et al. 2022** (*Diabetes Care* 2023;46:e97–e98) — PubMed carries no abstract and the full text is behind Silverchair.
- **The exact pooled effect sizes in the WHO-commissioned Rios-Leyvraz & Montez (2022) review** — I confirmed the review's existence, scope (283 unique studies) and direction of findings via WHO IRIS and the SACN statement, but did not retrieve its own numeric estimates.
- **The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans wording direct from dietaryguidelines.gov.** The quoted sentence ("no amount of added sugars or non-nutritive sweeteners is recommended or considered part of a healthy or nutritious diet") was verified only through secondary reporting (ADA News, 7 Jan 2026, corroborated by FoodNavigator-USA, 17 Feb 2026). **Verify against the primary DGA document before publishing this quote.**
- **Whether any Mendelian randomization study addresses artificially sweetened beverages and adiposity** — I did not search for this and it is a live gap; MR would be the strongest available test of reverse causality.
- I found no evidence that EFSA or FDA has issued a weight-specific position on LNCS; both bodies address safety, not efficacy. This is an absence, not a verified negative.
