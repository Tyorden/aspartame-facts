# Aspartame vs the Other Sweeteners, and vs Sugar: Evidence Depth and Regulatory Standing

*Research brief. Last updated 2026-08-05. Scope: how aspartame compares with the other sweeteners it is routinely lumped in with, and with sugar. Weight/RCT evidence is in `weight-obesity-rcts.md`; cancer cohorts in `cancer-cohort-studies.md`; the 2025 Neurology sweetener–cognition study in `elsa-brasil-cognition-2025.md`; EFSA's aspartame opinion in `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md`. This brief deliberately does not re-argue aspartame's own safety case.*

## Bottom line

"Artificial sweeteners" is not a chemical category. It is a shopping category. The compounds pooled under that label are a dipeptide methyl ester (aspartame), a chlorinated disaccharide (sucralose), a benzothiazole sulfonimide (saccharin), an oxathiazinone dioxide (acesulfame-K), plant diterpene glycosides (stevia), fruit triterpene glycosides (monk fruit), and a family of sugar alcohols (erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol) that are not high-intensity sweeteners at all and behave like bulk carbohydrates. They differ in absorption, in metabolism, in dose by three orders of magnitude, and in the shape of their safety literature. Findings genuinely do not transfer between them — and the transfer runs in *both* directions, which is the honest version of the point. The erythritol thrombosis signal is not evidence against aspartame; equally, aspartame's very large human literature is not evidence for monk fruit.

On regulatory standing, aspartame sits in the strongest tier: a full food-additive approval in both the US and EU, an ADI a habitual consumer would find hard to reach (FDA's own arithmetic: **75 tabletop packets a day** for a 60 kg adult, more than for any other approved sweetener), and the largest human dataset of any of them. The genuinely open questions in this field are mostly attached to *other* compounds: erythritol and xylitol cardiovascular signals, polyol GI tolerance, sucralose under prolonged baking, monk fruit's thin toxicology, allulose's failed EU novel-food dossier. And there is one caveat that cuts against aspartame specifically: **EFSA has now re-evaluated erythritol (2023), saccharin (2024), steviol glycosides (2024), acesulfame-K (2025), neotame (2025) and sucralose (2026) — aspartame's 2013 opinion is now the oldest of the lot.**

## Key findings

**1. FDA's own comparison table makes aspartame the most permissive ADI in practical terms.** Number of tabletop packets a 60 kg (132 lb) person would need per day to reach the ADI: **aspartame 75; saccharin 45; rebaudioside A 27; acesulfame-K 23; sucralose 23; neotame 23; advantame 4,920.** ADIs in mg/kg bw/day: aspartame 50, acesulfame-K 15, saccharin 15, sucralose 5, Reb A 12 (from JECFA's 4 mg/kg steviol-equivalents), neotame 0.3, advantame 32.8. FDA assumes a packet is as sweet as ~8 g of sugar. ([FDA, *Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food*, content current 27 Feb 2025](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)) — **strong (regulator primary)**

**2. FDA's own study counts do not support "aspartame is the most studied" as a *count*.** FDA states it reviewed "more than 100 studies" for aspartame, "more than 110" for sucralose, "more than 110" for neotame, "more than 90" for acesulfame-K, "37 animal and human studies" for advantame, and "more than 30 human studies" for saccharin after the rat bladder finding. FDA's actual wording is "**one** of the most studied food additives in the human food supply." The site already treats "most studied" cautiously; this is the primary-source basis for doing so. Aspartame's real distinction is the size and length of its *human epidemiological* record, not FDA's premarket dossier count. ([FDA, same page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)) — **strong**

**3. Regulatory standing is genuinely tiered, and "natural" sits in the weaker tier.** In the US, six sweeteners have full food-additive listings (saccharin, aspartame, Ace-K, sucralose, neotame, advantame). Steviol glycosides, monk fruit extract and thaumatin are not approved additives — FDA has merely "not questioned" GRAS notices submitted by their sponsors. FDA has set **no ADI at all for monk fruit or thaumatin**. Whole-leaf and crude stevia extracts remain **subject to Import Alert 45-06 and are not permitted as sweeteners**; only highly purified (≥95%) steviol glycosides are. Cyclamate is prohibited outright in the US while permitted in the EU. ([FDA, same page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [FDA, *High-Intensity Sweeteners*, current 19 Dec 2017](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/high-intensity-sweeteners)) — **strong**

**4. EFSA has now re-evaluated almost every sweetener except aspartame, and mostly *raised* the ADIs.** Erythritol (E 968), adopted 20 Dec 2023: first-ever numerical ADI, **0.5 g/kg bw/day**, derived from diarrhoea in human studies. Saccharin (E 954), 15 Nov 2024: ADI raised from the SCF's 5 mg/kg to **9 mg/kg**, explicitly because the rat bladder tumours are now agreed to be male-rat-specific and not human-relevant. Steviol glycosides, 4 Nov 2024: ADI **held at 4 mg/kg steviol equivalents**, EFSA rejecting the applicant's request to raise it. Acesulfame-K (E 950), 30 Apr 2025: ADI raised from 9 to **15 mg/kg**, based on a rat NOAEL of 1,500 mg/kg bw/day. Neotame (E 961), 2025. Sucralose (E 955), 17 Feb 2026: ADI **confirmed at 15 mg/kg** on a BMDL of 55 mg/kg (reduced body weight in rats). ([EFSA Journal opinions, see Sources 3–8](https://www.efsa.europa.eu)) — **strong**

**5. The sucralose heat question is real, is regulator-endorsed, and is more nuanced than the headlines.** EFSA's February 2026 opinion confirmed sucralose is safe at current authorised uses and found "no safety concerns arose for genotoxicity of sucralose (E 955) and its impurities and degradation products." Its baking trials found that "the inclusion of sucralose as a sweetening agent did not give rise to any significant increase in risk from 3-MCPD and 3-MCPD esters." **But** EFSA still refused the requested extension into fine bakery wares (food category 7.2): given "uncertainties regarding the potential formation of chlorinated compounds under the wide range of baking processes… the Panel could not conclude on the safety of the proposed extension of use." Note the transatlantic tension: FDA's page flatly describes sucralose as "heat stable… suitable as a sugar substitute in baked goods." ([EFSA Journal 2026;24(2):e9854, 17 Feb 2026](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2026.9854); [PMC mirror](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12911467/)) — **strong**

**6. The erythritol cardiovascular signal is the strongest genuinely open question in this whole field — and it is about a compound aspartame has nothing in common with.** Witkowski et al. found, in a 1,157-patient discovery cohort undergoing cardiac risk assessment, that circulating polyols predicted 3-year MACE; in independent US and European validation cohorts the fourth-versus-first-quartile adjusted HRs were **1.80 (95% CI 1.18–2.77)** and **2.21 (1.20–4.07)**. A 30 g erythritol drink in 8 volunteers raised plasma erythritol roughly **1,000-fold** (basal 3.84 µM to 5.85 mM at 30 minutes) and stayed elevated for days; erythritol enhanced platelet reactivity in vitro and thrombosis in a mouse arterial injury model. ([Witkowski et al., *Nat Med* 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36849732/)) — **moderate; mechanistically suggestive, causally unproven**

**7. The counter-evidence on erythritol is substantial and should be given equal weight.** Erythritol is produced endogenously from glucose via the pentose-phosphate pathway, so plasma erythritol is partly a readout of glucose handling rather than of diet — the Witkowski cohorts had no dietary intake data at all, which critics call a severe limitation. The ARIC replication (n = 4,006, visit 5, 2011–2013) found people in the top tertiles of erythritol or its metabolite erythronate were older and more likely to have diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and reduced eGFR, and concluded these are "**markers of cardiometabolic health**" — with erythronate, not erythritol, associated with all cardiovascular outcomes assessed. EFSA's 2023 opinion reviewed the CVD literature and concluded "the available evidence does not demonstrate a causal relationship." FDA reviewed the paper in 2023 and "determined that the observational studies cited in the paper did not establish a causal link." ([Frontiers in Nutrition 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1195521/full); [ARIC, *JACC Advances* 2025;4(3):101605](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889355/); [EFSA erythritol opinion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10731997/); [FDA sweeteners page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)) — **strong as a set of caveats**

**8. The same group reported the same pattern for xylitol.** Third-versus-first tertile adjusted HR for incident MACE **1.57 (95% CI 1.12–2.21), P < 0.01**, plus in vitro platelet activation and enhanced thrombosis in a murine model; a xylitol-sweetened drink raised plasma levels and platelet responsiveness in all subjects. The authors' own conclusion is that "further studies examining the cardiovascular safety of xylitol are warranted" — i.e. hypothesis-generating. ([Witkowski et al., *Eur Heart J* 2024;45(27):2439](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38842092/)) — **moderate**

**9. Polyol GI effects are not a myth; they are the reason polyols carry a mandatory EU warning.** EFSA's erythritol reference point for diarrhoea is a human single-dose NOAEL of **0.5 g/kg bw**. Sorbitol is tolerated by most people at about 10 g/day with mild symptoms, while 20 g/day produces abdominal pain and diarrhoea; ~20 g/day of total polyols is the commonly cited threshold. Under EU rules, food or drink containing more than **10% added polyols** must be labelled "excessive consumption may produce laxative effects" — EFSA in 2023 explicitly refused to exempt erythritol from that warning. Erythritol is the best-tolerated polyol because most of it is absorbed in the small intestine and excreted unchanged in urine, so little reaches the colon. ([EFSA erythritol opinion, adopted 20 Dec 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10731997/); [*Adv Nutr* systematic review of polyols and GI health, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5508768/)) — **strong**

**10. "Natural" sweeteners have the *thinnest* regulatory files, not the thickest.** Monk fruit: no FDA ADI; not authorised as an EU food additive at all — only a single specific aqueous extract has been permitted, under Regulation (EU) 2024/2345 from October 2024, and no EFSA additive evaluation exists because no industry application was filed. At its 102nd meeting (Nanjing, 9–18 June 2026) JECFA gave monk fruit extract only a **temporary ADI of 0–10 mg/kg bw/day** and identified safety concerns at proposed use levels, with estimated dietary exposure for children of **~38 mg/kg bw/day, about four times the temporary ADI**. Steviol glycosides fare better but are not clean either: EFSA's November 2024 opinion found 95th-percentile toddler exposure of **6.9 mg/kg bw/day against a 4 mg/kg ADI** under the proposed use extensions. ([FDA sweeteners page](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food); [*Foods* 2025 review of monk fruit EU status](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/14/16/2810); [Food Safety Magazine on JECFA 102 — **trade press**](https://www.food-safety.com/articles/11609-jecfa-evaluates-safety-of-certain-food-additives-monk-fruit-presents-potential-concern); [EFSA steviol opinion, 4 Nov 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11533382/)) — **moderate-to-strong** (the JECFA figures are from trade press; see "could not verify")

**11. Allulose is the clearest case of a US/EU regulatory split.** FDA has no questions on GRAS notices for D-allulose, applies a caloric factor of 0.4 kcal/g, and since 17 April 2019 (finalised as guidance 19 Oct 2020) exercises enforcement discretion to exclude it from "Total Sugars" and "Added Sugars" on the label. EFSA, asked to assess it as a **novel food**, identified data gaps on identity, production process, uses, genotoxicity and human data; the applicant did not respond to repeated requests; the Panel concluded in 2025 that "**the safety of D-allulose cannot be established**." It is not authorised in the EU. That is a procedural failure by an applicant rather than a positive finding of harm — but it means allulose has nothing like aspartame's evidentiary standing. ([FDA constituent update on allulose](https://www.fda.gov/food/cfsan-constituent-updates/fda-seeks-input-nutrition-labeling-certain-sugars-and-issues-final-guidance-allulose); [EFSA Journal 2025;e9468](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2025.9468)) — **strong**

**12. Saccharin is the field's cautionary tale about rodent-to-human extrapolation — and it was resolved in saccharin's favour.** Sodium saccharin causes rat urothelial bladder tumours only at dietary concentrations of ~3% or more, via a mechanism dependent on rat-specific urine composition (pH, osmolality, protein precipitate causing urothelial damage and regenerative hyperplasia). NTP delisted saccharin from the Report on Carcinogens in 2000 on the grounds that the rodent data no longer met the criteria for "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen." California delisted it under Prop 65 effective 6 April 2001. EFSA's 2024 re-evaluation raised the ADI on the same reasoning. This is the closest available precedent for how the Ramazzini rat findings on aspartame are being handled. ([NTP, *Substances Delisted from the Report on Carcinogens*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK590763/); [OEHHA delisting notice](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/crnr/saccharin-delisted-effective-april-6-2001-known-state-cause-cancer); [EFSA saccharin opinion, 15 Nov 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39553702/)) — **strong**

**13. The actual comparator — sugar — has a harm literature that dwarfs all of these.** Dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohorts: each additional daily sugar-sweetened beverage serving is associated with a **27% higher risk of type 2 diabetes (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.15–1.41)** and **9% higher CVD risk (RR 1.09, 95% CI 1.07–1.12)**, both with linear dose-response. WHO recommends free sugars below 10% of total energy. Any comparison that evaluates a sweetener against zero rather than against the sugar it replaces is asking the wrong question. ([*Nutrients* 2021 dose-response meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8402166/); [WHO sugars guideline](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK285522/)) — **moderate-to-strong** (observational, but consistent and dose-responsive)

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **Aspartame's EU assessment is now the oldest in the class.** EFSA's aspartame opinion dates from 2013. Since then it has completed erythritol (2023), saccharin (2024), steviol glycosides (2024), acesulfame-K (2025), neotame (2025) and sucralose (2026). Aspartame has not been re-run, and no timetable is published. That is a real asymmetry and it cuts against aspartame, not for it.
- **The category-error argument cuts both ways.** If erythritol's thrombosis signal cannot be transferred to aspartame, then aspartame's large human literature cannot be used to reassure people about monk fruit, allulose or thaumatin. A site making the first argument must accept the second.
- **Sucralose has a live, regulator-recognised open question.** EFSA declined to extend sucralose into fine bakery wares in February 2026 over chlorinated-compound formation during baking, while FDA describes it as heat stable. Both statements are defensible on their own terms, but the honest framing is "regulators do not fully agree about sucralose in baked goods."
- **Sucralose-6-acetate.** Schiffman et al. (2023) reported that sucralose-6-acetate — a manufacturing impurity and possible gut metabolite — was clastogenic in a MultiFlow assay and micronucleus test, with estimated exposure from a single sweetened drink potentially exceeding the 0.15 µg/person/day threshold of toxicological concern for genotoxicity. These are in vitro screening assays on an impurity, not on sucralose, and EFSA's 2026 review found no genotoxicity concern for sucralose "and its impurities and degradation products." But the paper exists, was peer-reviewed, and the loudest published rebuttals came from the **Calorie Control Council, an industry trade association** — which is a weak place for a debunking site to rest.
- **Erythritol and xylitol are not exonerated.** FDA and EFSA both say causality is not established; neither says the question is closed. FDA's stated position is that it "will continue to monitor." The Cleveland Clinic group's mechanistic work (platelet activation, mouse thrombosis, human ingestion challenges) is not merely correlational, and it has not been refuted.
- **Erythritol exposure can exceed its own ADI.** EFSA found that both acute and chronic exposure estimates exceeded the new 0.5 g/kg ADI for high consumers, who "may be at risk of experiencing adverse effects." No comparable finding exists for aspartame.
- **Toddlers can exceed the steviol glycoside ADI.** 6.9 mg/kg at the 95th percentile against a 4 mg/kg ADI, under the proposed extended uses.
- **Monk fruit's children's exposure estimate is roughly 4× its temporary JECFA ADI.** If a site uses ADI exceedance as a safety metric, that metric currently indicts the "natural" options, not aspartame — but it must then be applied consistently.
- **Aspartame is the only one with a mandatory population-specific warning** (phenylalanine, for PKU). Sucralose, stevia and monk fruit have no equivalent. That is a genuine, if narrow, point in their favour.
- **Sugar's harm evidence is itself observational.** The SSB–diabetes and SSB–CVD associations come from prospective cohorts and carry the usual confounding problems. They are more consistent and more dose-responsive than the sweetener associations, but they are not RCT evidence.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "Artificial sweeteners cause X" (from a study of one compound) | The seven-sweetener cognition study, for example, assessed aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol and tagatose — four of which are sugar alcohols consumed in gram quantities, not high-intensity sweeteners consumed in milligrams. Pooling them assumes a shared mechanism that does not exist. |
| "Natural sweeteners like stevia and monk fruit are safer because they're natural" | They have the *thinnest* regulatory files. Monk fruit has no FDA ADI and only a temporary JECFA ADI with an identified exposure concern in children; it is barely authorised in the EU. Whole-leaf and crude stevia extracts are under US import alert. |
| "Erythritol causes heart attacks" | The finding is that *plasma* erythritol predicts events. Erythritol is made endogenously from glucose, the original cohorts had no dietary data, and the ARIC replication reads it as a marker of cardiometabolic health. Mechanistic platelet work is real and unresolved. |
| "Saccharin causes bladder cancer" | Only in rats, only at ~3%+ of the diet, by a mechanism that depends on rat urine chemistry. Delisted by NTP in 2000; EFSA raised the ADI in 2024 on the same basis. |
| "Sucralose becomes toxic when heated" | EFSA's own baking trials found no significant increase in 3-MCPD risk from adding sucralose. What EFSA actually did was decline to *extend* sucralose into fine bakery wares because it could not cover the full range of baking processes. That is a refusal to extrapolate, not a finding of harm. |
| "Aspartame is the most studied food additive" | FDA says "one of the most studied," and its own premarket review counts are higher for sucralose and neotame. The defensible version is that aspartame has the largest *human epidemiological* record. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- FDA packets-to-ADI for a 60 kg adult: **aspartame 75, saccharin 45, Reb A 27, Ace-K 23, sucralose 23, neotame 23, advantame 4,920** (packet = sweetness of ~8 g sugar).
- ADIs (mg/kg bw/day), FDA: aspartame 50, Ace-K 15, saccharin 15, Reb A 12, sucralose 5, advantame 32.8, neotame 0.3. EFSA: aspartame 40, Ace-K 15 (raised from 9, 30 Apr 2025), sucralose 15 (confirmed 17 Feb 2026), saccharin 9 (raised from 5, 15 Nov 2024), steviol 4 as steviol equivalents (held, 4 Nov 2024), erythritol 500 (new, 20 Dec 2023).
- Sweetness vs sucrose (FDA): advantame 20,000×; neotame 7,000–13,000×; thaumatin 2,000–3,000×; sucralose 600×; saccharin 200–700×; steviol glycosides 200–400×; aspartame 200×; Ace-K 200×; monk fruit 100–250×; sugar alcohols 25–100%.
- Erythritol: Witkowski Q4-vs-Q1 aHR **1.80 (1.18–2.77)** US and **2.21 (1.20–4.07)** EU validation cohorts; 30 g challenge in 8 volunteers raised plasma ~**1,000-fold** (3.84 µM → 5.85 mM at 30 min). ARIC replication n = **4,006**.
- Xylitol: T3-vs-T1 aHR **1.57 (1.12–2.21)**, P < 0.01.
- Polyols: EFSA erythritol diarrhoea NOAEL **0.5 g/kg bw** single dose; sorbitol ~**10 g/day** mild, **20 g/day** distressing; EU mandatory laxative warning above **10%** polyol content.
- Monk fruit (JECFA 102nd meeting, Nanjing, 9–18 June 2026): temporary ADI **0–10 mg/kg bw/day**; estimated child exposure **~38 mg/kg bw/day** (~4× the temporary ADI).
- Steviol glycosides: EFSA 95th-percentile toddler exposure **6.9 mg/kg bw/day** vs **4 mg/kg** ADI.
- Sucralose: EFSA BMDL **55 mg/kg bw/day** (reduced body weight in rats); sucralose-6-acetate TTC for genotoxicity **0.15 µg/person/day**.
- Allulose: FDA caloric factor **0.4 kcal/g**; excluded from Total and Added Sugars since **17 April 2019** (guidance finalised **19 October 2020**); EFSA 2025: "the safety of D-allulose cannot be established."
- Sugar: per additional SSB serving/day, type 2 diabetes **RR 1.27 (1.15–1.41)**, CVD **RR 1.09 (1.07–1.12)**; WHO free-sugars ceiling **<10% of energy**.
- Saccharin: NTP delisting **2000**; California Prop 65 delisting effective **6 April 2001**; rat tumours require ~**3%** of the diet.

## Corrections to the existing site

Nothing already published was found to be **wrong**. Three items need updating or sharpening:

1. **`the-evidence.html` (~line 288) and `caveats.html` (~line 279) understate the asymmetry.** Both say EFSA "has re-evaluated acesulfame K and neotame more recently." The complete list is now erythritol (Dec 2023), saccharin (Nov 2024), steviol glycosides (Nov 2024), acesulfame-K (Apr 2025), neotame (2025) and **sucralose (17 Feb 2026)**. Stating the full list makes the caveat stronger and more credible: aspartame's 2013 opinion is the oldest of the major EU-authorised sweeteners. The sucralose opinion post-dates the current site text and should be added.
2. **`the-evidence.html` / `caveats.html` say EFSA "is re-evaluating the aspartame–acesulfame salt (E 962)."** I did not re-verify the current status of that file in this brief and it should be re-checked before the next publish, since the standalone acesulfame-K re-evaluation completed in April 2025.
3. **`index.html` (~line 163) is accurate but could be materially strengthened.** It currently says the 2025 study "combined aspartame with sugar alcohols like erythritol and xylitol — different molecules, different metabolism." True. The stronger, checkable version is the dose gap: high-intensity sweeteners are consumed in **milligrams**, polyols in **grams** — a roughly thousand-fold difference in exposure — and polyols have their own EU laxative-warning threshold precisely because they are bulk ingredients.
