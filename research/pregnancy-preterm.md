# Pregnancy, preterm birth and children

*Scope note: this brief extends `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md` (which covers the phenylalanine mode-of-action pivot and the 360 µM fetal-protection modelling) and `pku.md` (maternal PKU, heterozygotes). Those numbers are cross-referenced, not repeated. This brief covers the **epidemiology**: the Danish and Norwegian preterm cohorts, offspring BMI, allergy, neurodevelopment, and what regulators actually say about pregnancy.*

## Bottom line

This is the area where the anti-aspartame case is at its **strongest** — and also where it is most often overstated. There is a real, replicated-in-places, statistically significant association between **artificially sweetened beverages** in pregnancy and preterm delivery, offspring overweight, and childhood asthma. But almost none of that evidence is about **aspartame**. Every major cohort measured "diet drinks" from a food-frequency questionnaire, in populations drinking blends of aspartame, acesulfame-K, cyclamate and saccharin, without measuring sweeteners in coffee, tea or foods. The one direct human pharmacokinetic study of placental transfer found aspartame is the *only* common sweetener that cannot be measured in cord blood — because it is fully hydrolysed in the gut lumen and never enters maternal circulation intact. The associations are low-to-very-low certainty by GRADE, are not consistent between the two flagship cohorts in *direction of subtype*, and have an obvious confounding structure. The honest position: **a genuine and unresolved signal for diet drinks as a category; no evidence that aspartame specifically is the agent; and regulators are unanimous that aspartame at or below the ADI is safe in pregnancy except in PKU.**

## Key findings

**1. The Danish flagship: a 38–78% elevation in preterm delivery, but not for aspartame specifically.** Halldorsson et al. analysed 59,334 women in the Danish National Birth Cohort (recruited Jan 1996 – Oct 2002; FFQ at ~week 25). Versus no intake, ≥1 serving/day of artificially sweetened **carbonated** soft drinks gave adjusted **OR 1.38 (95% CI 1.15–1.65)**; ≥4 servings/day gave **OR 1.78 (95% CI 1.19–2.66)**. Non-carbonated diet drinks at ≥4/day: **OR 1.29 (1.05–1.59)**. **No association for sugar-sweetened drinks** (carbonated P=0.29, non-carbonated P=0.93). Adjusted for maternal age, height, pre-pregnancy BMI, cohabitant status, parity, smoking, socioeconomic status. Authors' conclusion: *"Daily intake of artificially sweetened soft drinks may increase the risk of preterm delivery. Further studies are needed to reject or confirm these findings."* ([Halldorsson et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2010;92(3):626–33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20592133/)) — **moderate as an association; weak as evidence about aspartame**

**2. EFSA read the Danish paper closely and did not dismiss it.** EFSA's own assessment, verbatim: *"The Panel noted that the prospective design and large size of the study sample were major strengths, and there were no major flaws in the methods used."* It then flagged residual confounding (including by year of delivery, which the authors did not adjust for), no identifiable mode of action, no adjustment for other dietary methanol sources (fruit and vegetables), and — critically — *"the exposure assessment was for sweeteners in general and not specific for aspartame."* ([EFSA draft opinion §3.2.7.1.1](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf)) — **strong (primary source)**

**3. The subtype finding, which is the crux.** EFSA: *"A notable finding was that the trend in risk for artificially sweetened carbonated drinks was strongest for medically induced pre-term deliveries, and was not clearly apparent for spontaneous pre-term deliveries. No analysis was made by reason for pre-term delivery."* Medically induced preterm delivery is a **clinical decision**, which makes it far more susceptible to confounding by maternal comorbidity (obesity, hypertension, gestational diabetes) — exactly the conditions that predict diet-drink use. France's ANSES made the same point in 2011. ([EFSA draft opinion §3.2.7.1.1](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf); [ANSES, 1 Feb 2011](https://www.anses.fr/en/content/aspartame-update-anses-investigation)) — **strong**

**4. The Norwegian replication attempt largely failed, and pointed the other way.** Englund-Ögge et al. analysed 60,761 women in the Norwegian Mother and Child Cohort (MoBa; recruited 1999–2008, 38.5% participation; FFQ at ~week 22, 250 mL servings). Artificially sweetened beverages >1/day: **OR 1.11 (95% CI 1.00–1.24)**, P-trend 0.053 — borderline. Sugar-sweetened beverages >1/day: **OR 1.25 (1.08–1.45)**, P-trend 0.008, and ≥4/day **OR 1.41 (1.11–1.79)**. The AS signal was for **spontaneous** preterm (OR 1.15, 1.01–1.32) and **late** preterm (OR 1.14, 1.00–1.29); the SS signal was for **early** preterm <32 weeks (OR 1.75, 1.13–2.73). EFSA: the AS association *"was much weaker and barely discernible, applied more to spontaneous than medically induced deliveries, and was exceeded by an association with consumption of sugar-sweetened soft drinks."* ([Englund-Ögge et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2012;96(3):552–9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3417215/)) — **moderate**

**5. A third cohort found nothing for diet cola.** Born in Bradford (8,914 multi-ethnic UK women, recruited 2007–2010) found **no** relationship between artificially sweetened cola and preterm delivery; the association there was with **sugar**-sweetened cola at four cups/day. ([Petherick et al., *Eur J Clin Nutr* 2014;68(3):404–9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24398641/)) — **moderate**

**6. EFSA's formal conclusion on the whole preterm question, verbatim:** *"In summary, both studies appear to have been well designed and conducted. Noting this, the Panel concluded that even at high level of exposure to artificially sweetened soft drinks the risk of pre-term delivery is likely to be small, if any. This observation could be a consequence of uncontrolled residual confounding, and the inconsistencies in the patterns of association reinforce this uncertainty. When findings from the two studies are considered together, they do not point to a hazard concerning the intake of artificially sweetened soft drinks."* ([EFSA draft opinion §3.2.7.1.1](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf)) — **strong**

**7. Aspartame is the one sweetener that does not reach the fetus intact.** A controlled open-label clinical study dosed 19 pregnant women (plus 9 controls) with sweeteners two hours before planned caesarean, then measured maternal blood, cord blood and amniotic fluid by LC-MS/MS. Acesulfame-K (fetal/maternal ratio 0.80), cyclamate (0.80), saccharin (0.65) and sucralose (0.57) all crossed the placenta. **Aspartame** *"was not reported since it is metabolized in the gut lumen to its metabolites and therefore does not enter the circulation."* This is the single most important mechanistic fact in this brief: the exposure the cohorts measured includes several sweeteners that demonstrably reach the fetus, and one that does not. ([Leth-Møller et al., *Nutrients* 2023;15(9):2063](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10181363/)) — **strong**

**8. Offspring BMI: consistent direction, modest and unstable magnitude.** CHILD cohort (Canada, 3,033 dyads, 89% followed to 1 year): daily maternal ASB → infant BMI z-score **+0.20 (95% CI 0.02–0.38)** and **2.19-fold higher risk of infant overweight (1.23–3.88)**, not explained by maternal BMI, diet quality or total energy. ([Azad et al., *JAMA Pediatr* 2016;170(7):662–70](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27159792/)) — **moderate.** DNBC sub-study (918 dyads): daily ASB → large-for-gestational-age aRR **1.57 (1.05–2.35)**, BMI z at 7 y **β 0.59 (0.23–0.96)**, overweight/obesity at 7 y aRR **1.93 (1.24–3.01)** — but 7-year measurements were **parent-reported**. ([Zhu et al., *Int J Epidemiol* 2017;46(5):1499–1508](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5837735/)) — **weak-moderate.** Full DNBC (66,668 women): ≥1 ASB/day → offspring overweight OR **1.17 (1.05–1.29)** at 7 y, **1.19 (1.05–1.35)** at 11 y, **1.16 (1.03–1.32)** at 14 y, **1.26 (1.12–1.42)** at 18 y, adjusted for maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, healthy eating index, smoking, physical activity, breastfeeding duration, SES and **paternal BMI**. Authors state it was *"impossible to investigate specific artificial sweeteners."* ([Gjørup et al., *Br J Nutr* 2025;133(7):966–76](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12198349/)) — **moderate for the category**

**9. Asthma and allergic rhinitis: small, inconsistent by drink type.** DNBC, 60,466 women. Asthma at 18 months, non-carbonated diet drinks ≥1/day vs never: **1.23 (1.13–1.33)**; carbonated: not significant. Asthma at 7 y, carbonated, hospital registry: **1.30 (1.01–1.66)**; medication registry **1.13 (0.98–1.29)**. Allergic rhinitis at 7 y, carbonated: **1.31 (0.98–1.74)** — not significant. The pattern flips between drink type and between outcome ascertainment method, which is what confounding usually looks like. Authors concede *"our findings may be due to residual confounding."* ([Maslova et al., *PLOS ONE* 2013;8(2):e57261](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3584110/)) — **weak**

**10. The meta-analyses agree on direction and on low certainty.** Eleven studies, ~138,000 women: daily artificial sweetener use → preterm delivery **RR ≈1.18**, gestational age **−0.11 weeks**, birth weight **+24 g**, all at **"low" to "very low" certainty**. ([Cai et al., *Public Health Nutr* 2021;24(15):5024–33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33441213/)) — **moderate.** The most recent child-health meta-analysis (17 studies, 306,147 participants, 2000–2023) found **any** LCS use in pregnancy **not** associated with birth weight (SMD 0.03, −0.03 to 0.08) or offspring overweight; BMI z-score null at every age from birth to 7 y; overweight at 1 y **OR 1.19 (0.81–1.58)** — non-significant; congenital heart defects **OR 1.05 (0.92–1.20)** — null. Only *frequent* consumption showed elevations, and *"the estimates were imprecise."* All GRADE ratings **low or very low**. ([Gebremichael et al., *Matern Child Nutr* 2025;21:e13737](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11650048/)) — **strong as a source; its content is a caution against overclaiming in both directions**

**11. WHO's 2023 guideline explicitly covers pregnant women.** Verbatim: *"With the exception of individuals with diabetes… this recommendation is relevant for everyone: children and adults of any age, including pregnant and lactating women."* On the evidence: *"Meta-analysis of three prospective observational studies found an increased risk of preterm birth with higher NSS use during pregnancy (low certainty evidence)"* and *"associations between NSS use during pregnancy and outcomes in offspring, including increased risk of asthma and allergies, and poorer cognitive function (very low certainty evidence)."* ([WHO guideline on non-sugar sweeteners, 15 May 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK592246/)) — **strong.** This is a genuine caveat and the site must not omit it.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

1. **The preterm signal is not a single study that failed to replicate.** It is a moderately consistent direction across DNBC, MoBa (weakly), and meta-analysis. The site should not describe it as "debunked." EFSA itself said the Danish study had "no major flaws in the methods used."
2. **The DNBC offspring-overweight signal survives adjustment for paternal BMI**, which is a reasonably demanding negative-control-ish adjustment for shared familial obesogenic environment, and it persists to age 18. That is stronger than the usual "residual confounding" dismissal allows.
3. **No randomised trial exists, and none is likely.** Every regulator conclusion in this area rests either on modelling (EFSA's phenylalanine kinetics) or on observational data. There is no RCT of aspartame in pregnancy with birth outcomes.
4. **EFSA's pregnancy safety margin is a model, not a trial** — see `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md` for the 82%-of-simulations figure for PKU heterozygotes. That caveat applies here too and should be linked, not restated.
5. **Reverse causality is not a complete answer.** Women with pre-existing obesity, hypertension or glucose intolerance both drink more diet soda and deliver preterm more often. Adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI helps but cannot fully address a trajectory. This weakens the association without eliminating it.
6. **The most-cited critique of the autism paper is industry-funded.** The Ayoub-Charette *et al.* comment (below) is scientifically sound, but its authors disclose honoraria and grants from IFIC, IAFNS, the Calorie Control Council, the American Beverage Association and the International Sweeteners Association — all **industry advocacy bodies**. Cite the argument, disclose the funding, and do not lean on it alone.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "Aspartame causes premature birth — a study of 60,000 Danish women proved it." | The study measured **artificially sweetened soft drinks**, not aspartame. EFSA's reading: the exposure assessment "was for sweeteners in general and not specific for aspartame," and no other dietary sweetener sources were counted. The signal was strongest for *medically induced* preterm delivery — a clinical decision, not a birth process. |
| "The Norwegian study confirmed the Danish result." | It did the opposite in the ways that matter. AS beverages: OR 1.11 (1.00–1.24), P-trend 0.053. **Sugar**-sweetened beverages had the stronger and more consistent association (OR 1.25; 1.41 at ≥4/day; OR 1.75 for early preterm). And the AS signal there was for *spontaneous*, not induced, delivery — the reverse of the Danish subtype pattern. |
| "Aspartame crosses the placenta and reaches the baby." | Aspartame as such does not enter maternal circulation at all — it is hydrolysed in the gut lumen. The direct caesarean-section study could not measure it for that reason, while acesulfame-K, cyclamate, saccharin and sucralose all crossed. Its *metabolites* (phenylalanine, aspartate, methanol) do circulate, and phenylalanine is the exposure EFSA actually modelled. |
| "Diet soda in pregnancy triples autism risk in boys." | This comes from a **retrospective case-control** study (235 ASD cases, 121 controls) that asked mothers to recall soda intake from pregnancies **up to 30 years earlier**, collected no other dietary data, and did not adjust for maternal obesity or diabetes — both established autism risk factors. ORs 3.1 (1.02–9.7) for diet soda, 3.4 (1.1–10.4) for aspartame, with confidence intervals nearly touching 1.0. The design cannot support the claim. |
| "Regulators have never looked at the pregnancy epidemiology." | EFSA devoted a dedicated section (§3.2.7.1.1) to it, ANSES reviewed it in 2011, and WHO meta-analysed it in 2023. All three reached the same place: real association, unestablished causation, further studies needed. |

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`myths.html` #preterm and `the-evidence.html`: "a small but significant elevation" understates the Danish effect size.** That phrase is lifted from EFSA's *summary*, but EFSA's own detailed section reports OR 1.78 (1.19–2.66) at ≥4 servings/day. A 78% relative elevation is not "small" in ordinary usage. Recommend rephrasing to give the actual odds ratios and letting the reader judge, e.g. "38% higher odds at ≥1/day and 78% at ≥4/day — an elevation EFSA judged likely to reflect residual confounding." The current wording risks the exact charge the site exists to avoid: soft-pedalling an inconvenient number.
2. **`myths.html` #preterm frames the whole Danish finding as being about medically induced delivery.** The headline result (OR 1.38 / 1.78) was for **all** preterm delivery <37 weeks. The induced-vs-spontaneous point is a *subgroup* observation about where the trend was strongest. The current sentence structure implies the finding only ever concerned induced deliveries. Recommend: state the overall result first, then the subtype pattern as the interpretive caveat.
3. **Missing, and it is the best argument the site has:** neither `myths.html` nor `the-evidence.html` mentions that **aspartame does not enter maternal circulation intact and therefore cannot be measured in cord blood**, while every other common sweetener in those same diet drinks does cross the placenta. This single mechanistic fact does more to decouple aspartame from the diet-drink cohort findings than any confounding argument. It should be added.
4. **Missing: WHO 2023 explicitly extends its conditional recommendation to pregnant and lactating women.** `caveats.html` covers the children/ADI-margin issue well but does not state this. Given the site's standard of conceding every genuine caveat, it belongs there.
5. Not an error, but a gap: the site has no page or section covering **offspring BMI, asthma or neurodevelopment** at all. These are the claims most likely to be encountered by a pregnant reader searching the topic.

## What I could NOT verify

- **ACOG has no publicly retrievable position statement on aspartame or artificial sweeteners in pregnancy that I could find.** Searches of acog.org returned only the general "Nutrition During Pregnancy" patient pamphlet, which is paywalled/store-gated and whose contents I could not read. **The site must not attribute a pregnancy-safety endorsement to ACOG.** The verifiable US endorsements are FDA's general safety conclusion (which covers the general population and does not single out pregnancy) and secondary reports of an American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Nutrition position, which I could not trace to a primary AAP document.
- **The Halldorsson full text (AJCN and ScienceDirect) returned HTTP 403.** All Danish-cohort numbers above are from the PubMed abstract and EFSA's detailed second-hand description, which are mutually consistent. I could not read the funding/COI statement, and I could not confirm the exact spontaneous-vs-induced odds ratios (EFSA reports the *pattern* but not the subgroup figures).
- **A discrepancy I could not resolve:** the AJCN abstract states risk was stronger for early and moderately preterm than late preterm, while EFSA's expert reading states the associations "did not differ demonstrably" by gestational-age category. Do not quote either as settled.
- **Cohen 2018 (Project Viva, diet soda and child cognition):** full text returned 403. The Gebremichael meta-analysis characterises it as finding each additional daily serving associated with reduced cognitive scores, but I could not verify the exact coefficients or CIs and would not publish a number from it.
- **Plows et al. 2022**, cited by the Gebremichael meta-analysis as one of three aspartame-specific studies, was not independently retrieved.
- **The 2024 *Clin Nutr ESPEN* maternal-health meta-analysis** (19 studies, 203,706 participants, preterm RR 1.11, 95% CI 1.07–1.16) returned 403 on ScienceDirect. Those figures come from search-result summaries only and should be re-verified before publication.
