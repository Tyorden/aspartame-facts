# The ADI: how the number was built, and how much of it people actually use

## Bottom line

The 40 mg/kg bw/day ADI is a genuinely conservative number with a transparent derivation: a NOAEL of 4,000 mg/kg bw/day from a 104-week rat study, divided by a default 100-fold uncertainty factor (10× animal-to-human, 10× human-to-human). Measured population intake is nowhere near it — mean adult intake across national surveys sits around 1–5 mg/kg bw/day, and even the heaviest-consuming subgroups ever surveyed (diabetics, toddlers) top out around 7–14 mg/kg bw/day in real-consumption estimates. The one number that gets uncomfortably close, EFSA's 36.9 mg/kg for 95th-percentile toddlers, is a maximum-permitted-level model, not a measurement. The site's calculator inputs are almost all defensible, but **three things need fixing**: the claim that no adverse effect appeared at any dose tested in animals, the claim that 4,000 mg/kg is the highest dose ever tested, and the claim that the caffeine ceiling binds before the aspartame ceiling *at any body weight* — which is false for anyone under about 43 kg, i.e. most children.

## Key findings

**1. The ADI derivation, verbatim from the source.** JECFA's 96th meeting report states: *"At its twenty-fifth meeting, the Committee established an ADI of 0–40 mg/kg bodyweight (bw) for aspartame. This ADI was based on the no-observed-adverse-effect limit (NOAEL) of 4000 mg/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested, in a 104-week study in rats exposed to aspartame in the diet reported by Ishii et al., and the application of a 100-fold uncertainty factor."* ([JECFA 96th meeting Summary & Conclusions, 2023, PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/food-safety/jecfa/summary-and-conclusions/jecfa96-summary-and-conclusions.pdf), p.1) — **strong**

**2. What the 100 is made of.** The default 100 is the product of two 10-fold factors: **10× for interspecies extrapolation** (rats may not be a worst case for humans) and **10× for intraspecies variation** (humans differ from each other in absorption, metabolism and sensitivity). Under the WHO/IPCS chemical-specific adjustment factor framework, originating with Renwick (1993), each 10 is further split into toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic components: interspecies 4.0 (kinetic) × 2.5 (dynamic) = 10; intraspecies 3.16 × 3.16 ≈ 10. ([WHO/IPCS Harmonization Project Document No. 2, PDF](https://www.inchem.org/documents/harmproj/harmproj/harmproj2.pdf)) — **strong**

**3. FDA describes the same logic in plain language.** *"The ADI is typically based on evaluating toxicological studies to determine the highest appropriate experimental exposure dose level in animal studies that was shown to cause no adverse effect, multiplied by an appropriate safety factor… a safety factor is intended to provide an adequate margin of safety for consumers by accounting for variability, such as differences between animals and humans and differences in sensitivity among humans."* ([FDA, Aspartame and Other Sweeteners in Food](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food)) — **strong**

**4. The US 50 mg/kg figure dates from 1983, not 1981, and its provenance is thinner.** FDA's own regulatory timeline: *"The FDA approved the use of aspartame in carbonated beverages [July 1983]. The FDA also established the ADI of 50 mg/kg body weight because of its review of the use of aspartame in carbonated beverages."* JECFA's 40 mg/kg was set two years earlier, in 1981, and FDA notes it had a participant at that meeting. ([FDA, Timeline of Selected FDA Activities… Addressing Aspartame](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/timeline-selected-fda-activities-and-significant-events-addressing-aspartame)) — **strong** for the dates; I could not find an FDA document stating which NOAEL and which factor produce 50 rather than 40 (see "What I could NOT verify").

**5. JECFA's 2023 exposure conclusion.** *"dietary exposure estimates to aspartame at the mean of up to 10 mg/kg bw per day for children and 5 mg/kg bw per day for adults, and for high dietary exposures up to 20 mg/kg bw per day for children and 12 mg/kg bw per day for adults."* JECFA: these *"do not exceed the ADI"*. ([JECFA 96th, p.3](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/food-safety/jecfa/summary-and-conclusions/jecfa96-summary-and-conclusions.pdf)) — **strong**

**6. Actual national survey intakes are far lower than JECFA's conservative envelope.** IARC's Monograph 134 exposure chapter reports typical adult mean intakes ranging from *"almost 0 to about 5 mg/kg bw per day"* across national surveys; Italy 2005 mean 0.182 mg/kg bw/day; Republic of Korea under 1 mg/kg bw/day; US 2013–2016 means 1.1 mg/kg (adolescents) to 2.3 mg/kg (elderly); US/UK toddlers up to 4.8 mg/kg; Argentine children 2–4 up to 6.8 mg/kg; French children 13–36 months up to 14 mg/kg bw/day — the highest real-consumption figure in the chapter, and still 35% of the ADI. ([IARC Monographs Vol. 134, Aspartame, NCBI Bookshelf](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK609291/)) — **strong**

**7. Diabetics are the classic "high consumer" group and still come in around a quarter of the ADI.** Summarising the EU Scientific Committee on Food's 2002 update and the EC Food Additive Intake report (2001): UK diabetics aged 2–65, 97.5th percentile, 10.1 mg/kg bw/day (25% of ADI, 1995); French diabetics aged 2–20, 97.5th percentile, 7.8 mg/kg bw/day (19.5%, 2001); Netherlands all ages 95th percentile 7.5 mg/kg (18.75%, 2001); UK children 1–5, 90th percentile, 2.8 mg/kg (7%, 1992); Finnish children 1–6 under 4 mg/kg (<10%, 1999). ([GreenFacts digest of SCF "Update on the Safety of Aspartame" / EC Food Additive Intake report](https://www.greenfacts.org/en/aspartame/l-3/aspartame-2.htm)) — **moderate** (faithful secondary digest; I did not retrieve the SCF original)

**8. The most recent US estimate, from a non-industry group.** Riess, Huynh & Nachman (Johns Hopkins) combined NHANES 2017–2020 recalls with NielsenIQ purchasing data and the USDA Global Branded Food Products database to capture aspartame beyond diet soda. Aspartame exposure was prevalent (**62.6%** of the US population), and *"mean, 90th percentile, and 95th percentile aspartame intake estimates are below the acceptable daily intake (50 mg/kg/day) and are lower than estimates from previous decades."* Highest-intake groups: non-Hispanic whites, 60–69-year-olds, and **people on diabetic diets** — the same subgroup the 1990s European surveys flagged. ([J Expo Sci Environ Epidemiol, 9 May 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41370-024-00678-7)) — **strong** for the direction; I could not retrieve the exact mg/kg values behind the paywall.

**9. Global reviews find no ADI exceedance anywhere.** Martyn et al. 2018, reviewing a decade of global low-/no-calorie sweetener intake data, found no concerns regarding ADI exceedance in the general population globally. **Label this as industry-adjacent**: the authors are affiliated with Creme Global and the work sits in the ILSI Europe orbit; I could not retrieve the funding statement directly. ([Nutrients 2018;10(3):357](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu10030357)) — **moderate**, and corroborated independently by IARC and JECFA above

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

1. **"No adverse effect at any dose tested" is not true across the animal database.** JECFA's own text: the mouse developmental NOAEL was **5,700 mg/kg bw/day, the highest dose tested** — so 4,000 mg/kg is emphatically *not* the highest dose ever fed to animals. And EFSA 2013 identified a rabbit developmental/maternal NOAEL of **1,000 mg/kg bw/day**, meaning adverse effects *were* seen above that dose in rabbits, and stated it could not exclude developmental toxicity below 4,000 mg/kg in animals. EFSA attributed those effects to released phenylalanine and switched to human phenylalanine kinetics — a defensible mechanistic judgement, not a null result. (See `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md`.)

2. **The ADI is a per-kilogram number, which makes children the binding case.** JECFA's own high-exposure figure for children is **20 mg/kg bw/day — half the ADI**, not a hundredth of it. EFSA's worst-case (maximum-permitted-level) model reached **36.9 mg/kg for 95th-percentile toddlers (92% of the ADI)** and 32.9 for children 3–9. Both agencies call these substantial overestimates; neither exceeds the ADI. The honest framing is "high-percentile young children approach but do not exceed the ADI under conservative assumptions."

3. **Diabetics really are the high-consumer group, and the evidence for that is consistent across three decades and two continents** (UK 1995, France 2001, US 2024). This is not a reason for alarm — 25% of the ADI is still a 400-fold margin on the NOAEL — but a debunking site should say it rather than imply everyone is equally far below.

4. **The 100-fold factor is a default convention, not a measurement.** It is applied across food additives generally; it was not derived from aspartame-specific human variability data. Its adequacy has been argued over in the risk-assessment literature for thirty years. Saying "a hundredfold margin" is accurate; implying the 100 was calculated *for aspartame* would not be.

5. **The FDA's 400 mg caffeine figure is guidance, not a regulatory ceiling.** FDA's exact wording is that 400 mg/day is *"an amount not generally associated with negative effects"* for most adults, immediately qualified by *"there is wide variation in both how sensitive people are to the effects of caffeine and how fast they eliminate it."* Calling it a "ceiling" is shorthand that slightly overstates its status.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **Myth: "The ADI is the dose at which aspartame becomes dangerous."** No. It is the NOAEL divided by 100. Exceeding it on one day is not a toxicological event; the ADI describes a lifetime daily average.
- **Myth: "Nobody knows how much people actually consume."** Wrong — this is one of the best-characterised additive exposures in existence, with national duplicate-diet and survey-based estimates from at least a dozen countries plus a 2024 US estimate that goes beyond diet soda to all ingredient-listed products.
- **Myth: "Regulators just assume nobody eats much."** The opposite: EFSA's headline model assumes every eligible food contains aspartame at the *maximum permitted level* and still lands under the ADI.
- **Myth (from the pro-aspartame side): "Real intake is ~1% of the ADI."** Overclaim. Mean adult intake is roughly 2–12% of the ADI; high-consuming children under conservative models reach 50–92%.

### Calculator input audit

- **200 mg aspartame per 12 oz can — OK, and conservative.** WHO's 2023 release uses *"200 or 300 mg"* per can and 70 kg. Taking the low end is the conservative choice (it yields *more* cans to reach the ADI, which is the anti-alarmist direction — worth a one-line disclosure). The widely repeated "192 mg" figure does **not** appear anywhere on FDA's current aspartame page; do not attribute it to FDA.
- **40 mg per tabletop packet — arithmetically correct, but it is a sweetness-equivalence construct, not a measured packet.** FDA's footnote reads: *"Number of sweetener packets a 60 kg (132 pound) person would need to consume to reach the ADI. The FDA assumed a sweetener packet is as sweet as two teaspoons (approximately 8 grams) of sugar for these comparisons."* With aspartame at ~200× sucrose, 8,000 mg ÷ 200 = 40 mg, and 60 kg × 50 mg/kg ÷ 40 mg = 75 packets. The site's derivation reproduces FDA exactly. Two things to flag: (a) real retail packets are commonly quoted at ~35 mg, so 40 mg is a regulatory construct rather than a product spec; (b) the calculator divides the **EU 40 mg/kg** ADI by 40 mg, so a 60 kg user sees **60 packets**, not FDA's 75 — fine, but the table caption invites the reader to expect 75.
- **46 mg caffeine per 12 oz — plausible and within FDA's 23–83 mg range, but weakly sourced.** The site cites caffeineinformer.com, a consumer aggregator. Replace with the FDA range plus the Coca-Cola Company's published product figure (labelled as manufacturer data), or USDA FoodData Central. I could not independently retrieve a 46 mg value from either FDA or USDA.
- **400 mg/day caffeine — verified verbatim.** FDA: *"For most adults, the FDA has cited 400 milligrams a day — that's about two to three 12-fluid-ounce cups of coffee — as an amount not generally associated with negative effects."* URL: https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`calculator.html` line ~121 and `perspective.html` line ~130: "no adverse effect turned up at any dose tested" / "found no adverse effect at any dose they tested" — overstated and contradicts EFSA.** EFSA identified a rabbit maternal/developmental NOAEL of 1,000 mg/kg bw/day and said it *could not exclude* developmental toxicity below 4,000 mg/kg in animals. Correct wording: "no adverse effect at any dose tested **in the pivotal chronic rat study on which the ADI rests**."

2. **`calculator.html` assumptions table: "4,000 mg/kg/day — the highest dose ever tested in animals" is false.** JECFA's own text gives a mouse developmental NOAEL of **5,700 mg/kg bw/day, the highest dose tested**, and Ramazzini fed higher dietary concentrations still. Correct to: "the highest dose tested in the 104-week rat study the ADI is derived from."

3. **`perspective.html` line 136: "the caffeine ceiling arrives first at any body weight" is false, and the calculator's dynamic text breaks accordingly.** Aspartame cans-to-ADI = 40 mg/kg × kg ÷ 200 mg = 0.2 × kg. Caffeine cans = 400 ÷ 46 = 8.7, a **constant**. They cross at **43.5 kg (96 lb)**. Below that weight — i.e. essentially every child under about 12 — the *aspartame* ceiling arrives first. For a 20 kg five-year-old the calculator computes 4.0 aspartame cans vs 8.7 caffeine cans and renders "you run out of caffeine headroom roughly **0.5× sooner**", which is incoherent. Given the calculator has an age input and explicitly addresses children, fix both: gate the sentence on `cansToAdi > cansToCaffeine`, and change perspective.html to "at adult body weights" (or "above about 43 kg").

4. **`calculator.html` assumptions table: "US Acceptable Daily Intake 50 mg/kg/day — US FDA" should be dated 1983,** not implicitly 1981. FDA established it "because of its review of the use of aspartame in carbonated beverages."

5. **`sources.html` line ~151: "FDA's own pages were not directly retrieved" for the 400 mg caffeine figure — this is now retrievable.** Replace with the direct URL and verbatim quote above, and add the FDA 23–83 mg per-12-oz soft-drink range as a companion. Also soften "caffeine daily ceiling" to FDA's actual phrasing ("an amount not generally associated with negative effects"), since FDA frames it as guidance, not a limit.

6. **`the-evidence.html` line 252: "FDA equivalent, tabletop packets — 75/day at 60 kg" is right but should carry FDA's sweetness-equivalence footnote,** otherwise a reader will assume 40 mg is what is physically in an Equal packet.

## What I could NOT verify

- **Why FDA's ADI is 50 rather than 40 mg/kg.** FDA's timeline confirms the 1983 date and that it followed the carbonated-beverage review, but I could not locate an FDA statement naming the NOAEL and safety factor behind 50 mg/kg. The 1981 Federal Register PDF's OCR is too degraded to search reliably, and the 1983 PDF did not yield an ADI derivation passage. Do not assert on the site that 50 mg/kg comes from 5,000 ÷ 100 or any similar arithmetic — I found no source for it.
- **Exact mg/kg values from Riess et al. 2024.** Paywalled at Nature; SSRN preprint returned 403. The directional claims (all percentiles below 50 mg/kg; highest in 60–69s and diabetic-diet respondents; 62.6% prevalence) are verified; the specific means are not.
- **Martyn et al. 2018 funding statement.** MDPI returned 403; I could not read the declaration directly. Treat the industry-adjacency note as an inference from author affiliation, not a verified disclosure.
- **A primary source for 46 mg caffeine in a 12 oz Diet Coke.** The Coca-Cola Company FAQ page returned no extractable content and I did not query USDA FoodData Central (requires an API key). The value is within FDA's published 23–83 mg range, but the site's current citation (caffeineinformer.com) remains the weakest link on the page.
- **The measured aspartame content of a retail Equal/NutraSweet packet.** Search results split between 35 mg and 40 mg with no authoritative source for either; only FDA's sweetness-equivalence construct (40 mg) is verifiable.
- **The full EU SCF 2002 opinion text.** I relied on the GreenFacts digest for the diabetic and child intake figures rather than the original.
