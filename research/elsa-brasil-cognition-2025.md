# ELSA-Brasil: Low- and No-Calorie Sweeteners and Cognitive Decline (Gomes Gonçalves et al., *Neurology*, 2025)

*Research brief. Last updated 2026-08-04.*

## Bottom line

This is a real, large, prospective cohort study (12,772 Brazilian adults, ~8 years) that found a statistical association between higher intake of low- and no-calorie sweeteners (LNCSs) and faster cognitive decline. It is also the single most corrected high-profile nutrition paper in this area: **the article has received two separate published Correction notices — both confirmed as actual notices, not rumour — the second of which followed a full internal audit and a complete re-run of the analysis.** The exposure levels involved are extraordinarily low (top-tertile mean 191 mg/day of *all seven sweeteners combined*, under 7% of aspartame's ADI even if every milligram were aspartame), sucralose and stevia were not assessed at all, and the authors themselves, in a published reply to critics, describe their findings as "preliminary and hypothesis-generating." The honest framing for a debunking site is not "this study was retracted" (it was not) but "this is a hypothesis-generating observational study at trace exposures that needed two corrections."

## Key findings

- **The paper exists and is real.** Gomes Gonçalves N, Martinez-Steele E, Lotufo PA, Bensenor I, Goulart AC, Barreto SM, Giatti L, de Faria CP, Molina MDCB, Caramelli P, Marchioni DM, Suemoto CK. *Neurology* 2025;105(7):e214023. Received 24 Oct 2024, accepted 17 Jun 2025, online 3 Sept 2025, print issue 7 Oct 2025. ([PubMed 40902134](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40902134/)) — **strong / verified primary**

- **CORRECTION #1 — CONFIRMED.** *Neurology* 2025;105(11):e214393, published online 6 Nov 2025, print issue 9 Dec 2025, PMID 41197106, DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000214393. Verbatim substance: "there were errors in the CIs for some estimates presented in the article and the supplementary eTables." The article and supplement were **replaced** with corrected versions; the original with changes highlighted was posted alongside. The notice states "The corrections do not affect the overall conclusions of the study," and carries an authors' regret statement. ([Neurology correction](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214393)) — **strong / verified primary**

- **CORRECTION #2 — CONFIRMED.** *Neurology* 2026;107(4):e218380, published online 30 Jul 2026, print issue 25 Aug 2026, PMID 42531540, DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218380. Substance: the authors performed "a comprehensive internal audit of the entire analytical process" and found further issues. "The entire analysis has now been rerun from the raw analytical data set using fully scripted, reproducible code," with independent cross-checking by the senior author. All estimates, CIs, tables and figures were regenerated directly from model outputs; **Figures 3 and 4 were regenerated** with corrected estimates and CIs. The notice again states "The changes did not affect the main conclusions from the paper." ([Neurology correction](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000218380)) — **strong / verified primary**

- **Exactly two corrections; no retraction, no expression of concern.** Confirmed independently on three registries: PubMed lists both under "Erratum in"; the [Crossref API record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214023) lists both `is-corrected-by` relations (6 Nov 2025 and 30 Jul 2026); Europe PMC's `commentCorrectionList` shows both. A site search of Retraction Watch returned "Nothing Found" — **Retraction Watch has not covered this paper**, and there is no retraction or expression of concern. — **strong / verified across three independent registries**

- **Headline effect sizes (post-correction abstract).** In adults <60 at baseline, highest vs lowest tertile of combined LNCS intake: verbal fluency β = −0.040 (95% CI −0.066 to −0.015); global cognition β = −0.026 (95% CI −0.043 to −0.008). No significant association in adults ≥60. ([Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214023)) — **moderate** (these are the corrected values; treat any pre-November-2025 CI you find in news coverage as superseded)

- **The famous "62% / 1.6 years" figures come from the AAN press release, not the abstract.** Top tertile vs bottom: 62% faster global cognitive decline, "about 1.6 years of aging"; middle tertile: 35% faster, "about 1.3 years." ([AAN press release, 3 Sept 2025](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/5281)) — **moderate** (a relative-rate framing of a very small absolute β)

- **Sucralose was not included.** The seven sweeteners assessed were aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol and tagatose. Sucralose — the world's other dominant high-intensity sweetener — and steviol glycosides/stevia were **not** in the analysis. This is confirmed by the sweetener list in the abstract and stated explicitly in the published Wölnerhanssen et al. reader response. Six of the seven (all but tagatose) showed associations. ([Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214023)) — **strong**

- **Published correspondence: three reader responses and two author replies.** (1) Wölnerhanssen, Meyer-Gerspach & Söderling (29 Oct 2025) — exposure misclassification from FFQ, 8–9 years too short, reverse causality, no low-sweetness comparator, and the pooling of chemically unrelated artificial sweeteners with natural polyols; they note the classic Turku xylitol trials used tens of grams/day with no evident cognitive harm. (2) Arduini & Masola (6 Nov 2025) — endogenous xylitol production via the glucuronic acid pathway runs to 5–15 g/day, dwarfing the ~3 mg/day dietary intake modelled. (3) Wang & Liao (23 Dec 2025) — residual confounding by prediabetes/insulin resistance, and the absence of a sugar-sweetened-beverage comparator, which is the question clinicians actually face. — **strong / verified primary**

- **The authors' own replies concede a great deal.** Suemoto & Gomes Gonçalves (3 Nov 2025) accept the study is observational and cannot establish causality. Suemoto (12 Nov 2025) agrees endogenous xylitol synthesis "can reach gram quantities daily," states that natural dietary sources need not be avoided, clarifies the association refers to "overall LNCS exposure patterns, not to natural dietary sources," and says the findings should be interpreted as **"preliminary and hypothesis-generating."** — **strong / verified primary; this is the most quotable concession available**

- **An accompanying editorial framed it positively.** Thomas Monroe Holland (Rush Institute for Healthy Aging), "The Dark Side of Sweet: Neurocognitive Consequences of Artificial Sweeteners," *Neurology*, 3 Sept 2025, DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000214129 — canvasses mechanistic hypotheses (erythritol and brain microvascular endothelium, aspartame and microglial neuroinflammation, microbiome-mediated glucose intolerance). Note this is a commentary, not new evidence. — **weak as evidence, but relevant context**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **This is a real study in a top-5 neurology journal, and it survived two corrections with conclusions intact.** Do not imply it was withdrawn, debunked, or fraudulent. Nothing in the record supports that. The corrections were author-initiated and transparent, including a voluntary full audit — behaviour that is, if anything, creditable.
- **The direction of the finding is genuinely unfavourable to aspartame.** Aspartame specifically was among the six sweeteners associated with faster decline in global cognition, memory and verbal fluency. A site claiming "no study has ever found an aspartame–cognition association" would be wrong.
- **The cohort is large, prospective, multi-wave, and reasonably diverse** (54.8% women, 43.2% Black/mixed race), with a validated FFQ and adjustment for a wide confounder set. It is not a junk study.
- **The subgroup where the association appeared — under-60s and people with diabetes — is not implausible**, and the authors report the association persisted among participants without diabetes, which partly (not fully) answers the reverse-causality objection.
- **"Conclusions unchanged" is the journal's and authors' assessment, not an independent audit.** Two rounds of CI errors, then a self-described re-run "using fully scripted, reproducible code," implies the original analysis was not scripted or independently reproduced. That is a legitimate reason for reduced confidence, but it is not evidence the results are wrong.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **Myth: "The sweetener–dementia study was retracted."** False. Two Corrections, zero retractions, no expression of concern, and no Retraction Watch coverage as of 4 Aug 2026. Verified on PubMed, Crossref and Europe PMC.
- **Myth: "One diet soda a day ages your brain 1.6 years."** Overstated. The 1.6-year figure is a press-release translation of a relative rate difference (β = −0.026 SD/year on global cognition) between tertiles of *seven combined sweeteners* in an observational cohort, restricted to under-60s, and it did not appear in the over-60 group where dementia risk actually concentrates.
- **Myth: "The study proves sweeteners cause cognitive decline."** The authors explicitly deny this in print: observational, non-causal, "preliminary and hypothesis-generating."
- **Myth (from the other direction): "This shows sucralose harms the brain."** Sucralose was never measured in this study. Neither was stevia.
- **Half-myth: "The doses were realistic — just a diet soda."** The top-tertile mean of 191 mg/day *across all seven sweeteners* is roughly one can's worth of aspartame — but that is the ceiling of the highest exposure group, i.e. this cohort had very low sweetener exposure overall (baseline 2008, when Brazilian LNCS use was low, as the authors concede). It cannot speak to heavy consumers.

## What I could NOT verify

- The **exact numerical before/after values** changed by either correction. Neither notice publishes a delta table; both point readers to a "version with changes highlighted" PDF on neurology.org that I could not retrieve (neurology.org returns HTTP 403 to automated fetches; the Chrome extension was unavailable this session). Correction #2's text explicitly does not list which values moved. **Do not claim on the site that any specific number changed.**
- Whether a **complete-case sensitivity analysis was null**. Josh Bloom at ACSH (30 Jul 2026) asserts that restricting to participants with complete follow-up showed "no association between tertiles of LNCS consumption and cognitive decline." I could not confirm this against the paper's own supplementary material, and ACSH is an advocacy organisation with historical industry funding. **Treat as unverified; do not cite as fact.**
- Whether the FDA's consumer materials give a specific "cans of diet soda to reach the ADI" figure — the FDA pages I tried returned 404 this session. The 50 mg/kg ADI itself is well established in FDA documentation.
