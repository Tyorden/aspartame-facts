# What IARC Group 2B Actually Means

## Bottom line

Group 2B is not a danger ranking, a potency ranking, or a list of things regulators think you should avoid. It is the answer to one narrow question — *how strong is the published evidence that this agent can cause cancer in humans under some circumstance?* — and 2B is the weakest of the three positive answers. Aspartame sits at the very floor of that tier: under the 2019 Preamble, Group 2B requires **only one** of three qualifying findings, and aspartame met exactly one (limited evidence in humans). Its animal and mechanistic evidence were graded *limited*, which does not qualify for anything.

The "same category as your phone" comparison is a fair and useful teaching device, but IARC itself warns that "comparisons within a category can be misleading" — and the comparison has already partly rotted: **automotive gasoline, one of the four examples currently on perspective.html, was moved out of Group 2B and into Group 1 in 2025.** Radiofrequency fields are flagged by IARC's own priorities group for re-evaluation before 2029 on "new human cancer and animal cancer evidence." Any site that leans on the punchline should expect to keep maintaining it.

## Key findings

**1. There are four groups, not five, and the counts are confirmed.** Parsing IARC's own machine-readable classification dataset (the file that backs the public list, volumes 1–142, updated 3 July 2026) returns exactly **Group 1 = 135, Group 2A = 98, Group 2B = 329, Group 3 = 498** — 1,060 classified agents plus 68 cross-reference entries. ([IARC, Agents Classified by the IARC Monographs](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/agents-classified-by-the-iarc/)) — **strong** (primary, independently recomputed)

**2. Group 4 no longer exists.** The January 2019 Preamble merged the old Group 4 ("probably not carcinogenic") into Group 3. Caprolactam, the only agent ever placed in Group 4, is now listed in Group 3. IARC deliberately has no "proven safe" category. ([Samet et al., *JNCI* 2020;112(1):30–37](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/112/1/30/5566248)) — **strong**

**3. "Limited evidence" is a defined term, not a hedge.** Preamble, verbatim, for cancer in humans: *"Limited evidence of carcinogenicity: A causal interpretation of the positive association observed in the body of evidence on exposure to the agent and cancer is credible, but chance, bias, or confounding could not be ruled out with reasonable confidence."* That is a real finding of a credible positive association — it is not "no evidence." ([IARC Preamble, amended January 2019, PDF](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Preamble-2019.pdf)) — **strong**

**4. Group 2B's threshold is one qualifying finding.** Preamble, verbatim: *"This category generally applies when only one of the following evaluations has been made by the Working Group: Limited evidence of carcinogenicity in humans, Sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in experimental animals, Strong evidence that the agent exhibits key characteristics of carcinogens."* Group 2A needs at least two, one of which must involve exposed humans or human cells/tissues. Group 1 needs sufficient human evidence. — **strong**

**5. Aspartame met exactly one criterion, the weakest way into the group.** IARC's own Summary of Findings: Group 2B *"based on limited evidence for cancer in humans (for hepatocellular carcinoma)"*; there was *"also limited evidence for cancer in experimental animals"* and *"limited mechanistic evidence."* Because "limited" animal evidence is not "sufficient," and "limited" mechanistic evidence is not "strong," neither of those two streams counted toward the classification at all. Three limiteds do not add up to a stronger category. ([IARC/JECFA Summary of Findings, 14 July 2023, PDF](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Summary_of_findings_Aspartame.pdf)) — **strong**

**6. Red meat is one tier above aspartame for a precise, explainable reason.** Red meat is Group 2A on *"limited evidence that the consumption of red meat causes cancer in humans and strong mechanistic evidence"* — two qualifying findings. Processed meat is Group 1 on *sufficient* human evidence for colorectal cancer. Alcoholic beverages (and ethanol in them, and acetaldehyde from them) are Group 1. ([IARC Press Release 240, 26 October 2015, PDF](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf); classification list) — **strong**

**7. IARC states the hazard/risk distinction in its own words, repeatedly.** Preamble: *"A cancer hazard is an agent that is capable of causing cancer, whereas a cancer risk is an estimate of the probability that cancer will occur given some level of exposure… The Monographs identify cancer hazards even when risks appear to be low in some exposure scenarios."* Q&A: *"the classification does not indicate the level of risk associated with a given level or circumstance of exposure. The cancer risk associated with substances or agents assigned the same classification may be very different."* ([IARC Monographs Questions and Answers, 10 December 2019, PDF](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/IARCMonographs-QA.pdf)) — **strong**

**8. IARC explicitly disclaims regulatory advice.** Preamble: *"no recommendations are given in the Monographs with regard to regulation, legislation, or other policy approaches, which are the responsibility of individual governments or organizations."* A 2B listing is therefore not, and cannot be, an intake recommendation. — **strong**

**9. IARC itself made the within-category comparison — and then warned against it.** The 2019 Q&A uses exactly this device: *"radiofrequency electromagnetic fields and the prescription drug digoxin are both classified in Group 2B."* But the same document says: *"The types of exposures, the extent of risk, the people who may be at risk, and the cancer types linked with the agent can be very different across agents. Therefore, comparisons within a category can be misleading."* And in its coffee FAQ: very hot beverages and DDT share Group 2A, but *"this does NOT mean that they are equally dangerous."* ([IARC, "Debunking the Myths of the Monographs," 15 June 2016, PDF](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Debunking-the-Myths-of-the-Monographs116.pdf)) — **strong**

**10. Group 2B is heterogeneous in *vintage*, not just in subject matter.** Of the 329 entries, **163 (about half) carry a most-recent publication year before 2000**, and **87 date to 1989 or earlier** — many to Supplement 7 (1987), including "Carpentry and joinery." The list mixes 2025 systematic reviews with judgements made before the 2019 Preamble existed. — **strong** (recomputed from the IARC dataset)

**11. Agents move, in both directions.** Occupational exposure as a firefighter went 2B → **Group 1** (Vol 132, 2023). Talc went to **2A** (Vol 136, 2025). Automotive gasoline went 2B → **Group 1** (Vol 138, evaluated Feb–Mar 2025). Coffee went 2B → **Group 3** in 2016. Saccharin — the sweetener of the 1970s cancer panic — is **Group 3**; so are cyclamates; sucralose has never been classified. Aspartame is the only sweetener in 2B. — **strong**

**12. IARC's own priorities group declined to look at artificial sweeteners again.** In the Advisory Group report for 2025–2029, *"Sucralose"* and *"Artificially sweetened beverage consumption"* were nominated and placed in Table 3, **not recommended for evaluation**, under the rationale *"Existing evidence does not appear to support a first-time classification."* ([Advisory Group to Recommend Priorities for the IARC Monographs during 2025–2029, November 2024, PDF](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/AGP_Report_2025-2029.pdf)) — **strong**. Note the same table also declined to revisit glyphosate and night shift work, so this is a general "no new evidence" verdict rather than an endorsement.

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The mobile-phone anchor is unstable.** The same Advisory Group put *"Non-ionising radiation (radiofrequency)"* in **Table 1, high priority**, advised for the latter half of 2025–2029, with the rationale *"New human cancer and animal cancer evidence to warrant re-evaluation of the classification."* If RF-EMF is upgraded, "same category as your phone" stops being reassuring overnight. As of the current meeting schedule (Volume 143, cannabis smoking, November 2026), no RF meeting has been announced.
- **Group 2B contains plenty of things nobody finds funny.** Alongside aloe vera and pickled vegetables sit PFOS (added 2025), carbon tetrachloride, chloroform, hexachlorobenzene, chlordane, titanium dioxide, lead, nickel metal, occupational exposure in dry cleaning and in printing, and marine diesel fuel. Cherry-picking the amusing members is exactly the selective reading the site criticises elsewhere.
- **The comparison is an association fallacy run in reverse.** "X is in the same box as harmless things, therefore X is harmless" has the identical logical form as "X is in the same box as gasoline, therefore X is dangerous." Both are invalid for the same reason, and IARC has said so in print. The comparison legitimately explains *what the box is*; it cannot carry any inference about aspartame.
- **Aspartame is in the minority of 2B agents that got there on human data.** Most of Group 2B rests on animal bioassays with inadequate or absent human evidence. Aspartame's classification rests on the human stream — three cohort studies all showing a positive association for liver cancer. That is a weaker basis than 2A but a *more human-relevant* basis than much of the group. Framing aspartame as "just another animal-study entry" would be wrong.
- **"Not classifiable" (Group 3) does not mean safe, and IARC says so.** Preamble: *"An evaluation in Group 3 is not a determination of non-carcinogenicity or overall safety. It often means that the agent is of unknown carcinogenic potential and that there are significant gaps in research."* Readers of the counts table will assume otherwise unless told.
- **IARC does consider exposure — just not dose–response for risk.** The Monographs include an exposure characterization section, the priorities process requires "evidence of human exposure," and the Preamble notes that some epidemiological studies *"are also used to estimate an exposure–response relationship within the range of the available data."* Saying IARC "does not ask whether anyone is exposed" overstates it.
- **IARC has quantified risk when it could.** For processed meat it published *"each 50 gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases the risk of colorectal cancer by 18%."* No comparable figure exists for aspartame — which is a statement about the state of the evidence, not proof of a small effect.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Group 2B is the WHO's cancer list." | It is one of four tiers of an evidence-strength taxonomy covering 1,060 agents. IARC issues no regulatory advice at all. |
| "Group 2B means it might cause cancer, so avoid it." | It means one of three specified evidence findings was met. Whether to avoid it is a risk-assessment and policy question IARC explicitly does not answer. |
| "There's a Group 4 for safe things, and aspartame isn't in it." | Group 4 was abolished in the January 2019 Preamble. Only caprolactam was ever in it; it is now Group 3. |
| "IARC put aspartame in the same group as gasoline." | Not any more. Automotive gasoline is **Group 1** as of IARC Volume 138 (evaluated Feb–Mar 2025). Aviation gasoline and gasoline engine exhaust remain 2B. |
| "Bacon is Group 1, so bacon is as dangerous as asbestos." | Same error, opposite polarity. Group 1 means the evidence is conclusive, not that the risks are comparable. IARC's Q&A uses active smoking vs second-hand smoke vs air pollution — all Group 1, wildly different risks. |
| "Red meat is Group 1." | Red meat is **2A**. Processed meat is Group 1. Alcoholic beverages are Group 1. |
| "Saccharin was banned as a carcinogen, so sweeteners are suspect." | Saccharin and its salts are **Group 3**, downgraded in IARC Volume 73 (1999). |
| "Aspartame had three findings against it, so it's a strong 2B." | The opposite. Only the human-evidence finding qualified; "limited" animal and "limited" mechanistic evidence meet no Preamble criterion. It is the minimum possible basis for the group. |

## Numbers worth quoting on a website

- **Group counts, volumes 1–142, updated 3 July 2026**: Group 1 = **135**; Group 2A = **98**; Group 2B = **329**; Group 3 = **498**. Total classified = **1,060**.
- **Group 2B entry threshold**: **one** of three qualifying findings. Group 2A: **at least two**, one involving exposed humans or human cells/tissues.
- **Aspartame**: Group 2B, IARC Volume 134, working group **6–13 June 2023**, published online **2024**; basis = limited human evidence (hepatocellular carcinoma) only.
- **Age of the group**: **163 of 329** Group 2B entries (≈50%) were last published before 2000; **87** in 1989 or earlier. "Carpentry and joinery" = Volume 25 / Supplement 7, **1987**.
- **Radiofrequency electromagnetic fields**: Group 2B, evaluated **May 2011**, Volume 102 published **2013**; flagged **high priority for re-evaluation, latter half of 2025–2029**.
- **Automotive gasoline**: **Group 1**, Volume 138, working group **February–March 2025**, list entry "2026 online". Causes bladder cancer and acute myeloid leukaemia.
- **Red meat**: Group **2A**; **processed meat**: Group **1**; IARC Volume 114, 22 experts from 10 countries, **26 October 2015**; **+18% colorectal cancer risk per 50 g/day of processed meat**.
- **Alcoholic beverages**: Group **1** (Volumes 44, 96, 100E, 2012). **Solar radiation**: Group 1. **Plutonium**: Group 1. **Firefighting**: Group 1 since 2023.
- **Saccharin and cyclamates**: Group **3**. **Sucralose**: never classified, and **not recommended for evaluation** in 2025–2029.
- **Group 2B members that undercut the punchline**: PFOS (2025), carbon tetrachloride, chloroform, hexachlorobenzene, chlordane, titanium dioxide, lead, marine diesel fuel, occupational exposure in dry cleaning and printing.
- **FDA caffeine reference points**: **400 mg/day** for healthy adults; **~1,200 mg** rapidly consumed for toxic effects such as seizures.

## Corrections to the existing site

1. **`perspective.html`, line 50 — "Gasoline" is no longer a Group 2B example.** Automotive gasoline was reclassified to **Group 1** in IARC Volume 138 (working group February–March 2025; the classification list entry reads `group:"1", volume:["45","138"], year:"2026 online"`). The site's own footer dates its counts to the 3 July 2026 list, which already contains this change. Replace with a still-current example (e.g. **titanium dioxide**, **progestogen-only contraceptives**, or **digoxin** — the last being IARC's own chosen illustration), or narrow the wording to "aviation gasoline" / "gasoline engine exhaust," both of which remain 2B. **This is the most consequential error found.**
2. **`perspective.html`, callout "Use the comparison, but know its limits" — understates IARC's position.** IARC does not merely say the comparison isn't the argument; it says *"comparisons within a category can be misleading"* (Q&A, 2019) and, of two Group 2A agents, *"this does NOT mean that they are equally dangerous"* (Myths of the Monographs, 2016). Quoting IARC against the site's own rhetorical device would be stronger and more honest than the current soft framing.
3. **`perspective.html`, line 70–72 — "It does not ask… whether anyone is exposed to that much" is an overstatement.** The Monographs include an exposure characterization section, the priorities process requires demonstrated human exposure, and the Preamble says epidemiological studies are sometimes used to estimate exposure–response within the range of available data. Suggested wording: "It does not estimate the probability of cancer at any particular level of exposure."
4. **`perspective.html` and `the-evidence.html` — the Group 3 row needs IARC's caveat.** "Not classifiable" invites readers to read Group 3 as "cleared." IARC: *"An evaluation in Group 3 is not a determination of non-carcinogenicity or overall safety."* One clause fixes it, and it makes the site's handling of the taxonomy visibly even-handed.
5. **`perspective.html`, footer — "Caffeine figures from FDA guidance" does not match the table.** FDA's published figures are 400 mg/day and ~1,200 mg for toxic effects. The table's "~10 mg/kg" acute-toxicity row (≈0.7 g) and "~150 mg/kg" lethal row are from the toxicology literature, not FDA. Either cite the literature separately or use FDA's 1,200 mg. (Flagged for the owner of that section; caffeine is not this brief's remit.)
6. **`perspective.html`, line 98 — "roughly 200 mg of aspartame" per 12 oz can.** The CPS-II cohort brief in this repo uses a measured range of **70–180 mg per 12-oz serving**. 200 mg is at or above the top of that range. The error is conservative (it makes the "14 cans" figure smaller), but the site should say "up to about 180 mg" or label 200 mg as an upper bound. Cross-check with whoever owns `calculator.html`.
7. **`myths.html`, line 108 and `the-evidence.html`, line 177 — both cite gasoline as a 2B example.** Same fix as item 1.

Nothing else on `perspective.html` failed verification. The counts (329 / 135 / 98 / 498, 3 July 2026), the RF-EMF 2011 date, aloe vera whole leaf extract (Vol 108), traditional Asian pickled vegetables (Vol 56), carpentry and joinery (Vol 25/Sup 7), and "sunlight and processed meat sit in Group 1 alongside plutonium" all check out against the primary list.
