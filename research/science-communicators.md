# What the Public-Health Communicators Actually Say: Dr Idz, Jessica Knurick, The Plant Slant, and "Morticia"

*Research brief. Last updated 2026-08-05.*

## Bottom line

All four are broadly on the evidence-based side, and two of them (Mughal, Layton) have made
aspartame-specific claims worth borrowing. But the research turns up one fact this site must
handle before citing any of them: **Dr Idrees Mughal received a formal FTC warning letter on
13 November 2023 over two aspartame-safety videos that the FTC says were paid for by the
American Beverage Association.** The letter is a verified primary document and quotes his posts
verbatim. Separately, he hosts a sweetener podcast produced by a commercial branded-podcast
studio whose funder I could not confirm. None of this makes his science wrong — the FTC objected
only to *disclosure conspicuousness*, not to accuracy — but a site whose credibility rests on
being scrupulous cannot cite him as an independent voice without disclosing it.

Practical upshot: **use their framings, not their authority.** Every argument below that is worth
adopting is worth adopting because it traces to a regulator or a paper, not because a
communicator said it. And two of Mughal's specific formulations ("36 cans", "JECFA confirmed
aspartame does not cause cancer") should **not** be repeated — see below.

## Key findings

### 1. Dr Idrees Mughal ("Dr Idz")

- **Credentials as claimed: MBBS, MRes, DipIBLM.** He posts under these post-nominals himself
  ([@dr_idz on Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/dr_idz/)); Penguin Random House's author page
  and his own [School of Dr Idz](https://www.schoolofdridz.com/) carry the same. *Saturated Facts:
  A Myth-Busting Guide to Diet and Nutrition in a World of Misinformation* (Penguin Life, 2024,
  ISBN 9780241588222) is real and traceable to the publisher
  ([Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/452351/saturated-facts-by-mughal-dr-idrees/9780241588222)).
  — **strong for the book and self-description; see "could not verify" for GMC registration**

- **Following: roughly 1.5 million+ across TikTok and Instagram.** This figure comes from a
  third-party profile, not from a platform-verified count — treat as approximate.
  — **weak**

- **FTC warning letter, verified verbatim.** Federal Trade Commission, Division of Advertising
  Practices, signed by Associate Director Serena Viswanathan, dated **13 November 2023**
  ([FTC warning letter to Dr Idrees Mughal, PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/warning-letter-mughal.pdf)).
  The letter states, quoting his posts directly:
  - **30 June 2023** (Instagram *and* TikTok): aspartame has *"not been shown to cause cancer in
    humans"* and *"a small adult would need up to 36 cans of diet Coke every day to be at risk,
    so stop freaking out over headlines."*
  - **13 July 2023** (Instagram): *"the Joint Expert Committee on Food Additives just confirmed
    right now that aspartame does not cause cancer in humans and that it is safe,"* and
    *"aspartame is safe."*
  - FTC: *"It appears that you were paid by the American Beverage Association ('ABA') to make
    those posts."*
  — **strong / verified primary document**

- **What the FTC did and did not object to.** The letter's complaint is entirely about disclosure.
  He *did* disclose — he used the Instagram "Paid partnership with ameribev" tool and "#ad"/"#AD"
  in the caption. The FTC's position was that (a) nothing appeared in the video itself, (b) the
  built-in "Paid partnership" tool alone is insufficient, (c) "#ad" appeared "in or below the
  fifth line of the text description," where truncation hides it, and (d) *"many viewers may not
  understand what 'ameribev' is."* Penalty exposure cited: up to **$50,120 per violation**.
  **Nothing in the letter challenges the scientific content.** — **strong / verified primary**

- **He was one of ten dietitians/advice-givers named in the parallel letter to the ABA.** The
  others: Valerie Agyeman, Nichole Andrews, Leslie Bonci, Keri Gans, Stephanie Grasso, Cara
  Harbstreet, Andrea Miller, Adam Pecoraro, Mary Ellen Phipps
  ([FTC warning letter to American Beverage Association, PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/warning-letter-american-bev.pdf),
  13 Nov 2023, addressed to CEO Kevin Keane). The ABA had argued that a "#safetyofaspartame"
  hashtag was adequate disclosure; the FTC replied "We disagree with that assertion."
  — **strong / verified primary**

- **Reported by an independent investigative outlet, not only by regulators.**
  ([The Examination, "US regulator cracks down on food industry for paid dietitian 'influencer'
  posts"](https://www.theexamination.org/articles/ftc-crackdown)) — Mughal did not respond to their
  request for comment. The underlying investigation was the *Washington Post*/Examination piece of
  13 Sept 2023, which the FTC letter itself footnotes. — **strong**

- **Ongoing sweetener content with an industry-adjacent production.** He hosts a podcast, *The
  Sweet Taste of Balance*, produced by London Podcast Studios as a branded "original"
  ([London Podcast Studios](https://londonpodcaststudios.co.uk/originals/sweet-taste-of-balance),
  episode 1 dated 29/05/2024). Episode 4 (promoted 27 June 2025) is specifically on aspartame:
  *"Where did aspartame's bad reputation come from? … This episode dives into the origins of the
  concern, separates science from headlines and looks at what the current evidence actually says."*
  ([Instagram reel DLZZYsGIYP1](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DLZZYsGIYP1/)) — **moderate for
  existence and content; the funder is UNVERIFIED, see below**

### 2. Dr Jessica Knurick

- **Credentials verify cleanly.** PhD, Arizona State University (physical activity, nutrition and
  wellness); MS, East Stroudsburg University of Pennsylvania; BS, Florida Atlantic University.
  Registered Dietitian Nutritionist with an active NPI (1205309911, Scottsdale AZ) — an
  independent federal registry entry, which is the strongest verification available for a US RD.
  Taught nutrition science at UNLV from 2015; now consults.
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessica_Knurick),
  [NPI record](https://npiprofile.com/npi/1205309911)) — **strong**

- **She is NOT among the influencers named in the FTC action.** Worth stating explicitly, because
  the "paid dietitian" story is often applied to the whole cohort of RD communicators. Her name
  appears in neither FTC letter. — **strong / verified by absence from a primary document**

- **Her documented positions are adjacent to, not about, aspartame.** Wikipedia's summary of her
  public positions covers water fluoridation, food dyes ("safe, but marketed at children"), MAHA
  and RFK Jr. misinformation, and access to care. Her only indexed sweetener-specific long-form
  writing is *"The Truth About High-Fructose Corn Syrup — Why Switching to Cane Sugar Won't Save
  Your Health or Fix Our Food System"* (Substack, 21 Jul 2025). **I could not locate a substantive,
  sourced public statement by her specifically on aspartame.** — **this is a gap, not a finding**

### 3. Liam Layton ("The Plant Slant")

- **He is a nutrition graduate, not a dietitian — do not describe him as one.** BS in Nutrition
  Science, Russell Sage College; previously a sleep technician; reported as a graduate student in
  nutrition. He does not hold the RD/RDN credential. Reported following: **~7 million across
  TikTok, Instagram, YouTube and Facebook** — by far the largest reach of the four.
  ([Russell Sage College news](https://www.sage.edu/news/from-7m-social-media-followers-to-nutrition-student/),
  [MOFC partner story](https://mofc.org/partner-stories/liam-layton/)) — **moderate**

- **Two framings from his own captions are directly usable.** From TikTok captions surfaced in
  search indexing (TikTok itself is not fetchable):
  - *"You do NOT have to use artificial sweeteners, but the claims about them being toxic are
    ridiculous."* ([video 7213882902734507310](https://www.tiktok.com/@theplantslant/video/7213882902734507310))
  - On Diet Coke: *"unless you have rare conditions like PKU it's very safe."*
    ([video 7324116084695633198](https://www.tiktok.com/@theplantslant/video/7324116084695633198))
  — **moderate — caption-level evidence only; I could not retrieve the videos or transcripts**

- **This is the single best framing available to this site.** "You don't have to drink it; the
  toxicity claims are ridiculous" concedes the entire preference argument while giving up nothing
  on the science. It is unfalsifiable in the good sense: it cannot be attacked as shilling, because
  it does not recommend the product. Adopt this posture. — **assessment, not a finding**

  — **strong (absence across all searches)**

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

- **The FTC letter is real and it is about aspartame specifically.** Anyone attacking this site
  will find it in one search. Pre-empt it: state it, link the PDF, and note that the objection was
  to disclosure placement, not accuracy. Concealing it would be far more damaging than disclosing it.
- **Being paid by a trade body does not make a claim false**, and this site's own
  `industry-funding.md` brief presumably already establishes how funding shifts *probability* of a
  favourable result rather than determining truth. Apply that standard consistently — including to
  people you agree with. The test is whether the underlying regulator documents say what the
  communicator says they say.
- **Conversely: "he disclosed, the FTC just wanted it bigger" is a fair defence but an incomplete
  one.** The FTC's fourth point is substantive, not cosmetic: viewers who saw "ameribev" mostly did
  not know that meant the soft-drink industry's trade association. That is a real information
  deficit, not a formatting quibble.
- **Two of these four have no verifiable aspartame position at all** (Knurick, Morticia). Citing
  them as "public-health communicators who say aspartame is safe" would be fabrication by
  implication. Don't.
- **Layton has a video captioned "Diet Soda and Alzheimer's: What You Need to Know"**
  ([7552597792930254094](https://www.tiktok.com/@theplantslant/video/7552597792930254094), posted
  after the *Neurology* ELSA-Brasil paper). I could not retrieve its content. Do not assume it
  aligns with the site's framing.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **Myth: "The dietitians defending aspartame were exposed as frauds / the FTC fined them."**
  False. These were **staff warning letters**, not fines, not enforcement actions, not findings of
  violation. The letters say practices "may be deceptive or unfair" and request a response within
  15 working days. No penalty was assessed against anyone.
- **Myth: "Dr Idz was caught hiding that he was paid."** Overstated. He used Instagram's "Paid
  partnership with ameribev" label and "#ad". The FTC's finding was that these were not *clear and
  conspicuous* — buried below the fifth line, absent from the video itself, and using a sponsor name
  most viewers wouldn't recognise.
- **Myth (from the pro-sweetener side): "JECFA confirmed aspartame does not cause cancer."** This
  is Mughal's own 13 July 2023 wording, per the FTC letter, and it is **not what JECFA said**. JECFA
  reaffirmed the 0–40 mg/kg bw ADI and judged the evidence of an association "not convincing"; on the
  same day IARC classified aspartame Group 2B. Regulatory bodies do not certify absence of causation.
  **Do not repeat this phrasing on the site.**

**On the "36 cans" figure.** It is not invented — a 12-to-36-can range for a 60 kg adult circulates
in UK coverage, and 36 is the arithmetic you get for a low-aspartame blended drink (many UK diet
drinks cut aspartame with acesulfame-K, so a can may hold well under 100 mg). But it is the most
flattering end of a range, it is attached in his phrasing to a *"small adult"* — the least
favourable body weight — and it conflates "exceeding the ADI" with being "at risk", which are not
the same thing (the ADI already carries a 100× safety factor below the NOAEL). **The site should
keep the 9–14 figure it already uses.** Do not adopt 36.

## Corrections to the existing site

**No factual contradictions found.** I checked every `.html` file: none of them mentions Mughal,
Knurick, Layton or Morticia, so nothing published is wrong about them. Two observations rather than
corrections:

1. **The site's "9–14 cans" figure (used in `caveats.html`, `sources.html`, and "14 cans" in
   `the-evidence.html`) is the correct and defensible one** and matches both WHO/JECFA (2023) and
   Cancer Research UK. If anyone proposes upgrading it to the more dramatic "36 cans" that Dr Idz
   used, **reject that** for the reasons above.
2. **If the site ever cites Dr Idz, the FTC letter must be disclosed in the same breath.** Given
   that this site's whole proposition is scrupulousness, citing a communicator with an undisclosed
   ABA-paid history on this exact molecule would be a self-inflicted wound. The safest handling is
   to cite the regulators he cites, and not him.
