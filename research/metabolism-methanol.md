# Metabolism of aspartame: phenylalanine, aspartate and methanol

*Scope note: this brief extends the existing `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md` and `jecfa-iarc-2023.md` files. Those cover the ADI, the regulatory verdicts and the pregnancy/phenylalanine modelling. This one covers the digestive and metabolic mechanism in detail, the methanol quantities, and the formaldehyde argument. Where the EFSA brief already states a number, it is cross-referenced rather than repeated.*

## Bottom line

Aspartame is a dipeptide methyl ester. It is hydrolysed completely in the gut lumen and gut wall before absorption, and intact aspartame is undetectable in blood in rats, dogs, monkeys and humans. What enters the bloodstream is three ordinary dietary constituents: phenylalanine (~50% by weight), aspartic acid (~40%) and methanol (~10%). The methanol argument fails on arithmetic — EFSA's own exposure modelling puts aspartame at **1–10% of total methanol exposure**, with endogenous production supplying **>80%** — and the formaldehyde argument fails on both dose and mechanism: formaldehyde is a high-flux endogenous metabolite of the folate one-carbon cycle, and aspartame at the *full* ADI would add roughly **0.3–0.4% of the body's own daily formaldehyde turnover**.

Two things genuinely cut the other way and the site must concede them. First, aspartame delivers phenylalanine *differently* from protein: it raises the plasma Phe/LNAA ratio, whereas a protein meal lowers it. Second, at deliberate abuse doses (100–200 mg/kg) blood methanol *does* rise measurably, and urinary formate excretion rises 2–3 fold. The honest claim is "no measurable methanol or formate signal at real-world intakes," not "aspartame never produces methanol you can detect."

## Key findings

**1. Complete pre-systemic hydrolysis; no intact aspartame in blood.** EFSA: *"after oral ingestion, aspartame is fully hydrolysed within the gastro-intestinal tract… Hydrolysis is very efficient and the amount of intact aspartame that enters the bloodstream has been reported as undetectable in several studies conducted in various species, including rats, dogs, monkeys and humans."* The methyl ester is cleaved by pancreatic/intestinal esterases in the lumen; the resulting dipeptide Asp-Phe is handled exactly like a protein-derived dipeptide, by brush-border dipeptidases and cytosolic hydrolysis. Even the potential intermediate phenylalanine methyl ester is broken down in the GI tract (shown in monkeys and pigs). [EFSA draft opinion §68](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf); [Gastroenterology 1986 intestinal hydrolysis study](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/0016-5085(86)90697-9/fulltext) — **strong**

**2. Stoichiometry.** ~50% phenylalanine, ~40% aspartic acid, ~10% methanol by weight. [Nutrition Reviews 2016, "Biological fate of low-calorie sweeteners"](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/74/11/670/2281652); [EFSA §68](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**

**3. Aspartate does not reach the circulation in meaningful amounts.** Dietary aspartate is transaminated to oxaloacetate inside enterocytes on first pass. Stegink gave 12 normal adults aspartame at 34 mg/kg or an equimolar aspartate load (13 mg/kg) in crossover: *no* change in plasma or erythrocyte aspartate at any timepoint after either. This retires the "aspartame is an excitotoxin" line at the level of exposure, not just effect. [J Nutr, Stegink et al.](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(23)27226-0/abstract) — **strong**

**4. Phenylalanine: the dose is small relative to protein.** Møller (1991) matched **1.0 g of aspartame** against **12.2 g of bovine albumin** as *phenylalanine-equivalent* doses — both delivered 0.56 g Phe. So aspartame is roughly a 12:1 concentrate of protein for phenylalanine content. A 330 mL can at the EU legal maximum for flavoured drinks (600 mg/L → ~198 mg aspartame) therefore carries about as much phenylalanine as **~2.4 g of protein** — a third of one egg's worth. [Pharmacol Toxicol 1991;68:408–412](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1600-0773.1991.tb01262.x) — **strong**

**5. Blood phenylalanine after a large aspartame bolus stays inside the normal postprandial range.** Stegink 1981: aspartame at **34 mg/kg** (the 99th-percentile *projected* daily intake, taken all at once — Koeppe et al. calculated this as equivalent to about **5 litres of diet soft drink** for a 76 kg adult) raised mean plasma Phe in normal men from **58.6 ± 12.5 µmol/L to a peak of 120 ± 37.9 µmol/L**, and in women from 48.3 to 89.5 µmol/L. For scale, the clinical fetal-protection ceiling is **360 µmol/L** (see `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md`). [Toxicology 1981;20:81–90](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0300483X81901086); [J Neurochem 1991;56:1526–35](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2013754/) — **strong**

**6. Chronic dosing at ~2× the ADI changes nothing.** Leon et al. (1989): 108 healthy adults, **75 mg/kg bw/day in three divided doses for 24 weeks**. No change in plasma phenylalanine, aspartate or methanol, no change in the Phe/LNAA ratio, no change in urinary formate excretion. This is the single most useful study for the site — it is a long, human, supra-ADI exposure with the exact endpoints the critics name. [Summarised in EFSA draft opinion §1627–1631](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**

**7. Brain amino-acid transport is unaffected at real doses.** A PET study using [¹¹C]ACHC in 15 volunteers found aspartame at 34 mg/kg produced an 11.5% fall in the transport rate constant K₁ across the blood–brain barrier, and concluded that *"under conditions of normal dietary use, aspartame is thus unlikely to cause changes in brain amino acid uptake that are measurable by PET."* Note this is a real (if small) measured effect at a 5-litre-equivalent bolus. [Koeppe et al., J Neurochem 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2013754/) — **moderate to strong**

**8. Blood methanol is undetectable at realistic doses, and only rises at abuse doses.** Stegink 1981 (30 adults): at **34 mg/kg**, methanol was **below the 0.4 mg/dL (4 mg/L) detection limit**. Mean peaks were **1.27 ± 0.48 mg/dL at 100 mg/kg**, **2.14 ± 0.35 at 150 mg/kg**, **2.58 ± 0.78 at 200 mg/kg**. Blood formate did not increase significantly even at 200 mg/kg. Methanol half-life 2.5–3 h. No blood-chemistry or ophthalmological changes at 24 h. [J Toxicol Environ Health 1981;7:281–90](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7230276/) — **strong**

**9. The methanol arithmetic, from EFSA's own exposure model.** Methanol from aspartame across five population groups: **0.05–1.6 mg/kg bw/day (mean)**, **0.2–3.7 mg/kg bw/day (95th percentile)**. Methanol from *all* sources (basal endogenous + endogenously metabolised pectin + natural food + aspartame): **8.4–18.9 mg/kg bw/day (mean)**, **15.1–35.1 (high consumers)**. Contribution shares: **endogenous 84–97%**, **natural food occurrence 1.2–7.8%**, **aspartame 0.5–9.7%**. EFSA's own summary: *"for average consumers of aspartame, the contribution from aspartame to the overall exposure to methanol ranged from 1% to 10%… the exposure from aspartame-derived methanol is similar to methanol exposure from natural food occurrence."* [EFSA draft opinion Tables 10–13, §1188–1213](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**

**10. Measured methanol in ordinary foods dwarfs a diet drink.** From concentration data compiled in the EFSA opinion (WHO 1997; Wu 2007; Hou 2008; Anthon & Barrett 2010; Hang & Woodams 2010): fruit juices **1–640 mg/L, average 140 mg/L**; apple/banana/grape/orange juice **8–148 mg/L**; **tomato juice up to 240 mg/L** and canned diced tomatoes up to 200 mg/kg; vegetable juices **53–194 mg/L**; beer **6–27 mg/L**; **wine 96–321 mg/L**; distilled spirits **10–220 mg/L**; **apple cider 300–700 mg/L**; apple spirit up to **1,600 mg/L**. [EFSA draft opinion §1084–1101](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**

**11. Pectin is the real methanol firehose.** Lindinger et al. (1997), measuring breath methanol by proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometry with ethanol used to block ADH: **basal endogenous production 300–600 mg/day**; ingesting **10–15 g of pectin (≈ 1 kg of apples) adds a further 400–1,400 mg/person/day**, which the authors noted is equivalent to the methanol in **0.3 L of 80-proof brandy**. EFSA's own panel conclusion: basal endogenous production **2–9 mg/kg bw/day** in adults, plus **6–20 mg/kg bw/day** more after pectin-containing food. [Alcohol Clin Exp Res 1997;21:939–43](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9267548/) — **strong**

**12. The formaldehyde argument, killed by dose.** Formaldehyde from methanol is a transient intermediate with a blood half-life of **~1 minute**; it is essentially undetectable in blood even after intravenous infusion in animals. EFSA's dedicated 2014 assessment estimated **endogenous formaldehyde turnover at 0.61–0.91 mg/kg bw/min, i.e. 878–1,310 mg/kg bw/day** (≈ 61–92 g/day for a 70 kg adult), against background dietary formaldehyde of ~1.4–1.7 mg/kg bw/day. Its explicit conclusion: at the ADI of 40 mg/kg bw/day, aspartame-derived formaldehyde is **~4 mg/kg bw/day, about 0.3–0.4% of endogenous turnover**. [EFSA Journal 2014;12(2):3550](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/3550) — **strong**

**13. Formaldehyde is not a foreign poison — it is a one-carbon metabolite.** Formaldehyde is generated by oxidative decomposition of the folate backbone and is fed back into the folate-driven one-carbon cycle for nucleotide and amino-acid synthesis; its oxidation product formate is a substrate for purine synthesis. In humans ~80% of an oral formate dose is exhaled as CO₂. [Burgos-Barragan et al., *Nature* 2017;548:549–554](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature23481); [EFSA §4037–4042](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **strong**

**14. The best DNA-adduct data go the right way.** Lu et al. (2012) dosed rats with stable-isotope [¹³CD₄]-methanol at **500 and 2,000 mg/kg bw/day for 5 days** — doses corresponding to *thousands* of mg/kg of aspartame — and used isotope labelling to separate exogenous from endogenous formaldehyde-DNA adducts. Exogenous N²-hydroxymethyl-dG adducts were **lower than endogenous** in every tissue: exogenous/endogenous ratios of **0.18 and 0.45 in bone marrow, 0.056 and 0.2 in liver**. [Described in EFSA draft opinion §4219](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/130108.pdf) — **moderate** (EFSA itself raised methodological concerns about the adduct quantification; see caveats)

## Genuine caveats / what cuts the other way

1. **Aspartame is not metabolically identical to a protein meal, and the site must not say it is.** A protein meal delivers phenylalanine alongside the other large neutral amino acids that compete with it for the blood–brain-barrier carrier; Stegink's hamburger-and-milkshake study (1 g protein/kg) found the plasma **Phe/LNAA ratio *decreased***. Aspartame delivers phenylalanine essentially alone, so the ratio **rises**: Burns et al. measured a **26% increase at 1 hour** after 500 mg aspartame. The correct defence is not "same as protein" but the one Burns actually supplies: **100 g of sucrose raised the same ratio by 19%** (via insulin-driven clearance of the competing LNAAs), so the effect size is in the range of ordinary sugar. [AJCN 1991;53:670–5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2000820/); [Pharmacology 1991;43:210–9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1771173/)
2. **Per gram of phenylalanine delivered, aspartame produces a somewhat larger plasma excursion than protein.** Møller found the plasma Phe AUC was **~40% greater after aspartame than after phenylalanine-equivalent albumin**, plausibly because albumin also raises insulin (aspartame does not) and insulin speeds Phe clearance. The difference was not statistically significant, but n = 6 — this is an underpowered null, not a demonstrated equivalence.
3. **Blood methanol from aspartame is not always undetectable.** At 100 mg/kg (≈ 7 g for a 70 kg adult, ~2.5× the ADI) mean peak blood methanol was **12.7 mg/L**, above the NTP-CERHR **<10 mg/L** "minimal concern" benchmark for developmental effects. At 200 mg/kg, EFSA reports urinary formic-acid excretion **2–3 fold above basal** over 0–8 h. These are abuse doses, but the honest phrasing is dose-conditional.
4. **At the full ADI, aspartame-derived methanol is of the same order as basal endogenous production.** 40 mg/kg × 70 kg = 2,800 mg aspartame → **~280 mg methanol/day**, against basal endogenous production of 300–600 mg/day. The "1–10%" figure depends on the denominator including **endogenously metabolised pectin**, which is the largest single term in EFSA's model (7.9–12.5 mg/kg bw/day in toddlers). EFSA itself says aspartame's contribution is *"similar to methanol exposure from natural food occurrence"* — similar, not negligible. Say that, not "a rounding error."
5. **The Trocho (1998) formaldehyde-adduct paper was rebutted by argument, not by replication.** Trocho gave rats ¹⁴C-methyl-labelled aspartame at 10 mg/kg and found label bound to tissue protein and nucleic acids, more so after 10 days of 200 mg/kg pretreatment. Tephly (1999) and EFSA both argue the method **cannot distinguish** label incorporated normally via the tetrahydrofolate one-carbon pathway from covalent formaldehyde adducts, and could not exclude Maillard artefacts from sample processing. EFSA's wording is careful: the work *"does not demonstrate unequivocally"* that formaldehyde binds tissue macromolecules. That is a verdict of "unproven," not "disproven." **I could not find a properly controlled replication.**
6. **The endogenous-formaldehyde turnover figure is model-derived.** 878–1,310 mg/kg bw/day is calculated from an assumed 1.5-minute half-life, not measured by mass balance. It is the right order of magnitude and is used by EFSA, but it should not be quoted as a measurement.
7. **EFSA had its own reservations about Lu et al. (2012)** — specifically about the low count of exogenous adducts at 500 mg/kg relative to endogenous adducts, and about the indirect (in vitro derivatisation) measurement of the adducts. Do not present it as airtight.
8. **PKU remains a real exclusion**, and PKU heterozygotes show roughly double the phenylalanine excursion. See `efsa-2013-reevaluation.md`.

## Myths in this area and what is actually true

- **"Aspartame enters your bloodstream and circulates as a chemical."** False and well-evidenced as false: intact aspartame is undetectable in blood across four species. Whatever aspartame does, it does not do it as aspartame.
- **"Aspartame contains wood alcohol / turns into embalming fluid."** Both technically true and rhetorically dishonest. Methanol is methanol regardless of source, and a glass of tomato juice or a pint of cider supplies more of it than a diet cola. Formaldehyde is produced by your own folate cycle at tens of grams per day.
- **"Formaldehyde accumulates from aspartame."** The claim rests on Trocho 1998. The label that accumulates is a **carbon atom**, which is exactly what the one-carbon pool is for. Radiolabel in tissue protein is the expected result of feeding a labelled methyl group, whether it comes from aspartame, pectin or fruit juice.
- **The sophisticated version — "fruit-juice methanol is safe because the ethanol in fruit protects you" (Woodrow Monte's argument, an activist source I have not read in the original).** The strongest counter is Lindinger's own data: **1 kg of plain apples releases 400–1,400 mg of methanol** from pectin in the colon, with no meaningful ethanol. And the ADH-blocking ethanol concentrations used in Lindinger's protocol were **>150 mg/L blood** — far above anything reachable from juice.
- **"Aspartate from aspartame is an excitotoxin."** Plasma and erythrocyte aspartate do not change after 34 mg/kg aspartame. There is no exposure to be toxic with.

## What I could NOT verify

- **FDA's own page** (`fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food`) returned HTTP 404 on two attempts. All FDA-attributed statements here are second-hand via search summaries and are **not** used as load-bearing support. Someone should re-fetch it.
- **A primary per-serving measurement of methanol in retail tomato juice.** The "85 mg per 12 oz" figure circulating online traces to secondary and advocacy sources. My comparison is derived from EFSA-tabulated concentration ranges and is labelled as derived.
- **Woodrow Monte's 1984 paper** *"Aspartame: methanol and the public health"* — I did not read the original, only search-summarised characterisations. The "ethanol in fruit protects you" argument is attributed to him on that basis and should be checked before quoting him directly.
- **Any properly controlled replication of Trocho et al. (1998)** using isotope discrimination between one-carbon-pool incorporation and covalent adducts. I found none. The critique (Tephly 1999, EFSA 2013) is argumentative rather than experimental. This is the weakest link in the pro-safety case on formaldehyde and should be stated as such.
- **Møller 1991 and Stegink 1981 (Toxicology) full texts** — both publisher pages returned 403. Numbers are from PubMed/abstract-level and search-tool extraction; the plasma Phe figures for Stegink 1981 (5.86 → 12.0 µmol/dL) appear in multiple independent summaries but I did not read the paper itself.
- **The provenance of the 1.5-minute formaldehyde half-life assumption** underlying the 878–1,310 mg/kg/day turnover estimate. EFSA states it; I did not trace it to the underlying kinetic study.
- **EFSA Journal 2013;11(12):3496** (the final published opinion) returned 403; all EFSA quantitative material here is from the **public-consultation draft**, which is textually near-identical on these sections but is formally a draft. Section numbers cited are the draft's.
