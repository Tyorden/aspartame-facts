What it becomes

Aspartame never gets into your blood

It's taken apart in the gut before absorption, into three things your body already handles by the gram. That fact does most of the work in this argument — but not all of it, and the gaps are worth knowing.

It's dismantled before it's absorbed

Aspartame is a dipeptide with a methyl group attached. Enzymes in the intestinal lumen cut the methyl ester off and split the remaining two amino acids, exactly as they would with any dipeptide from a piece of chicken. What crosses into the bloodstream is not aspartame.

This isn't inference. Intact aspartame is undetectable in blood in rats, dogs, monkeys and humans. There is no measurement showing it circulating, because it doesn't.

56% phenylalanine — an essential amino acid you must eat daily
40% aspartic acid — non-essential, made by your own cells
10% methanol — the component every scare story is built on

So "aspartame accumulates in your body" describes something that cannot happen. There is nothing to accumulate. The real question is whether the three pieces cause problems, and each has a different answer.

Aspartate: the excitotoxin claim

The argument is that aspartic acid is an excitatory neurotransmitter, and flooding the brain with it damages neurons.

The measurement that settles it: after 34 mg/kg of aspartame — equivalent to drinking five litres of diet soft drink in one sitting for a 76 kg (168 lb) adult — plasma and red-cell aspartate did not change at any timepoint. Not modestly. Not transiently. They did not move.

Dietary aspartate is transaminated inside the intestinal cells on first pass, before it ever reaches the portal blood. There is no systemic exposure for an excitotoxin argument to act on. This one is simply finished.

Phenylalanine: the one with a real story

This is the component where the detail matters, and the pro-aspartame version usually oversimplifies it.

What's solid: that same five-litre-equivalent bolus raised mean plasma phenylalanine in healthy men from 58.6 to a peak of 120 µmol/L. The clinical ceiling used to protect a fetus is 360 µmol/L. And in a 24-week trial, 108 adults took 75 mg/kg/day — roughly twice the European daily limit, every day, for six months — with no change in plasma phenylalanine, aspartate or methanol, and no change in urinary formate.

The right comparison is sugar, not chicken

You'll often read that aspartame is "just like eating protein." It isn't. Protein delivers phenylalanine alongside competing amino acids, so a normal protein meal actually lowers the ratio of phenylalanine to its competitors. Aspartame delivers phenylalanine nearly alone, so it raises that ratio — by about 26% an hour after a 500 mg dose.

The defensible comparison isn't protein. It's sugar: 100 g of sucrose shifts the same ratio by 19%, a similar magnitude. That's a fair thing to say. "It's the same as chicken" is not.

Methanol: the wood alcohol argument

"Aspartame contains wood alcohol" is true and, as an argument, empty. Methanol is methanol whatever produced it, and your diet is full of it.

And the largest source isn't food at all. Your own metabolism generates 300–600 mg of methanol a day before you eat anything. Eat a kilo of apples and gut bacteria break down the pectin to release another 400–1,400 mg — which the researchers who measured it noted is about what you'd get from a third of a litre of brandy.

Set against that, EFSA's modelling puts aspartame at 0.5–9.7% of total methanol exposure, with endogenous production accounting for 84–97%.

Two things that cut the other way

Blood methanol does rise at high doses. It was undetectable at 34 mg/kg, but reached 12.7, 21.4 and 25.8 mg/L at 100, 150 and 200 mg/kg. The 12.7 figure sits above a benchmark used for developmental effects. Those are two-to-five times the daily limit, which is why this belongs in a caveat rather than a headline — but "aspartame doesn't raise blood methanol" is only true at ordinary doses, and should always be said that way.

The denominator does a lot of work. At the full daily limit, aspartame would supply around 280 mg of methanol for a 70 kg (154 lb) adult — the same order of magnitude as basal endogenous production. EFSA's own careful wording is that aspartame's contribution is "similar to methanol exposure from natural food occurrence." Similar, not negligible.

Formaldehyde: you make 60 grams a day

Methanol is oxidised to formaldehyde, and "aspartame turns into embalming fluid" is the most viscerally effective claim in circulation. The arithmetic dismantles it completely.

61–92 g of formaldehyde your own body turns over every day, at 70 kg (154 lb)
0.3–0.4% of that total contributed by aspartame — at the full daily limit

Formaldehyde also isn't a foreign poison being smuggled in. It's a normal one-carbon metabolite, generated by the breakdown of your own folate, and fed straight back into the cycle that builds nucleotides and amino acids. Its blood half-life is about a minute. It is essentially undetectable in blood even when infused directly into an animal's vein.

The "embalming fluid" framing works because formaldehyde is genuinely used to preserve tissue — at concentrations thousands of times higher than anything metabolic, sustained rather than cleared in sixty seconds. Same molecule, entirely different situation.

The one study still standing

There is one paper the dose argument doesn't fully dispose of.

Trocho and colleagues (1998) fed rats radiolabelled aspartame and found the label bound to tissue protein and DNA, which they interpreted as formaldehyde adducts accumulating. It remains the scientific backbone of every formaldehyde claim you'll read.

The rebuttal is strong and, we think, correct: the method can't distinguish a carbon atom incorporated normally through the one-carbon pathway from a covalent formaldehyde adduct — and incorporation is precisely what that pathway is for. Feed a labelled methyl group from aspartame, pectin or fruit juice and you'd expect to find the label in tissue either way.

But note what's missing

That rebuttal is an argument, not an experiment. We could find no properly controlled replication using isotope methods capable of telling the two possibilities apart. EFSA's own verdict is that the work "does not demonstrate unequivocally" what it claims — which is a finding of unproven, not disproven.

This is the weakest link in the case for aspartame on formaldehyde, and anyone arguing the subject should know it's there. It doesn't rescue the "embalming fluid" claim, because the dose arithmetic above is independent of it. But "Trocho was debunked" overstates what happened. Nobody has repeated the experiment properly.
