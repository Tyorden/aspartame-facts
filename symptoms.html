How it makes you feel

The one place the critics have blinded trials

Most claims about aspartame have never survived a controlled test. Headache is the exception — and the real finding is narrower and more interesting than either side usually says.

Headache: a genuine, narrow finding

This is the only symptom with positive randomised evidence in humans, and it deserves to be stated properly rather than dismissed.

In a blinded challenge study, a subset of people who already identified themselves as aspartame-sensitive reported significantly more headaches at 30 mg/kg than on placebo. They didn't know which they were getting. That's a real result and it isn't explained away by expectation.

But the trials conflict, and the largest and best-controlled challenge study — 96 participants, government-funded rather than industry-funded — found nothing.

What that combination probably means

An effect that appears in a self-selected sensitive minority and vanishes in a larger general sample is the signature of an idiosyncratic trigger, not a population-level hazard. Some people genuinely do seem to get headaches from it, the way some people get them from red wine or aged cheese. That's a real phenomenon and it's also not a public health problem.

Regulators' position is that the headache evidence is too thin to conclude either way. That is more honest than "myth debunked," and this site isn't going to claim otherwise.

Worth noting the gap: there has been no modern dose-ranging replication of that positive finding. It's over thirty years old and nobody has properly retested it.

Seizures: three trials, one unreplicated positive

Three randomised controlled trials exist. Two are clearly null. One found a positive result on a subclinical EEG measure — not on actual seizures — in children with newly diagnosed epilepsy, and it has never been replicated in the thirty-four years since.

That unreplicated status should be held to the same standard this site applies elsewhere. We say on the metabolism page that nobody has properly replicated the 1998 formaldehyde-adduct study, and that this counts against treating it as settled. The same logic applies here in the opposite direction: an unreplicated positive is weak evidence, but it's not no evidence, and it hasn't been refuted so much as ignored.

Children's behaviour and ADHD: as settled as this field gets

This one is genuinely closed. Behaviour and cognition in children, including attention and hyperactivity, is about as close to a settled null as nutrition science produces. The trials are blinded, they're consistent, and they don't find an effect.

If you're a parent who has been told aspartame makes children hyperactive, that specific claim has been tested repeatedly and it doesn't hold up.

Mood: two small studies, both awkward

Two trials report worse mood. One was stopped early by an ethics board — it achieved only 13 participants, not the 40 its protocol planned, and it is routinely misdescribed in secondary sources as a larger study than it was. The other found worse mood at high dose in a small sample.

Neither has been replicated, and both come with serious caveats. That's a genuinely unresolved corner rather than either a scandal or a debunking.

Multiple sclerosis and lupus

These two travel together because they came from the same source — a 1990s chain email traced in detail on the origins page. But the evidence position for each is slightly different, and precision matters.

ClaimStatus
Aspartame causes MS, or MS is "really" methanol poisoning False The MS Trust's wording is the precise one: no published, peer-reviewed research supports the link. Also rejected by the MS Foundation and the FDA.
Aspartame causes or worsens lupus Unsupported The Lupus Foundation of America says aspartame is safe for people with lupus. No study has tested the hypothesis and ruled it out.
Say "no research supports it," not "studies show no link"

These sound the same and they aren't. For MS and lupus, in most cases no study has looked — so claiming studies found no link is itself a false statement, and a critic who knows the literature will catch it.

The weaker-sounding phrasing is the true one, and it's still sufficient: a claim that originated in an anonymous chain email and has never generated a single supporting paper in thirty years is not a claim anyone needs to take seriously. You don't need to overstate it.

When citing the Lupus Foundation, note it pairs its safety statement with a caution about long-term artificially sweetened beverage consumption and weight — so quoting only the reassuring half misrepresents the source.
