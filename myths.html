Claim by claim

What holds up, and what doesn't

Each claim gets a verdict and a source. Several come back "partly true" — those are the interesting ones, and flattening them into "false" is how debunking turns into spin.

Claims about the 2023 WHO reviews

False "The WHO declared aspartame a carcinogen."

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the WHO's cancer research arm, placed it in Group 2B — possibly carcinogenic — the weakest of the three positive tiers, shared with 328 other agents as of the July 2026 list. IARC's own text states that its classifications "do not indicate the degree of risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level." On the same day, the WHO's JECFA reaffirmed the safe intake level unchanged.

IARC/JECFA Summary of Findings, July 2023 · WHO news release, 14 July 2023

False "Aspartame was reclassified in 2023."

It was classified, not reclassified. IARC had never evaluated aspartame before. Monographs Volume 134, from the meeting held 6–13 June 2023, was the first-ever IARC evaluation of it. There was no previous IARC category to move from.

IARC Monographs Volume 134, published 2024

False "The WHO lowered or questioned the safe limit."

The Acceptable Daily Intake was reaffirmed unchanged at 0–40 mg/kg body weight per day. It has stood since 1980.

JECFA 96th meeting, Summary and Conclusions, June–July 2023

Mostly false "The two WHO agencies contradicted each other."

They answered different questions — hazard versus risk — and coordinated deliberately: three JECFA members attended the IARC meeting as observers, and both secretariats attended both meetings.

But they did genuinely disagree on two of three evidence streams. IARC found "limited evidence for cancer in experimental animals" where JECFA found "no concern"; IARC found "consistent and coherent evidence" of oxidative stress in experimental systems where JECFA judged those studies limited by design. Sites claiming the agencies agreed completely are overclaiming.

IARC/JECFA Summary of Findings, July 2023

False "IARC found that aspartame causes liver cancer."

IARC found three studies showing positive associations in which, in its own words, "bias or confounding could not be ruled out as an explanation for the positive findings." All three used artificially sweetened beverages as a proxy rather than measuring aspartame. A 2026 pooled analysis of 11 prospective cohorts — 1,518,411 participants, 2,811 liver cancers — found "little evidence" of any association between artificially sweetened beverage intake and liver cancer.

IARC Summary of Findings · Watling et al., JAMA Network Open 2026;9(6):e2617754

False "Group 2B means it's as dangerous as [alarming thing in the same group]."

Group membership encodes evidence strength, not potency or exposure. IARC states directly that "the cancer risk … associated with two agents classified in the same IARC Group may be very different."

The comparison runs the other way too: mobile phone signals, aloe vera and pickled vegetables sit in the same group. That's a genuinely useful way to show what the label covers — see what Group 2B is a list of — as long as it's used to explain the category rather than substituted for the actual argument, which is that hazard classification says nothing about dose.

IARC Monographs preamble and classification list

Claims about EFSA and the regulators

Partly true "EFSA just rubber-stamped industry studies."

False in that EFSA obtained and publicly released the 112 original 1980s dossier studies in November 2011, ran two public calls for data, held a consultation drawing 200+ comments, and produced a 263-page opinion with an original exposure assessment and de novo dose-response modelling.

Fair in that the unpublished study set was overwhelmingly supplied by the manufacturers — 173 unpublished study documents from Ajinomoto and NutraSweet — and a peer-reviewed critique alleges EFSA appraised those studies asymmetrically. See the caveats page; this one deserves more than a dismissal.

EFSA news, 24 Nov 2011 · EFSA draft opinion, 8 Jan 2013 · Millstone & Dawson, Archives of Public Health 2019;77:34

False "EFSA ignored the Ramazzini cancer studies."

EFSA assessed them in 2006, 2009 and again in 2013, and rejected them on specified, published grounds: high background chronic inflammatory disease in the animal colony, uncertainty about tumour diagnoses, and — for the 2010 mouse study — tumour incidences falling within the Ramazzini Institute's own historical control range. The US EPA had separately concluded that many "malignant neoplasms" from that laboratory were hyperplasias linked to chronic infection.

Whether that rejection was correct remains contested. That it was reasoned and documented is not.

EFSA draft opinion §94–105 · EFSA Journal 2009;7(3):945

False "EFSA declared it safe in pregnancy without looking at it."

The pregnancy question was the analytical centrepiece of the 2013 opinion. The entire phenylalanine mode-of-action pivot exists because EFSA could not exclude developmental toxicity in animals below 4,000 mg/kg. It then modelled human plasma phenylalanine against the 360 µM clinical guideline protective of the fetus.

EFSA draft opinion §106–225

False "EFSA covered up the Danish preterm birth finding."

EFSA discussed it directly. A large Danish prospective cohort found a small but significant elevation in medically induced preterm delivery with higher artificially sweetened drink consumption — "but not aspartame specifically." A Norwegian prospective study found a much weaker association, applying more to spontaneous deliveries, and exceeded by the association with sugar-sweetened soft drinks.

EFSA draft opinion, summary section

False "The 40 mg/kg ADI was invented by EFSA to protect industry."

The ADI was set by JECFA and the EU Scientific Committee for Food long before EFSA existed — at JECFA's 25th meeting in 1981, from a NOAEL of 4,000 mg/kg bw/day divided by a 100-fold uncertainty factor. EFSA reviewed it and declined to change it; JECFA independently reaffirmed it in 2023.

JECFA 96th meeting summary · EFSA Journal 2013;11(12):3496

Claims about the 2025 cognition study

False "The sweetener–dementia study was retracted."

Two Corrections, zero retractions, no expression of concern, and no Retraction Watch coverage as of 4 August 2026 — verified on PubMed, Crossref and Europe PMC. The corrections were author-initiated and transparent, including a voluntary full audit and re-run of the analysis. That is not the same as a withdrawal, and describing it as one is a misuse of a genuinely useful fact.

PubMed 40902134 · Crossref DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000214023 · full correction record

Overstated "One diet soda a day ages your brain 1.6 years."

The 1.6-year figure comes from the American Academy of Neurology's press release, not the paper's abstract. It is a relative-rate translation of a very small absolute effect (β = −0.026 SD/year on global cognition) between tertiles of seven combined sweeteners, in an observational cohort, restricted to adults under 60 — and it did not appear in the over-60 group, where dementia risk actually concentrates.

AAN press release, 3 Sept 2025 · Neurology 2025;105(7):e214023 (corrected values)

False "The study proves sweeteners cause cognitive decline."

The authors explicitly deny this in print. Suemoto and Gomes Gonçalves, replying to correspondence on 3 November 2025, accept the study is observational and cannot establish causality; Suemoto describes the findings as "preliminary and hypothesis-generating."

Published author replies, Neurology, 3 and 12 Nov 2025

False "This shows sucralose harms the brain."

Sucralose was never measured in the study. Neither was stevia. The seven sweeteners assessed were aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol and tagatose. This one circulates in the anti-sweetener direction and is worth correcting regardless of which side it helps.

Neurology 2025;105(7):e214023 · Wölnerhanssen et al. reader response

Half-true "The doses were realistic — just one diet soda a day."

191 mg/day is roughly one can's worth of aspartame, so the framing isn't invented. But that figure is the mean of the highest exposure tertile, across all seven sweeteners combined — under 7% of the ADI even if all of it were aspartame. The cohort had very low sweetener exposure overall, with baseline in 2008 when Brazilian use was low, as the authors concede. The study cannot speak to heavy consumers in either direction.

Neurology 2025;105(7):e214023 · authors' reply, 12 Nov 2025
