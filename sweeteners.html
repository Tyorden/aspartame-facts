The other sweeteners

A shopping category, not a chemical one

"Artificial sweeteners" groups together compounds with nothing in common but a supermarket aisle. Findings don't transfer between them, in either direction.

Look at that last row. The polyols aren't merely different molecules — they're eaten at roughly a thousand times the dose, they behave like carbohydrates, and they circulate in the blood at concentrations high-intensity sweeteners never approach. They're in the same category as aspartame the way a bag of flour is in the same category as a pinch of saffron.

That dose gap is also why the polyols carry an EU-mandated laxative warning and aspartame doesn't. You can't consume enough aspartame for bulk effects to exist.

The point cuts both ways

It's tempting to deploy this argument only in one direction — the erythritol clot signal isn't about aspartame, the 2025 cognition study pooled seven unrelated compounds, and so on. All true. But intellectual consistency requires the reverse:

Aspartame's evidence base is not evidence for anything else

Aspartame has by far the largest human dataset of any sweetener. That is not a reason to be relaxed about monk fruit, whose toxicology is comparatively thin, or about allulose, whose EU novel-food dossier failed. "Sweeteners are well studied" is false as a general statement. Aspartame is well studied. The others vary enormously.

Where aspartame sits

In the strongest tier, on three measures: full food-additive approval in both the US and EU, the largest human dataset of any sweetener, and the most headroom before you reach its limit — the FDA's own arithmetic puts it at 75 tabletop packets a day for a 60 kg (132 lb) adult, more than for any other approved sweetener.

Most of the genuinely open questions in this field are attached to other compounds: the erythritol and xylitol cardiovascular signals, polyol gastrointestinal tolerance, sucralose under prolonged baking, monk fruit's thin toxicology.

On the date of the EFSA opinion

EFSA works through previously approved additives on a rolling schedule, and has lately reached erythritol (2023), saccharin (2024), steviol glycosides (2024), acesulfame-K (2025), neotame (2025) and sucralose (2026). Aspartame's turn came earlier — its full re-evaluation was published in 2013.

The date of a review is not a measure of its depth. That opinion runs to 263 pages and drew on more than 600 datasets, and its conclusions were independently reproduced by the WHO's JECFA in 2023 — the most recent international assessment of aspartame by anyone. A further EU opinion is pending: the Commission confirmed in March 2025 that the ongoing re-evaluation of the aspartame–acesulfame salt (E 962) will consider new aspartame data.

What the gut studies actually found

Two papers made "sweeteners wreck your gut" a headline. Both are real and well-conducted. Neither says what was reported about aspartame.

The 2014 Nature paper

Aspartame appears in exactly one mouse experiment. Every human experiment in that paper, and every mechanistic experiment that established the microbiome as the causal route, used saccharin only. The famous finding is a saccharin finding.

The 2022 Cell trial

This is the best human evidence in the area — four sweeteners, 20 people each. And the result is the opposite of how it was reported:

SweetenerImpaired glycaemic response?
SaccharinYes
SucraloseYes
AspartameNo
SteviaNo

The glucose-intolerance finding that made these papers famous does not extend to aspartame. It was one of the two that didn't do it.

The residue, which isn't nothing

That same 2022 trial found aspartame — like all four sweeteners — measurably shifted stool and oral microbiome composition and the plasma metabolome relative to controls, at a dose of roughly one can of diet soda a day.

Those shifts were small, largely reversible, and came with no glycaemic change and no demonstrated clinical consequence. But "aspartame is completely inert to the gut" is an overclaim. The defensible sentence is: detectable but small microbiome shifts at real-world intakes, with no demonstrated metabolic or clinical effect — and the glucose finding that drove the headlines doesn't apply to it.

The comparator nobody names

Every one of these compounds is being compared against the wrong baseline. The relevant question is almost never "sweetener versus nothing." It's sweetener versus the sugar it replaced — and on that comparison, the evidence for harm from sugar is enormously stronger than anything in this entire field.
