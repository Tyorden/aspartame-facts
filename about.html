Where the money comes from

Nowhere. That's the entire answer, but the specific version is more useful than the general one, so here it is.

Not funded, not affiliated, not commissioned
  • No industry money. Nothing from Ajinomoto or any other manufacturer, nothing from Coca-Cola, PepsiCo or any bottler, nothing from the Calorie Control Council, the International Sweeteners Association, or any other trade body. No contact with any of them.
  • No money from the other direction either. No campaign group, no NGO, no supplement seller, no organic or natural-foods business, no wellness brand. The anti-sweetener side has commercial interests too, and they aren't behind this.
  • Nothing to sell you. No ads, no affiliate links, no tracking, no newsletter, no book, no course, no consultancy, no Patreon.

The running costs are a domain name and some evenings.

This matters more here than it would on most subjects, because funding really has distorted this literature. Industry-sponsored reviews of artificially sweetened beverages are substantially more likely to reach favourable conclusions, and 42% of them disclosed no conflicts at all. That's a replicated finding, it's on the caveats page, and it's a fair thing to hold against the field.

It's also the reason industry sources are labelled wherever they appear on this site and are never the only support for a claim. If a page rests on the Calorie Control Council, it says so and you should discount it accordingly. Mostly the pages rest on regulator documents and journal articles you can open yourself.

Who wrote it

Nobody you've heard of. I'm not a toxicologist, a dietitian, an epidemiologist or a food scientist, and this site would be worse if it pretended otherwise.

That's the reason for the citation habit. Every number here is chained to a primary source — a regulator's own PDF, a journal page, a systematic review — because you are not being asked to take my word for anything. My word isn't worth anything. Open the source. If it doesn't say what I said it says, that's a bug and I want to hear about it.

Anonymous because a byline would add nothing. A name would either be an appeal to authority I haven't earned or a target for whoever wants to argue about motives rather than evidence, and the second one is how most of these conversations get derailed. There's a handle attached to the repository — this isn't a secret identity, it's just not a credential.

Why bother with aspartame specifically

Because of how the pipeline works now, and because aspartame is the place where you can actually measure how badly it fails.

Someone sends you a link. The link is a wellness site, summarising a news article, summarising a press release, describing a mouse study. Four steps back from the finding, "was associated with, in mice, at doses no human reaches" has become "causes," and the mice have disappeared. Nobody in that chain lied outright. Each step just rounded slightly toward the more alarming version, because the more alarming version travels further.

That runs on everything — seed oils, microplastics, sunscreen, non-stick pans, 5G. Most of the time you can't do much about it, because the underlying evidence genuinely is thin and "nobody really knows" is the honest answer.

Aspartame is the exception, and that's what makes it worth the effort. It has been studied for fifty years, across more than 600 datasets, by regulators in different countries, under different legal systems, in different decades. It is one of the most heavily investigated compounds in the food supply. The evidence isn't thin at all — and the gap between what that evidence says and what people confidently believe is still enormous. When a subject this well-documented can be this misrepresented, that tells you something about the pipeline rather than about the sweetener.

The specific thing that pushed this from irritation to a website: most of the scary claims you've encountered descend from a single Usenet post written by one activist in 1995. Its central quote — an EPA scientist supposedly announcing an epidemic — was the author quoting something she herself had said from the audience, attributed to the speaker, who later put her denial in writing. It went on to circulate under a byline nobody has ever traced to a real person. Thirty years on it's still going, sometimes word for word, sometimes rewritten as a TikTok script. No supporting paper, no correction, no decay. That story is traced in full on the origins page, and it illustrates how health misinformation actually works better than anything I could invent.

Assume I've got something wrong

Somewhere in here, something is out of date, misread, or stated more confidently than the source supports. That's true of any reference document and it's certainly true of one built by a single person at night.

So the site is set up to be checked rather than trusted. Every figure traces to something on the sources page. Where a claim couldn't be verified against a primary source, the page says so rather than quietly rounding it up. Errors found after publication get fixed in place and logged in RESEARCH-STATUS.md instead of disappearing.

If you can show me a primary source that contradicts something here, the page changes. That isn't good manners — it's the only thing that makes a site like this worth reading, and it's the standard I'd want applied to whoever sent you the link in the first place.

Not medical advice. If you have phenylketonuria, aspartame genuinely is contraindicated for you — see the caveats page.
