The cancer question

One lab, one tumour type, one problem

Almost the entire cancer alarm around aspartame rests on rat studies from a single institute — and on the one diagnosis that an independent re-read of that lab's slides found it was getting wrong.

The rat studies

Between 2005 and 2010 the Ramazzini Institute in Italy published bioassays reporting dose-related increases in lymphoma and leukaemia in rats fed aspartame. The headline finding — an effect appearing at doses close to the human daily limit — is where "aspartame causes cancer" comes from. Almost nothing else in the literature says it.

Two features of the data are worth looking at before anything else.

The effect was female-only. And in the same study, male controls ran at 20.7% lymphoma/leukaemia — higher than four of the six treated male groups. When your untreated animals out-tumour most of your dosed animals in one sex, the more economical reading isn't that the compound protects male rats. It's that the control rates are unstable.

The female control rate was anomalously low. At 8.7%, against 20.7% in males of the same strain in the same experiment. The "significant increase" in treated females is measured against that number.

What 13,011 slides showed

This is the part that settles it, and it's rarely reported.

An independent Pathology Working Group, sponsored by the US National Toxicology Program and EPA, re-examined the Ramazzini Institute's own slides — 13,011 slides from 800 rats in the institute's methanol study, and 6,751 more from 360 rats in another. Same animals, same tissue, independent pathologists.

They found no treatment-related increase in lymphoma. And the counts diverged sharply from the institute's:

GroupRamazzini's countIndependent re-read
Male controls2613
High-dose females256

The explanation was specific: the colony had chronic lung infection, and inflammatory infiltrate was being read as lymphoma. That is a known hazard of the diagnosis. It inflates counts in whichever groups happen to be sicker, which is not a treatment effect.

Why this is the strongest single fact on the page

This isn't a regulator disagreeing with a lab's conclusions from the outside. It's independent pathologists looking at the same tissue and reaching different counts — and the discrepancy falls precisely on the diagnosis the aspartame alarm depends on. Notably, the same group found the institute's solid tumour data held up well. The problem is specific to lymphoma, and lymphoma is the finding.

What the regulators did about it

  • EPA, June 2010: formally decided not to rely on Ramazzini lymphoma/leukaemia data in its assessments — while continuing to accept the institute's solid tumour data, where the independent re-read agreed.
  • FDA, April 2007: concluded the data supplied "do not appear to support the aspartame-related findings reported by ERF." It couldn't complete a definitive review because the institute didn't provide the full study data, and its offer to examine the pathology slides was not taken up.
  • EFSA: assessed and rejected the findings four separate times — 2006, twice in 2009, and again in 2013 — on stated grounds including the background lung inflammation, absent dose-response, and unjustified aggregation of all malignant tumours together.
  • NTP's own studies in three genetically modified mouse strains, at doses up to roughly 7,660 mg/kg/day, found no treatment-related neoplasms.

No independent laboratory has reproduced the finding.

This is not universally accepted

In June 2025 Philip Landrigan and Kurt Straif — the latter a former IARC official — published "Irregularities in the IARC Working Group Evaluation of Ramazzini Institute Aspartame Studies" in Annals of Global Health, alleging that the working group's handling of those studies was itself flawed.

Their specific charges are not summarised here, because doing so fairly requires working through the paper in detail. It is flagged because treating the Ramazzini question as fully closed would be premature while researchers of that standing are actively disputing how the evaluation was conducted. The pathology re-read above still stands on its own terms; whether IARC weighted it correctly is a separate argument, and it is live.

The human evidence

There is no single "aspartame cohort study." There are around a dozen, they disagree, and the disagreement tracks study design more closely than it tracks biology.

The two largest studies that measured aspartame itself found nothing. NIH-AARP followed 473,984 people. The Nurses' Health Studies ran roughly 30 years and counted 10,814 invasive breast cancers, returning a hazard ratio of 1.00 (0.98–1.03) per 200 mg/day of aspartame.

That second figure deserves a moment. It isn't "no significant association" from a study too small to tell — it is an estimate of exactly one, with a confidence interval two percentage points wide, across nearly eleven thousand cancers. Precise nulls at that scale are rare in nutrition research, and they are much harder to argue with than a wide one.

Two cohorts did report positive associations. NutriNet-Santé (2022) and the American Cancer Society's CPS-II Nutrition Cohort (2026) reported hazard ratios of 1.13–1.22 for total cancer. Both are serious studies by serious groups — NutriNet-Santé has better exposure measurement than most, using repeated 24-hour records and a brand-level sweetener database rather than a single questionnaire.

But neither found a coherent dose-response, and the authors of each say so themselves. A dose-response is the thing that separates a causal signal from a confounded one. Without it, two studies agreeing on a hazard ratio of about 1.15 is a pattern in search of an explanation — and there's a good one available.

Reverse causality, actually measured

In NutriNet-Santé, roughly one third of higher sweetener consumers started a weight-loss diet within two years — against about one in eight non-consumers. People take up sweeteners because of their metabolic situation, and metabolic situation drives cancer risk. That's not a hand-wave; it's a measured difference in the same cohort that produced the positive finding.

One more structural problem: most cohorts never measured aspartame at all. EPIC, the pooled liver-cancer analyses and most meta-analysis inputs measured "artificially sweetened beverages," which since the mid-1990s has increasingly meant aspartame blended with acesulfame-K or sucralose. In NutriNet-Santé, acesulfame-K carried its own total-cancer hazard ratio of 1.13. Attributing those results to aspartame is an inference — and that cuts in both directions.

The largest test of the specific signal the International Agency for Research on Cancer cited came in 2026: a pooled analysis of 11 prospective cohorts, 1,518,411 participants and 2,811 liver cancers, found sugar-sweetened beverages associated with liver cancer but "little evidence" that artificially sweetened beverages were.

What's genuinely still open

Three findings don't fit the reassuring summary.

  • Leukaemia. A meta-analysis found a relative risk of 1.35 (1.03–1.77) with zero heterogeneity and a linear dose-response, carrying GRADE moderate certainty — the only site-specific finding in this whole literature with that grade. An earlier pooled analysis found 1.42 (1.00–2.02). A site-specific leukaemia question is genuinely unresolved.
  • Pancreatic cancer in CPS-II. Hazard ratio 1.11 (1.02–1.20), and it survived adjustment for BMI. Most sweetened-beverage signals disappear once you account for weight. This one didn't, and nobody has explained why.
  • Two independent cohorts landed on the same number. NutriNet-Santé at 1.15 and CPS-II at 1.13, in different countries, populations and methods. That isn't proof of anything. It also isn't nothing.

A defensible summary of all of it: no cohort has produced a signal that survives both dose-response and replication, the rodent finding that started the alarm failed independent pathology review, and the largest human studies measuring aspartame directly found nothing — but the positive studies are real work by credible groups, and leukaemia in particular deserves a proper answer rather than a dismissal.
